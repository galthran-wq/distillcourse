Stringing one-period replications back to front prices any claim on any
number of periods: the wealth equation propagates a portfolio forward,
backward induction propagates values backward, and the two meet at every
node.

This is the fifth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the first move of its second module. The first module
manufactured a forward from a loan and a share, fenced a call between
model-free bounds, and then, in <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the
one-period model</Ref>, closed the fence to a point: with two states next
period every claim is $\Delta_0$ shares plus a money market position, and its
cost is $\frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T)]$ with
$\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}$ read off the prices. What stands open is
whether the construction survives more than one period, where the terminal
payoff takes many values and no single line through two points can match it.
This lesson shows that it does, by re-solving the one-period problem at every
node of a tree: the hedger rebalances after each toss, paying for every
adjustment out of the portfolio itself, and the replication theorem, proved
in full, says the portfolio lands on the payoff along every one of the $2^N$
paths. What the lesson leaves open is the nature of the recursion that
produces the prices, which averages over the next toss given the tosses so
far; <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the next lesson</Ref> builds the
probability on the coin-toss space that makes that average a conditional
expectation.

The route: the $N$-period model and the wealth equation, which is the exact
statement that no money enters or leaves the hedge; backward induction run
node by node on a three-period call, with the delta read off at each node;
the hedge walked tick by tick along two paths, meeting the claim at every
date; the replication theorem proved by induction on the number of tosses;
and the computational shortcut of recombining trees, with the path-dependent
claim on which it fails.

## The tree: N tosses, one rebalancing per toss, and one equation for wealth

The one-period fence closed because a payoff with two values is a line
through two points. Over $N$ periods the terminal payoff of a call takes up
to $N + 1$ values and the payoff of a general claim up to $2^N$, and a
portfolio fixed at time zero, which is still one line, cannot match them.
The repair is to let the hedger trade again after every toss, and the first
thing to fix is what that trading is allowed to do.

### The model: the same coin, tossed N times

The running market of the first module, a stock at $4$ that doubles or
halves while cash grows by $1.25$, is now run for three periods. After the
first toss the stock is $8$ or $2$; after the second, $16$, $4$ or $1$; after
the third, $32$, $8$, $2$ or $0.50$. Eight toss sequences lead to four
prices, because a head followed by a tail and a tail followed by a head both
multiply the price by $ud = 1$.

<Definition title="N-period binomial model">
An **$N$-period binomial model** consists of a stock with price $S_0 > 0$
and a coin tossed $N$ times, with outcomes $\omega_1 \omega_2 \cdots
\omega_N$, each $\omega_n \in \{H, T\}$ and every sequence of positive
probability; after each toss the stock price is multiplied by $u$ on heads
and by $d$ on tails, so that
$S_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) = u\,S_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$ and
$S_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T) = d\,S_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$;
together with a money market account that turns one dollar at time $n$ into
$1 + r$ dollars at time $n + 1$, for lending and borrowing alike, with the
same $u$, $d$, $r$ in every period. The model is **admissible** if
$0 < d < 1 + r < u$.
</Definition>

The notation carries one fact: $S_n$ depends on the first $n$ tosses and on
nothing that comes later. Explicitly $S_n = u^{k} d^{\,n-k} S_0$ where $k$ is
the number of heads among the first $n$ tosses, so the price at time $n$ is
determined by the count of heads, not their order; the order is what a
path-dependent claim reads, as the lookback option of the recombining-trees
section does through the running maximum of the path. Admissibility is the
condition of <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period admissibility
theorem</Ref> applied to a single period, and it is all that is assumed: if
it failed, the trade of that theorem run in any one period would be an
arbitrage, and if it holds, that theorem says each period, taken alone, has
none. What $u$, $d$ and $r$ are numerically is left open; the running market
has $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$, and the 80-stock tree of the
backward-induction section has $u = 1.5$, $d = 0.5$, $r = 0.1$.

### The wealth equation: rebalancing paid for out of the portfolio

The one-period lesson's closing exercise priced the strike-$5$ call over two
periods and found a hedge whose share holding changed from
$\Delta_0 = \tfrac{11}{15}$ to $\Delta_1(H) = \tfrac{11}{12}$ at the upper
node. What the exercise did not examine is where the money for the extra
shares came from. At time zero the hedger held $\tfrac{11}{15}$ share, bought
for $2.933$, with a loan of $1.173$ against $X_0 = 1.76$. On heads the shares
are worth $5.867$ and the loan has grown to $1.467$, so the portfolio is
worth $X_1(H) = 4.40$. The new hedge calls for $\tfrac{11}{12}$ share, which
is $\tfrac{11}{60} = 0.183$ share more, costing $0.183 \times 8 = 1.467$ at
the new price. That purchase is financed by borrowing a further $1.467$,
taking the loan to $2.933$: the portfolio is still worth $7.333 - 2.933 =
4.40$ immediately after the trade, exactly what it was worth before. No
money came in and none went out; the shares bought were paid for with debt,
and the debt is part of the portfolio.

That is the whole content of the *wealth equation*. At time $n$ the hedger
has wealth $X_n$, chooses a share holding $\Delta_n$, and holds the
remainder $X_n - \Delta_n S_n$ in the money market. One toss later the
shares are worth $\Delta_n S_{n+1}$ and the money market position has grown
by $1 + r$:
$$
X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)\,(X_n - \Delta_n S_n).
$$

<Definition title="portfolio process and self-financing wealth">
A **portfolio process** in the $N$-period model is a sequence
$\Delta_0, \Delta_1, \ldots, \Delta_{N-1}$ in which $\Delta_n$ may depend on
the first $n$ tosses $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$ and on nothing later. Given
an initial wealth $X_0$ and a portfolio process, the **wealth process** is
defined recursively by the wealth equation
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$, so that $X_n$
depends on the first $n$ tosses. A strategy whose wealth obeys this
equation is **self-financing**: its value changes only through the prices
of the two assets it holds, never through money added or withdrawn.
</Definition>

Four instances fix what the definition covers. The canonical one is the
hedge just examined, whose $\Delta_n$ is read off the claim at each node. A
buy-and-hold position, $\Delta_n = \Delta_0$ for all $n$, is a portfolio
process too, and its wealth equation reduces to a share plus a growing cash
balance, which is the static replication of the first two lessons. A
strategy that holds no shares, $\Delta_n = 0$ throughout, has
$X_n = (1 + r)^n X_0$: the money market alone. The near miss is a rule that
sets $\Delta_n$ by the toss $\omega_{n+1}$, holding a share exactly when the
next toss will be heads; it violates the single clause that $\Delta_n$ is
fixed before toss $n + 1$, and the exercise below measures what that
foresight is worth.

<Exercise>
Write two rules for $\Delta_1$ on the running market stated in terms of the
tosses rather than the price: one reading $\omega_1$, one reading
$\omega_2$. For each, decide whether it is a portfolio process under the
definition, and whether it can be rewritten as a function of $S_1$. For the
one that is not a portfolio process, find the wealth it produces at time
two from $X_0 = 0$ with $\Delta_0 = 0$, as a number at each of the four
time-two nodes.

<Solution>
A rule reading the first toss: $\Delta_1 = 1$ if $\omega_1 = H$ and
$\Delta_1 = 0$ if $\omega_1 = T$. It is a function of $\omega_1$ alone, so it
is a portfolio process. It can be rewritten as a function of $S_1$, since
$S_1 = 8$ exactly when $\omega_1 = H$ and $S_1 = 2$ exactly when
$\omega_1 = T$: $\Delta_1 = 1$ if $S_1 = 8$, else $0$. On a tree where
distinct toss histories give distinct prices, every rule reading the first
$n$ tosses can be rewritten this way; the two descriptions part company
only where the price forgets the path, as at time two, where $HT$ and
$TH$ share the price $4$. A rule reading the second toss:
$\Delta_1 = 1$ if $\omega_2 = H$ and $0$ if $\omega_2 = T$. It is not a
portfolio process, because $\Delta_1$ may depend on $\omega_1$ only, and it
is not a function of $S_1$ either: the paths $HH$ and $HT$ have the same
$S_1 = 8$ and the rule assigns them $1$ and $0$. From $X_0 = 0$ and
$\Delta_0 = 0$ the wealth at time one is $X_1 = 0$ on both branches; at
time two the rule holds a share exactly when the price is about to double,
so $X_2 = \Delta_1 S_2 + 1.25\,(0 - \Delta_1 S_1)$ is
$2S_1 - 1.25\,S_1 = 0.75\,S_1$ when $\omega_2 = H$ and $0$ when
$\omega_2 = T$. At the four nodes: $X_2(HH) = 0.75 \times 8 = 6$,
$X_2(HT) = 0$, $X_2(TH) = 0.75 \times 2 = 1.5$, $X_2(TT) = 0$. Money from
nothing on half the paths and no loss on the rest, an arbitrage in the
sense of <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the first lesson's
definition</Ref>. The clause that decides whether a rule is a portfolio
process is the one clause in the portfolio-process definition, that
$\Delta_n$ depends on the first $n$ tosses and on nothing later.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Remark title="two names for one number">
Shreve's convention, kept here: $X_n$ is the wealth of any self-financing
strategy started from any $X_0$; when $X_0$ and the $\Delta_n$ are chosen to
replicate a claim, the same number is written $V_n$ and called the claim's
price at time $n$. The replication theorem is the statement that the two
coincide at every node.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Every hedging argument in this course is the wealth equation plus one
choice of $\Delta_n$ per date: nothing enters the portfolio after time zero,
and the whole cost of rebalancing is absorbed by the money market line. In
continuous time the same equation becomes
$dX = \Delta\,dS + r\,(X - \Delta S)\,dt$, and
<Ref lesson="black-scholes-pde">the Black–Scholes derivation</Ref> is the
one-line induction step of this lesson's theorem written with that
differential.
</KeyFact>

over the replicating wealth evolves on its own.

At time zero the hedger buys $\tfrac{11}{15}$ share for $2.933$ from
$X_0 = 2.00$, borrowing $0.933$ rather than $1.173$; the loan grows to
$1.167$. On heads the shares are worth $5.867$ and $X_1(H) = 5.867 - 1.167 =
4.70$; on tails they are worth $1.467$ and $X_1(T) = 0.30$. The differences
from $V_1$ are $0.30$ on both branches: $0.24 \times 1.25$, the extra $0.24$
of initial wealth with one period's interest.

At the upper node the hedger holds $\tfrac{11}{12}$ share worth $7.333$
against $X_1(H) = 4.70$, borrowing $2.633$, which grows to $3.292$:
$X_2(HH) = 14.667 - 3.292 = 11.375$ and $X_2(HT) = 3.667 - 3.292 = 0.375$.

$0.375 = 0.24 \times 1.25^2$.

The reason is linearity: if $X_n$ and $V_n$ both obey the wealth equation
with the same $\Delta_n$, their difference obeys
$X_{n+1} - V_{n+1} = (1 + r)(X_n - V_n)$, since the share terms cancel. A
wrong starting wealth is never corrected by the hedge and never made worse
by the coin; it grows at the money market rate, which is what a hedger who
sells the call for $2.00$ and hedges it for $1.76$ collects. The
transferable move: the wealth equation is affine in the starting wealth, so
a hedging error at any date propagates as a deterministic deposit or debt.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Backward induction: the 80-stock call, node by node

The wealth equation propagates a portfolio forward once the $\Delta_n$ and
$X_0$ are known, and the wealth-equation section used deltas taken from the
claim without saying how. What fixes them is the claim's payoff at time $N$, and
it fixes them from the end: at every node of the last period the one-period
problem has a solution, the solutions are the claim's values one period
earlier, and the same step repeats until the root.

### The market and the claim

The tree is the one Cox, Ross and Rubinstein use in the 1979 paper that
named the binomial model, chosen so that every value works out in a few
decimals: a stock at $80$, $u = 1.5$ and $d = 0.5$, a money market growing
by $1.1$ per period, and a European call with strike $80$ expiring after
three periods. The model is admissible, since $0.5 < 1.1 < 1.5$, and the
risk-neutral probability is
$\tilde p = (1.1 - 0.5)/(1.5 - 0.5) = 0.6$, with $\tilde q = 0.4$. The stock
tree is $80$; then $120$ or $40$; then $180$, $60$ or $20$; then $270$, $90$,
$30$ or $10$.

<Example title="a three-period call priced by backward induction (Cox, Ross and Rubinstein 1979, §4)">
**Time three.** The call pays $(S_3 - 80)^+$: $190$ at $270$, $10$ at $90$,
$0$ at $30$, $0$ at $10$.

**Time two.** Each node is a one-period market with the same $u$, $d$, $r$,
so the one-period formula applies with $\tilde p = 0.6$ at all three:
$$
V_2(180) = \frac{0.6 \times 190 + 0.4 \times 10}{1.1} = \frac{118}{1.1} = 107.27, \qquad
V_2(60) = \frac{0.6 \times 10 + 0.4 \times 0}{1.1} = 5.45, \qquad
V_2(20) = 0.
$$

**Time one.** The three time-two values are now the claim to replicate over
the second period:
$$
V_1(120) = \frac{0.6 \times 107.27 + 0.4 \times 5.45}{1.1} = \frac{66.55}{1.1} = 60.50, \qquad
V_1(40) = \frac{0.6 \times 5.45 + 0.4 \times 0}{1.1} = 2.98.
$$

**Time zero.** $V_0 = \dfrac{0.6 \times 60.50 + 0.4 \times 2.98}{1.1} =
\dfrac{37.49}{1.1} = 34.08$.

**The deltas.** At each node the delta is the slope of the next period's two
claim values against the two stock prices:
$$
\Delta_0 = \frac{60.50 - 2.98}{120 - 40} = 0.719, \qquad
\Delta_1(120) = \frac{107.27 - 5.45}{180 - 60} = 0.848, \qquad
\Delta_1(40) = \frac{5.45 - 0}{60 - 20} = 0.136,
$$
$$
\Delta_2(180) = \frac{190 - 10}{270 - 90} = 1, \qquad
\Delta_2(60) = \frac{10 - 0}{90 - 30} = 0.167, \qquad
\Delta_2(20) = \frac{0 - 0}{30 - 10} = 0.
$$
</Example>

### The delta is a process, not a number

The six deltas are not one hedge ratio but a rule that reads the node. From
$0.719$ at the root, a head takes the holding to $0.848$ and a second head
to a full share: at $180$ the call finishes in the money on both branches,
$190$ or $10$, so it behaves like a share less a fixed debt, and the hedge
is a share. From $0.719$ a tail takes the holding to $0.136$ and a second
tail to nothing: at $20$ the call is worthless on both branches, so there is
nothing left to hedge. The intermediate node $60$, where the call is still
undecided, keeps a partial holding of $0.167$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/delta-tree.svg"
  alt="The 80-stock tree with the delta at each of the six hedging nodes, drawn as discs whose shading is the delta: 0.719 at the root, 0.848 and 0.136 at time one, 1.000, 0.167 and 0.000 at time two, with the terminal payoffs 190, 10, 0, 0 listed muted at time three"
  caption="The delta tree: the hedge leans harder as the call goes deeper into the money and lets go as it goes out, reaching a full share at 180 and nothing at 20."

The pattern is general. A call's delta is the slope of its next-period
values against the stock's, and a claim that is in the money on both
branches has slope $1$ while one that is out on both has slope $0$; a node
whose two branches straddle the strike has a slope strictly between. In the
Black–Scholes model the same holding is the derivative
$\partial c / \partial S$, called delta there too, and
<Ref lesson="greeks-hedging-error">its sensitivity to the stock</Ref>, gamma,
is what makes the discrete rebalancing of this lesson cost something when it
is done only at intervals.

<KeyFact>
On the 80-stock tree the call is worth $34.08$ and the hedge starts at
$0.719$ share, a process rather than a number: the shares' contribution to
wealth between two dates is $\Delta_n\,(S_{n+1} - S_n)$, a holding fixed
before the price move it multiplies, and the sum of those terms over dates
is what becomes the stochastic integral $\int \Delta\,dS$ in
<Ref lesson="ito-integral">the Itô integral lesson</Ref>, where the rule
that $\Delta_n$ cannot read the toss after it is the whole construction.
</KeyFact>

## The hedge walked tick by tick: the portfolio meets the claim at every node

The values $V_n$ were produced by a recursion, and the claim that they are
prices rests on a forward check: a hedger who starts with $V_0$ and follows
the deltas must hold exactly the payoff at time $N$ whichever way the coin
falls. This section runs that check on two paths of the 80-stock call, with
every trade and every loan written out, and records what it shows at the
intermediate dates as well as the last.

### Path H, H, T: the call is delivered

The hedger sells the call for $34.08$, buys $\Delta_0 = 0.719$ share for
$57.52$, and borrows the difference, $23.44$.

**Time one, heads, stock at 120.** The shares are worth $86.28$ and the loan
has grown to $25.79$: the portfolio is worth $60.50$, which is $V_1(120)$.
The new delta is $0.848$, so the hedger buys $0.130$ share more at $120$,
spending $15.54$, and borrows it, taking the loan to $41.32$. The portfolio
is still worth $101.82 - 41.32 = 60.50$.

**Time two, heads, stock at 180.** The shares are worth $152.73$ and the
loan $45.45$: the portfolio is worth $107.27 = V_2(180)$. The new delta is
$1$; the hedger buys the remaining $0.152$ share at $180$ for $27.27$ on
credit, and the loan becomes $72.73$.

**Time three, tails, stock at 90.** The share is worth $90$ and the loan
$80.00$. The call is exercised against the hedger, who delivers the share
and receives the strike $80$, repays the loan with it, and has nothing left:
the position is worth $90 - 80 = 10$, exactly the call's payoff
$(90 - 80)^+$, and it has been paid out in full. Had the last toss been
heads, the share would be worth $270$ against a loan of $80$ and a call
payoff of $190$: the same zero.

Two facts have been observed that the theorem must reproduce. First, the

problem is a one-period market whose claim is $V_{n+1}$ on the two branches,
and <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period pricing theorem</Ref>
applied there says that any quote for the derivative other than $V_n$ hands
an arbitrage to a trader who starts trading at that node. A derivative in
the multiperiod model has a price at every node, and the $V_n$ of the
recursion are those prices.

node, not only at expiry: buy the claim back at $V_n$, sell the
$\Delta_n$ shares and repay the loan, and be left with exactly zero. A
claim whose holder may demand settlement at a date of their own choosing is
therefore not outside the method: the seller has a portfolio worth $V_n$ at
every node to settle with, and the question

is at which nodes $V_n$ covers the payoff of immediate exercise.
</KeyFact>

## The theorem: replication on every path, proved by induction

Three paths have been walked out of eight, on one tree, for one claim. The
theorem covers every path, every $N$, and every claim whose payoff is fixed
by the $N$ tosses, and its proof is the wealth equation for one period plus
the identity that defined $\tilde p$; the induction adds nothing but the
bookkeeping of which tosses are known when.

### Setting up: values and deltas as functions of tosses

A derivative security in the $N$-period model pays $V_N(\omega_1 \cdots
\omega_N)$ at time $N$, a known function of the full toss sequence: the call
pays $(S_N - K)^+$, the put $(K - S_N)^+$, and the lookback option of the
recombining-trees section pays $\max_{k \le N} S_k - S_N$, which reads the
whole path. Backward
induction defines $V_n$ as a function of the first $n$ tosses by
$$
V_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)\bigr],
$$
and the delta at that node as the slope
$$
\Delta_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \frac{V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) - V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)}{S_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) - S_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)}.
$$
The denominator is $(u - d)\,S_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$, nonzero because
$u \ne d$ and $S_n > 0$.

### The induction step is the one-period identity

The proof mode is forward induction on $n$, with the claim $X_n = V_n$ on
every path of length $n$. The roadmap: (1) the base case is the choice
$X_0 = V_0$; (2) at a fixed node, write $X_{n+1}$ on the heads branch from
the wealth equation; (3) substitute the delta and the induction hypothesis;
(4) use $(1 + r)V_n = \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)$, which is
the recursion read backwards, and $\tilde p + \tilde q = 1$; (5) repeat for
the tails branch. Step (4) is the entire content; steps (2) and (3) are
substitution.

  src="./figures/induction-step.svg"

  caption="The induction step at one node: the share term contributes q̃ times the spread of next-date values, the money market term contributes the recursion, and the two add to (p̃ + q̃) V_{n+1}(H), which is V_{n+1}(H) because p̃ + q̃ = 1."

<Theorem title="replication in the multiperiod binomial model (Shreve I, Theorem 1.2.2)">
Consider an admissible $N$-period binomial model, with
$\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}$ and $\tilde q = \frac{u - 1 - r}{u - d}$.
Let $V_N$ be a derivative security paying at time $N$, a function of the $N$
tosses, and define $V_{N-1}, \ldots, V_0$ by the backward recursion and
$\Delta_0, \ldots, \Delta_{N-1}$ by the slope formula above. If $X_0 = V_0$
and $X_1, \ldots, X_N$ are defined by the wealth equation with these
$\Delta_n$, then
$$
X_N(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N) = V_N(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)
\quad \text{for every toss sequence } \omega_1 \cdots \omega_N.
$$

<Proof>
The statement proved by induction is stronger than the conclusion:
$X_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = V_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$ for every
$n$ from $0$ to $N$ and every sequence of $n$ tosses. The case $n = N$ is
the theorem.

**Base case, $n = 0$.** $X_0 = V_0$ by hypothesis.

**Induction step.** Fix $n < N$ and a sequence $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$,
and assume $X_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = V_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$.
The toss $\omega_{n+1}$ is not yet known, so both values are computed. To
lighten the notation the fixed tosses $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$ are
suppressed: $X_n$, $V_n$, $S_n$, $\Delta_n$ are their values at the fixed
node, and $V_{n+1}(H)$, $S_{n+1}(H) = uS_n$ are their values on the heads
branch out of it.

*Heads.* The wealth equation gives
$$
X_{n+1}(H) = \Delta_n\,uS_n + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)
= (1 + r)X_n + \Delta_n S_n\,\bigl(u - (1 + r)\bigr).
$$
The delta at the node is
$\Delta_n = \dfrac{V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)}{(u - d)S_n}$, so
$\Delta_n S_n\,(u - 1 - r) = \bigl(V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)\dfrac{u - 1 - r}{u - d}
= \tilde q\,\bigl(V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)$. By the induction hypothesis
$X_n = V_n$, and by the recursion
$(1 + r)V_n = \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)$. Therefore
$$
X_{n+1}(H) = \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(H) - \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)
= (\tilde p + \tilde q)\,V_{n+1}(H) = V_{n+1}(H).
$$

*Tails.* The wealth equation gives
$X_{n+1}(T) = \Delta_n\,dS_n + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n) = (1 + r)X_n +
\Delta_n S_n\,\bigl(d - (1 + r)\bigr)$. Now
$\Delta_n S_n\,(d - 1 - r) = \bigl(V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)\dfrac{d - 1 - r}{u - d}
= -\tilde p\,\bigl(V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)$, since
$\dfrac{1 + r - d}{u - d} = \tilde p$. With the hypothesis and the
recursion as before,
$$
X_{n+1}(T) = \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T) - \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(T)
= (\tilde p + \tilde q)\,V_{n+1}(T) = V_{n+1}(T).
$$

Reinstating the suppressed tosses, $X_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n
\omega_{n+1}) = V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n \omega_{n+1})$ for both
values of $\omega_{n+1}$. Since $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$ was arbitrary,
the statement holds for $n + 1$ on every path, and the induction is
complete.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### What the proof did not use, and the price it defines

Two things the proof never touched fix how far the theorem reaches: the
constancy of $u$, $d$, $r$ across dates, and the actual probability of a
head.

<Remark title="node-by-node generality">
A tree in which $u$, $d$ and $r$ vary from node to node, each triple known
at the node and admissible there, is priced by the same recursion with the
node's own $\tilde p_n = \frac{1 + r_n - d_n}{u_n - d_n}$; the proof runs
unchanged, one node at a time. Constant coefficients buy one thing, that a
single $\tilde p$ serves everywhere, and that is what lets the closed-form
exercise among the closing exercises unroll the recursion into one binomial
sum. Baxter and Rennie's tree keeps a separate $q_j$ at every node for
exactly this reason.
</Remark>

The theorem defines the price, and the argument closing the hedge-walk
section, that any other quote at any node hands an arbitrage to a trader who
starts there, says why the definition is forced.

<Definition title="price of a derivative security at time n (Shreve I, Definition 1.2.3)">
In an admissible $N$-period binomial model the **price at time $n$** of a
derivative security paying $V_N$ is the random variable
$V_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$ produced by the backward recursion, for
$n = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1$; the **price at time zero** is $V_0$. The
**replicating portfolio** is the process $\Delta_0, \ldots, \Delta_{N-1}$ of
the theorem, started from $X_0 = V_0$.
</Definition>

<Remark title="the actual probability, once">
The actual probability $p$ of a head appears in neither the recursion, nor
the delta, nor the proof. It was used exactly once: in the requirement that
every toss sequence has positive probability, so that "every path" in the
theorem is the same thing as "every path that can occur". A hedge that
worked on all paths but one of probability zero would be as good, and a path
of positive probability on which the hedge missed would be an arbitrage
against whoever sold at $V_0$. The separation established in
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period lesson</Ref> holds for
every $N$: $p$ governs the profit distribution of any position that is not
hedged, and nothing about the price.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The theorem is stated for a payoff that is any function of the $N$ tosses,
and its proof never asks whether two paths with the same stock price carry
the same value: replication is path-general, the recombining-tree shortcut
of the next section is not, and the two must not be confused. What
<Ref lesson="martingale-representation-ftap">the second fundamental
theorem</Ref> calls completeness, that every claim can be hedged, is exactly
this theorem's conclusion for the tree.
</KeyFact>

## Recombining trees: N + 1 nodes instead of 2^N paths, and when the shortcut fails

The recursion of the theorem runs over toss sequences, and there are $2^N$
of them. A tree with a hundred periods, which is a modest size for a pricer,
has $2^{100} \approx 10^{30}$ terminal sequences, and an algorithm that
tabulates $V_{100}$ on all of them does not finish. The same tree has $101$
terminal prices, and for a claim whose payoff reads only $S_N$ that count is
the one that matters.

### One value per price: the functions $v_n(s)$

The call on the running market's three-period tree, priced in the
three-period pricing exercise on the running market, had
$V_2(HT) = V_2(TH) = 1.20$. That is not a
coincidence of the numbers. The two nodes have the same stock price $4$, so
the two branches out of each are the same pair of prices $8$ and $2$, and
for a call the two terminal payoffs are the same pair $3$ and $0$; the
one-period formula then returns the same value. The same reasoning applies
one date earlier, and the conclusion is that for a claim paying $g(S_N)$ the
value at time $n$ is a function $v_n$ of $S_n$ alone,
$V_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = v_n\bigl(S_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)\bigr)$,
with the recursion written on prices:
$$
v_n(s) = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,v_{n+1}(us) + \tilde q\,v_{n+1}(ds)\bigr], \qquad
\delta_n(s) = \frac{v_{n+1}(us) - v_{n+1}(ds)}{(u - d)\,s}.
$$
At time $n$ there are $n + 1$ prices, so the pass from time $n + 1$ to time
$n$ costs $n + 1$ evaluations, and the whole pricing costs about $N^2/2$
rather than $2^N$.

<Example title="a three-period put on the running market (Shreve I, Example 1.3.1)">
The put with strike $5$ on the \$4 stock, three periods,
$\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$, discount $0.8$ per period.

**Time three.** $v_3(32) = 0$, $v_3(8) = 0$, $v_3(2) = 3$, $v_3(0.50) =
4.50$: four values, where the path form has eight entries.

**Time two.** $v_2(16) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 0 + \tfrac12 \times 0) = 0$;
$v_2(4) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 0 + \tfrac12 \times 3) = 1.20$;
$v_2(1) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 3 + \tfrac12 \times 4.50) = 3$.

**Time one.** $v_1(8) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 0 + \tfrac12 \times 1.20) =
0.48$; $v_1(2) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 1.20 + \tfrac12 \times 3) = 1.68$.

**Time zero.** $v_0(4) = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 0.48 + \tfrac12 \times 1.68)
= 0.864$.

**Deltas.** $\delta_0(4) = (0.48 - 1.68)/(8 - 2) = -0.2$;
$\delta_1(8) = (0 - 1.20)/(16 - 4) = -0.1$; $\delta_1(2) = (1.20 - 3)/(4 - 1)
= -0.6$; $\delta_2(16) = 0$; $\delta_2(4) = (0 - 3)/(8 - 2) = -0.5$;
$\delta_2(1) = (3 - 4.50)/(2 - 0.50) = -1$. Every delta is negative or zero:
the put's hedge sells the stock short, most heavily where the put is
deepest in the money.

Check against the call: $v_0^{\text{call}} - v_0^{\text{put}} = 2.304 -
0.864 = 1.44$, and $S_0 - K/(1 + r)^3 = 4 - 5/1.953 = 1.44$, which is
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">put–call parity</Ref> in per-period form
at the root; the parity exercise among the closing exercises checks it at
every node.
</Example>

### The lookback: the same price, a different value

A claim that reads the path breaks the collapse. The *lookback option* on
the running market pays $\max_{0 \le k \le 3} S_k - S_3$ at time three: the
gap between the highest price seen and the final price. Its eight payoffs
are, path by path,
$$
\begin{aligned}
&V_3(HHH) = 32 - 32 = 0, \quad V_3(HHT) = 16 - 8 = 8, \quad V_3(HTH) = 8 - 8 = 0, \quad V_3(HTT) = 8 - 2 = 6, \\
&V_3(THH) = 8 - 8 = 0, \quad V_3(THT) = 4 - 2 = 2, \quad V_3(TTH) = 4 - 2 = 2, \quad V_3(TTT) = 4 - 0.50 = 3.50.
\end{aligned}
$$
The paths $HHT$ and $HTH$ end at the same price $8$ and pay $8$ and $0$: no
function of $S_3$ reproduces the payoff. The backward recursion, which the
theorem states for functions of tosses, runs anyway:
$V_2(HH) = 0.8\,(0 + 4) = 3.20$, $V_2(HT) = 0.8\,(0 + 3) = 2.40$,
$V_2(TH) = 0.8\,(0 + 1) = 0.80$, $V_2(TT) = 0.8\,(1 + 1.75) = 2.20$; then
$V_1(H) = 0.8\,(1.60 + 1.20) = 2.24$, $V_1(T) = 0.8\,(0.40 + 1.10) = 1.20$;
and $V_0 = 0.8\,(1.12 + 0.60) = 1.376$, with
$\Delta_0 = (2.24 - 1.20)/(8 - 2) = 0.173$. The nodes $HT$ and $TH$ both
have $S_2 = 4$ and carry the values $2.40$ and $0.80$: same price, different
running maximum, different value.

  src="./figures/recombining.svg"
  alt="Left, a two-period tree of the four-dollar stock in which the paths HT and TH meet at the price 4 and the call has the single value 1.20 there; right, the same two paths for the lookback shown as separate nodes, 4 with maximum 8 worth 2.40 and 4 with maximum 4 worth 0.80"
  caption="For a call the paths HT and TH merge into one node with one value; for the lookback they reach the same price with different maxima and stay two nodes with two values."

The replication theorem holds for the lookback exactly as for the call:
the hedge $\Delta_0 = 0.173$, $\Delta_1(H) = (3.20 - 2.40)/12 = 0.067$,
$\Delta_1(T) = (0.80 - 2.20)/3 = -0.467$, and the four time-two deltas
replicate it on all eight paths. The belief that a recombining tree is what
makes the replication argument work, so that a claim whose values do not
recombine is outside its reach, is wrong. Recombination was never a
hypothesis of the theorem: the theorem indexes values by toss sequences,
and its proof handles one node of the sequence tree at a time without ever
asking whether two nodes share a price.

What recombination provides is something else. A path-independent claim's
values happen to agree at sequences with equal $S_n$, which is a property
of the payoff and not of the tree, so the $2^n$ entries of $V_n$ can be
stored as $n + 1$ entries of $v_n$. The tree of sequences is always the
object; the tree of prices is a compression of it that a path-independent
claim permits and a path-dependent one does not. The pair of nodes at
$S_2 = 4$ separates the two cases: the call has one delta there, $0.5$, and
the lookback has two, computed in the lookback-hedge exercise that closes
this section.

For the lookback the compression is partial rather than absent. Its value at
time $n$ depends on the path only through the pair $(S_n, M_n)$ with
$M_n = \max_{k \le n} S_k$, so it can be tabulated on pairs: at time three
there are six distinct pairs, $(32, 32)$, $(8, 16)$, $(8, 8)$, $(2, 8)$,
$(2, 4)$, $(0.50, 4)$, rather than eight sequences, and the recursion on
pairs is $v_n(s, m) = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,v_{n+1}(us, \max(m, us)) +
\tilde q\,v_{n+1}(ds, m)]$. The count of pairs grows like $N^2$ rather than
$2^N$; the general principle, that a claim is priced on whatever state
variables its payoff reads, is the Markov property that
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>
states precisely.

<KeyFact>
Backward induction over sequences costs $2^N$; over prices it costs about
$N^2/2$, and at $N = 100$ that is $10^{30}$ against $5{,}000$. The saving is
available exactly when the payoff is a function of $S_N$, or more generally
of a small set of state variables, and it is a fact about the claim, never
about the theorem.
</KeyFact>

$2$; only the claim's values differ.

$\Delta_2(HT) = \dfrac{V_3(HTH) - V_3(HTT)}{8 - 2} = \dfrac{0 - 6}{6} = -1$
and $\Delta_2(TH) = \dfrac{V_3(THH) - V_3(THT)}{8 - 2} = \dfrac{0 - 2}{6} =

**From $HT$.** Wealth $2.40$, short one share at $4$ brings in $4$, so the
money market holds $2.40 + 4 = 6.40$, growing to $8.00$. On heads the short
share costs $8$ to close: $8.00 - 8 = 0 = V_3(HTH)$. On tails it costs $2$:
$8.00 - 2 = 6 = V_3(HTT)$.

**From $TH$.** Wealth $0.80$, short a third of a share brings in $1.333$,
money market $2.133$, growing to $2.667$. On heads closing the short costs
$2.667$: $2.667 - 2.667 = 0 = V_3(THH)$. On tails it costs $0.667$: $2.667 -
0.667 = 2 = V_3(THT)$.

Both hedges land exactly, as the theorem says they must; the price tree
cannot hold them because it has one node where the sequence tree has two.
The transferable move: when a claim reads the path, index the hedge by the
path, or by the state variables the payoff reads, and the theorem's
recursion is unchanged.
</Solution>

What is established: a self-financing strategy is a choice of $\Delta_n$ at
each node with wealth propagated by
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$; the price of any
claim on an admissible $N$-period tree is produced by the backward
recursion $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)]$
from its payoff, with the hedge $\Delta_n$ the slope at each node, and the
replication theorem, proved by induction with the one-period identity as
its only step, says the hedge started from $V_0$ equals $V_n$ at every node
of every path; the 80-stock call costs $34.08$ and its hedge runs from
$0.719$ share to a full share or none; and the recursion collapses from
$2^N$ sequences to $N + 1$ prices exactly when the payoff reads $S_N$ alone.
What is not established is what the recursion is. It averages $V_{n+1}$
over the toss not yet seen, given the tosses already seen, with the weights
$\tilde p$ and $\tilde q$, and that is a conditional expectation on the
space of toss sequences; <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the next
lesson</Ref> constructs that space, defines the conditional expectation on
it, and proves the five properties that turn the recursion into
$V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$, after which
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>
shows that the discounted price process this recursion builds is a fair
game under $\tilde p$.

<Exercise>
On the 80-stock tree ($S_0 = 80$, $u = 1.5$, $d = 0.5$, $1 + r = 1.1$,
three periods) price the European put with strike $80$: every $V_n$ and
every $\Delta_n$. Then compare each of the put's six deltas with the call's
at the same node and state the relation.

<Solution>
**Time three.** $(80 - S_3)^+$: $0$ at $270$, $0$ at $90$, $50$ at $30$,
$70$ at $10$.

**Time two.** $P_2(180) = 0$; $P_2(60) = (0.6 \times 0 + 0.4 \times 50)/1.1 =
18.18$; $P_2(20) = (0.6 \times 50 + 0.4 \times 70)/1.1 = 58/1.1 = 52.73$.

**Time one.** $P_1(120) = (0 + 0.4 \times 18.18)/1.1 = 6.61$;
$P_1(40) = (0.6 \times 18.18 + 0.4 \times 52.73)/1.1 = 32.00/1.1 = 29.09$.

**Time zero.** $P_0 = (0.6 \times 6.61 + 0.4 \times 29.09)/1.1 =
15.60/1.1 = 14.18$.

**Deltas.** $\Delta_0 = (6.61 - 29.09)/80 = -0.281$;
$\Delta_1(120) = (0 - 18.18)/120 = -0.152$;
$\Delta_1(40) = (18.18 - 52.73)/40 = -0.864$;
$\Delta_2(180) = 0$; $\Delta_2(60) = (0 - 50)/60 = -0.833$;
$\Delta_2(20) = (50 - 70)/20 = -1$.

Against the call's $0.719$, $0.848$, $0.136$, $1$, $0.167$, $0$: at every
node the call's delta minus the put's delta is $1$. The reason is that
$(S_3 - 80)^+ - (80 - S_3)^+ = S_3 - 80$, a forward, whose values against the
stock have slope $1$ at every node; the delta is a slope, and slopes
subtract. Parity at the root: $34.08 - 14.18 = 19.90$ and
$80 - 80/1.1^3 = 80 - 60.105 = 19.895$; the unrounded prices $34.080$ and
$14.185$ give $19.895$ on both sides.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For a claim paying $g(S_N)$ on an admissible $N$-period tree with constant
$u$, $d$, $r$, prove that backward induction unrolls to the closed form
$$
V_0 = \frac{1}{(1 + r)^N}\sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k}\,\tilde p^{\,k}\,\tilde q^{\,N-k}\,g\bigl(u^k d^{\,N-k} S_0\bigr),
$$
and check it on the 80-stock call.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Induct on $N$, treating the value at time one as the
root value of an $(N - 1)$-period tree started at $uS_0$ or $dS_0$.

For $N = 1$ the sum is $\frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde q\,g(dS_0) + \tilde p\,g(uS_0)]$,
the one-period formula. Assume the closed form for $(N - 1)$-period trees
with any starting price. On an $N$-period tree the subtree from the node
$H$ is an $(N - 1)$-period tree started at $uS_0$ with the same $u$, $d$,
$r$, and the backward recursion on it is the backward recursion of the
$N$-period tree restricted to sequences beginning with $H$; so by hypothesis
$$
V_1(H) = \frac{1}{(1 + r)^{N-1}} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} \binom{N-1}{j}\tilde p^{\,j}\tilde q^{\,N-1-j}\,g\bigl(u^{j+1} d^{\,N-1-j} S_0\bigr),
$$
and likewise $V_1(T)$ with $g(u^j d^{\,N-j} S_0)$. Then
$V_0 = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T)]$ collects, for
each $k$, the coefficient of $g(u^k d^{\,N-k} S_0)$: from $V_1(H)$ with
$j = k - 1$, $\tilde p \cdot \binom{N-1}{k-1}\tilde p^{\,k-1}\tilde q^{\,N-k}$,
and from $V_1(T)$ with $j = k$,
$\tilde q \cdot \binom{N-1}{k}\tilde p^{\,k}\tilde q^{\,N-1-k}$; both equal
$\tilde p^{\,k}\tilde q^{\,N-k}$ times a binomial coefficient, and
$\binom{N-1}{k-1} + \binom{N-1}{k} = \binom{N}{k}$. The discount factors
multiply to $(1 + r)^{-N}$.

**Check.** $N = 3$, $\tilde p = 0.6$: the weights are $0.6^3 = 0.216$ on
$270$, $3 \times 0.36 \times 0.4 = 0.432$ on $90$, $0.288$ on $30$, $0.064$
on $10$, and
$V_0 = (0.216 \times 190 + 0.432 \times 10 + 0 + 0)/1.331 = 45.36/1.331 =
34.08$. This is Cox, Ross and Rubinstein's equation for the call, and
$\binom{N}{k}\tilde p^k \tilde q^{N-k}$ is the probability of $k$ heads in
$N$ tosses of a coin with heads probability $\tilde p$: the price is the
discounted expectation of the payoff under that coin, which is the
one-period reveal repeated $N$ times. The transferable move: a recursion
with constant coefficients unrolls into a sum over paths weighted by path
probabilities, and paths that end at the same price pool their weights.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) Run the theorem on the claim that pays $1$ at time $N$ on every path.
Find $V_n$ and $\Delta_n$ at every node and say what has been priced.
(b) Run it on the claim that pays $S_N$, one share, at time $N$. Find $V_n$
and $\Delta_n$ and say what the hedge does. In each case name the property
of $\tilde p$ that the computation used.

<Solution>
**(a)** At time $N - 1$ both branches pay $1$, so
$V_{N-1} = \frac{1}{1 + r}(\tilde p + \tilde q) = \frac{1}{1 + r}$ at every
node, and $\Delta_{N-1} = (1 - 1)/(\ldots) = 0$. The same step repeats:
$V_n = (1 + r)^{-(N - n)}$ and $\Delta_n = 0$ throughout. The claim is a
zero-coupon bond, its price is the discount factor, and the hedge holds no
stock at any date: the money market alone, started with $(1 + r)^{-N}$,
grows to $1$. The property used is $\tilde p + \tilde q = 1$; nothing about
$u$, $d$ or the value of $\tilde p$ entered.

**(b)** At time $N - 1$ at a node with stock price $s$, the two branches pay
$us$ and $ds$, so $V_{N-1} = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,us + \tilde q\,ds] =
s$, using the defining property of $\tilde p$, that
$\tilde p\,u + \tilde q\,d = 1 + r$; and $\Delta_{N-1} = (us - ds)/(us - ds)
= 1$. Repeating, $V_n = S_n$ and $\Delta_n = 1$ at every node: the claim is
the stock, its price is the stock's price, and the hedge buys one share at
time zero and holds it, with the money market line identically zero. The
theorem, asked to price the stock, returns the stock; had it returned
anything else, the market of stock and money market alone would contain an
arbitrage, which admissibility excludes. The transferable move: the two
properties of $\tilde p$, summing to one and pricing the stock at its
growth rate $1 + r$, are the two boundary cases of the theorem, and every
other claim is a combination that the recursion handles the same way.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "On a recombining tree the hedge $\Delta_n$ depends only
on the current stock price $S_n$; therefore replication cannot handle
path-dependent claims, whose values depend on more than $S_n$." Split the
claim into its two halves, say which is true, for which claims, and name
the step that fails in the inference. Use the lookback's two nodes at
$S_2 = 4$ as evidence.

<Solution>
**First half.** "$\Delta_n$ depends only on $S_n$" is true for a claim
paying $g(S_N)$, because then $V_{n+1}$ is a function $v_{n+1}(S_{n+1})$ and
the slope formula reads only $v_{n+1}(uS_n)$, $v_{n+1}(dS_n)$ and $S_n$. It
is false in general: the lookback has $\Delta_2(HT) = -1$ and
$\Delta_2(TH) = -\tfrac13$ at two nodes with $S_2 = 4$. Whether the stock
tree recombines is irrelevant to the truth of this half; what matters is
whether the claim's values recombine, which is a property of the payoff.

**Second half.** "Replication cannot handle path-dependent claims" is
false, and the step that fails is "therefore". The replication theorem is
stated for $V_N$ any function of the $N$ tosses and its proof handles one
node of the sequence tree at a time, never asking whether two nodes share a
price. The lookback is replicated: from $X_2(HT) = 2.40$ with $\Delta = -1$
the wealth equation lands on $0$ and $6$, and from $X_2(TH) = 0.80$ with
$\Delta = -\tfrac13$ on $0$ and $2$, the four terminal payoffs on those
branches. What path dependence removes is the right to store the hedge on
the price tree, so the hedger must carry the path, or the state variables
the payoff reads, here $(S_n, M_n)$. The correct statement: replication is
a theorem about sequences; the price tree is a storage optimisation valid
exactly for path-independent claims.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A student hedging the three-period strike-$5$ call on the running market
(values from the three-period pricing exercise on the running market:
$V_2(16) = 12$, $V_2(4) = 1.20$, $V_1(8) = 5.28$) computes the time-one delta on heads as
$$
\Delta_1(H) = \frac{V_2(HH) - V_2(HT)}{S_2(HH) - S_2(TT)}
$$
and hedges with it from $X_1(H) = 5.28$. Find the error, compute the wrong
delta and the correct one, and give the portfolio's value at both time-two
nodes out of $H$; state at which node the wealth first misses the claim and
by how much, and what happens to the miss afterwards if the remaining
deltas are correct.

<Solution>
The denominator mixes branches: $S_2(TT) = 1$ is not a successor of the
node $H$. The correct denominator is $S_2(HH) - S_2(HT) = 16 - 4 = 12$, and
the correct delta is $(12 - 1.20)/12 = 0.9$. The student's denominator is
$16 - 1 = 15$, giving $10.8/15 = 0.72$.

With $0.72$ share bought at $8$ for $5.76$ from wealth $5.28$, the loan is
$0.48$, growing to $0.60$. At $HH$ the shares are worth $11.52$ and the
portfolio $11.52 - 0.60 = 10.92$ against $V_2(HH) = 12$: short by $1.08$. At
$HT$ the shares are worth $2.88$ and the portfolio $2.28$ against
$V_2(HT) = 1.20$: over by $1.08$. The first miss is at time two, on both
branches, of equal size and opposite sign. The size is
$(\Delta - \Delta^\ast)\,S_1\,(u - 1 - r) = (0.72 - 0.9) \times 8 \times
0.75 = -1.08$ on heads and $(0.72 - 0.9) \times 8 \times (0.5 - 1.25) =
+1.08$ on tails, which is the bracket in the theorem's induction step with
a delta that does not cancel it.

Afterwards, with correct deltas, the miss is a starting-wealth error at
time two and the wrong-starting-wealth exercise of the wealth-equation
section applies: it grows by $1 + r$ per period and the coin does not touch
it. Along $HH$ the hedger reaches time
three short by $1.08 \times 1.25 = 1.35$ on both branches; along $HT$, over
by $1.35$. The transferable move: a wrong delta produces a miss of opposite
signs on the two branches, proportional to the delta error times the
stock's spread around $1 + r$; a wrong wealth produces a miss of one sign
that compounds.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Put–call parity was proved in <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the
model-free lesson</Ref> as a statement about time-zero prices. On the
running market's three-period tree, using the call values of the
three-period pricing exercise on the running market and the put values of
the three-period put example, check
$c_n - p_n = S_n - K/(1 + r)^{3 - n}$ at all six non-terminal nodes, and
then prove it holds at every node of every admissible tree for any strike,
from the backward recursion alone.

<Solution>
**The check.** Time two: $12 - 0 = 12$ and $16 - 5/1.25 = 12$;
$1.20 - 1.20 = 0$ and $4 - 4 = 0$; $0 - 3 = -3$ and $1 - 4 = -3$. Time one:
$5.28 - 0.48 = 4.80$ and $8 - 5/1.5625 = 8 - 3.20 = 4.80$; $0.48 - 1.68 =
-1.20$ and $2 - 3.20 = -1.20$. Time zero: $2.304 - 0.864 = 1.44$ and
$4 - 5/1.953 = 1.44$. Six nodes, six agreements.

**The proof.** The backward recursion is linear in the payoff: if $V_N$ and
$W_N$ are two claims, the recursion applied to $V_N - W_N$ produces
$V_n - W_n$ at every node, because each step is a fixed weighted average
followed by a fixed discount. So $c_n - p_n$ is the price process of the
claim $(S_N - K)^+ - (K - S_N)^+ = S_N - K$, a forward. The bond-and-stock
exercise priced its two parts: the claim paying $S_N$ has price $S_n$ at every node,
and the claim paying $K$ has price $K(1 + r)^{-(N - n)}$. By linearity
again, $c_n - p_n = S_n - K(1 + r)^{-(N - n)}$ at every node.

The model-free proof used two static portfolios held to expiry; this proof
uses the tree and gets more, an identity between price processes rather
than between two numbers, and correspondingly the hedges satisfy
$\Delta^c_n - \Delta^p_n = 1$ at every node, as the 80-stock put exercise
found. The transferable move: linearity of backward induction turns
every identity between payoffs into an identity between price processes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the 80-stock tree with $\tilde p = 0.6$, treat $\tilde p$ as the
probability of heads and compute two expectations. (a) The expected
discounted stock at time three, $\tilde E[S_3]/1.1^3$, and compare it with
$S_0$. (b) The expected discounted call value at each date,
$\tilde E[V_n]/1.1^n$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3$, using the values of the worked
example. Report what the four numbers in (b) have in common and whether
(a) fits the pattern.

<Solution>
**(a)** The terminal prices $270$, $90$, $30$, $10$ have probabilities
$0.216$, $0.432$, $0.288$, $0.064$ under a $0.6$ coin, so
$\tilde E[S_3] = 58.32 + 38.88 + 8.64 + 0.64 = 106.48$ and
$106.48/1.331 = 80.00 = S_0$.

**(b)** $n = 0$: $34.08$. $n = 1$: $(0.6 \times 60.50 + 0.4 \times
2.98)/1.1 = 34.08$. $n = 2$: $(0.36 \times 107.27 + 0.48 \times 5.45 +
0.16 \times 0)/1.21 = 41.24/1.21 = 34.08$. $n = 3$: $(0.216 \times 190 +
0.432 \times 10)/1.331 = 34.08$.

All four are the same number, the price, and (a) fits: the discounted
stock's expectation at time three is the stock's price at time zero. Under
the weights $\tilde p$, the discounted value of every claim in the model,
the stock included, has an expectation that does not move with $n$. The
reason is visible in the recursion, which says the discounted value at any
node is the $\tilde p$-average of the discounted values one step later, so
averaging over the whole tree date by date returns the root each time. A
process whose conditional average of the next value is the current value is
a *martingale*, and the property just observed, that discounted prices are
martingales under $\tilde p$, is what
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>
proves in general once <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">conditional
expectation on the coin-toss space</Ref> has been defined; the recursion of
this lesson is that theorem computed by hand.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

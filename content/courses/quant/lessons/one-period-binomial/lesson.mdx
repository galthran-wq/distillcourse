Add the smallest possible model of movement — the stock goes to one of two
values — and the price interval collapses to a point: every claim is
replicated by $\Delta$ shares plus a bond position, solved from two linear
equations.

This is the third move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs. <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">The first lesson</Ref>
manufactured a forward's payoff from a loan and a share and read the price off
the cost; <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second</Ref> showed that with
no model of the stock the same method fences a call into an interval and ties
it to the put, and delivers no number, because a static line cannot match a
corner.

This lesson adds the one assumption that closes the fence: at the end
of the period the stock takes one of exactly two values. Under it every payoff
is a line through two points, the replicating portfolio is the solution of two
linear equations, its cost is the claim's only arbitrage-free price, and the
constants that appear while solving are the object the rest of the course is
built on. What stands open at the end is whether the construction survives
more than one period, which <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the next
lesson</Ref> settles by chaining it.

The route: the model and the inequality that makes it admissible, proved in
both directions; the \$4 stock and the \$5 call replicated in dollars, with the
trade against every other quote; the general solution, in which the delta is
a slope and two multipliers appear as bookkeeping and turn out to be
probabilities; the refutation of the reading that anyone has been assumed
indifferent to risk; and the reason two states, rather than three, is what the
method requires.

## The model: two prices next period, and the inequality that admits it

The fence of the last lesson had width because $S_T$ could take many values
on both sides of the strike. The model here restricts it to two, and before
anything is priced in it two things have to be fixed: what the restriction
assumes, and which parameter values are consistent with the absence of
arbitrage at all.

### Retrieval: what the last two lessons fixed

### The two assets

Time runs from $0$ to $1$: one period, of whatever length the application
needs. The stock costs $S_0 > 0$ now. At time one a coin is tossed; on heads
the price is $S_1(H) = uS_0$ and on tails it is $S_1(T) = dS_0$, where $u$ and
$d$ are fixed positive numbers with $d < u$, the *up factor* and the *down
factor*. The coin lands heads with probability $p$ and tails with probability
$q = 1 - p$; both are positive, and nothing else about them is assumed. The
model does not claim to know how likely a rise is. It claims that a rise and a
fall are both possible and that these two are the only outcomes.

The second asset is the *money market account*: a dollar deposited now returns
$1 + r$ at time one, and a dollar borrowed now costs $1 + r$ at time one, the
same rate both ways. This is the per-period convention announced in
<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the first lesson's compounding
remark</Ref>: for a period of length $\Delta t$ it corresponds to
$1 + r = e^{r_c \Delta t}$ with $r_c$ the continuously compounded rate, and the
mathematics needs only $r > -1$.

<Figure

  alt="A one-period branch: the stock at 4 goes to 8 on heads or 2 on tails, cash grows from 1 to 1.25 in both states, and beside each terminal node the call with strike 5 is worth 3 or 0"
  caption="The running market: the stock at 4 goes to 8 or 2, cash grows by 1.25 either way, and the call with strike 5 is worth 3 or 0."

<Definition title="one-period binomial model">
A **one-period binomial model** consists of a stock with price $S_0 > 0$ at
time zero and price $S_1 = uS_0$ on heads or $S_1 = dS_0$ on tails at time
one, with $0 < d < u$ and both outcomes of positive probability, together
with a money market account that turns one dollar at time zero into $1 + r$
dollars at time one, for lending and for borrowing alike, with $r > -1$. The
model is **admissible** if $d < 1 + r < u$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix what the definition covers, and the last clause is the
subject of the theorem that follows.

- **The canonical case.** $S_0 = 4$, $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$:
  the stock goes to $8$ or $2$ and cash grows by $1.25$. This is the course's
  running market; the multiperiod modules reuse it. Admissible, since
  $\tfrac12 < 1.25 < 2$.
- **A case with a different surface.** A stock at $20$ will be worth $22$ or
  $18$ in three months, and the continuously compounded rate is $12\%$ a year:
  $u = 1.1$, $d = 0.9$, and $1 + r = e^{0.12 \times 0.25} = e^{0.03} = 1.0305$
  for the quarter. Admissible, since $0.9 < 1.0305 < 1.1$. Nothing requires the
  period to be a year or the moves to be large.
- **The boundary case.** $u = 1.5$, $d = 1.1$, $r = 0.2$: the stock rises in
  both states. The definition nowhere requires $d < 1$; "down" means less
  than up, and the model is admissible because $1.1 < 1.2 < 1.5$. Cash still
  beats the stock on tails.
- **The near miss.** $u = 1.25$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$: the same
  running market with the up move shrunk until $u = 1 + r$. The clause that
  fails is $1 + r < u$ and only that clause; the theorem below shows what goes
  wrong.

A prompt for an instance of your own: take a stock whose price you know, write
a one-month model for it by choosing $u$ and $d$ from the range of moves you
consider possible and $1 + r$ from a monthly deposit rate, and decide which of
the two admissibility inequalities is the one your triple comes closest to
violating.

### The condition is a theorem, not an assumption

The inequality $d < 1 + r < u$ is usually stated as an assumption, and it is
a theorem: each inequality that fails hands one of the two assets a free
profit against the other. The converse also holds, and the pair is the whole
admissibility content of the model.

<Theorem title="admissibility (Shreve I, condition (1.1.2) and Exercise 1.1)">
In the market consisting of the stock and the money market account alone,
there is no arbitrage if and only if $d < 1 + r < u$.

<Proof>
The proof mode is direct in both directions: exhibit the arbitrage when an
inequality fails, and show that every zero-cost position fails one clause of
the definition when both inequalities hold.

**If $d \ge 1 + r$.** Borrow $S_0$ and buy one share. Cost now: zero. At time
one the debt is $(1 + r)S_0$ and the share is worth $uS_0$ or $dS_0$. On
tails the position is worth $dS_0 - (1 + r)S_0 \ge 0$, so it never loses;
on heads it is worth $uS_0 - (1 + r)S_0 > dS_0 - (1 + r)S_0 \ge 0$, a strict
gain, with probability $p > 0$. All three clauses hold: an arbitrage.

**If $u \le 1 + r$.** Sell one share short and deposit the proceeds $S_0$.
Cost now: zero. At time one the deposit is worth $(1 + r)S_0$ and replacing
the share costs at most $uS_0 \le (1 + r)S_0$: no loss in either state. On
tails the share costs $dS_0 < uS_0 \le (1 + r)S_0$, a strict gain, with
probability $q > 0$. An arbitrage.

**If $d < 1 + r < u$.** A position set up at zero cost holds $\Delta$ shares,
for any real $\Delta$, and cash $-\Delta S_0$. At time one it is worth
$\Delta S_0\,(u - 1 - r)$ on heads and $\Delta S_0\,(d - 1 - r)$ on tails.
The first parenthesis is positive and the second negative. If $\Delta > 0$
the position loses on tails; if $\Delta < 0$ it loses on heads; each of those
events has positive probability, so clause 2 fails. If $\Delta = 0$ the
position is worth zero in both states and clause 3 fails. No zero-cost
position in stock and cash is an arbitrage.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three comprehension checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: cash must
beat the stock in one state and lose to it in the other, otherwise one of the
two assets dominates the other and the dominated one is sold against it. The
step that uses $p > 0$ and $q > 0$: clause 3 in the first two cases, where the
strict gain has to be possible, and clause 2 in the third, where the loss has
to be possible. The case $d = 1 + r$ exactly: the buy-on-credit position never
loses and gains on heads, which is why the inequality in the theorem is strict
and the near miss above is excluded.

<KeyFact>
Admissibility constrains three numbers, $u$, $d$ and $1 + r$, and no
probability: whether $d < 1$, whether $r > 0$, whether a rise is likelier
than a fall, none of it enters. The same two inequalities return in the
general solution as the condition $0 < \tilde p < 1$ on the multiplier that
prices every claim, so a model that fails one of them does not only contain
free money in stock and cash; it hands the pricing formula a weight at or
outside the ends of $(0, 1)$, and the boundary exercise at the end shows what
that formula then prices.
</KeyFact>

## Replication in dollars: the \$4 stock, the \$5 call, and \$1.20

With the model admissible, the claim to price is the one the last two lessons
could not: the call with strike $5$ on the \$4 stock, worth $3$ on heads and
$0$ on tails. The last lesson's closing exercise found, by minimising over
static portfolios, that the corridor for this call collapses to the single
number $1.20$. This section rebuilds the number as a replication and then
writes out the trade against every other quote.

<Example title="the call replicated (Shreve I, Example 1.1.1)">
The market: $S_0 = 4$, $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$, so
$S_1(H) = 8$, $S_1(T) = 2$, and a dollar grows to $1.25$. The claim: a
European call with strike $K = 5$, worth $(8 - 5)^+ = 3$ on heads and
$(2 - 5)^+ = 0$ on tails, where $x^+$ denotes $\max(x, 0)$.

Start with wealth $X_0 = 1.20$ and buy $\Delta_0 = \tfrac12$ share. The half
share costs $2$, so $0.80$ is borrowed: the cash position is
$X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0 = 1.20 - 2 = -0.80$. At time one the debt has grown to
$1.25 \times 0.80 = 1$.

On heads the half share is worth $\tfrac12 \times 8 = 4$, and the portfolio is
worth $4 - 1 = 3$. On tails the half share is worth $\tfrac12 \times 2 = 1$,
and the portfolio is worth $1 - 1 = 0$. In both states the portfolio's value
equals the call's payoff. The call has been replicated, and $1.20$ is what
the replication cost.
</Example>

The half share and the $1.20$ were taken as given here; the next section
derives them. What can be verified at once is that no other price survives.

### The two arbitrages

**Quote 1.50.** Sell the call for $1.50$. Use $1.20$ of the proceeds to run
the replication, buying half a share and borrowing $0.80$, and deposit the
excess $0.30$. At time one the replication is worth exactly what the call
pays, $3$ or $0$, and is handed over to the call's buyer; the deposit has
grown to $0.375$. Net at time one: $0.375$ in both states, from nothing.

**Quote 1.00.** Buy the call for $1.00$ and run the replication in reverse:
sell half a share short for $2$, deposit $0.80$, and deposit the remaining
$0.20$ separately. At time one, on heads, the call pays $3$, the deposit
returns $1$, and buying back the half share costs $4$: net $0$. On tails the
call pays $0$, the deposit returns $1$, and the half share costs $1$: net
$0$. The separate deposit has grown to $0.25$ in both states.

  src="./figures/arbitrage-tables.svg"

Right, a call quoted at 1.00: buy the call, short half a share, deposit 0.80 and 0.20; the net is 0 now and +0.25 in both states"

In both trades the net is $|\text{quote} - 1.20| \times 1.25$, collected
whichever way the coin lands. At a quote of $1.20$ the excess deposit is
zero, and the seller who hedges ends the period with exactly nothing in both
states: the position that cancels the call has zero value in every state and
so has zero value now. This is the first lesson's forward theorem in a new
setting, with the loan-and-share replaced by a half-share-and-loan.

### The same hedge from the writer's desk

The construction is the one Cox, Ross and Rubinstein open their 1979 paper
with, in a market with the same $u$, $d$ and $r$ scaled to a \$50 stock and
seen from the option writer's side rather than the buyer's.

<Example title="write three calls, buy two shares, borrow 40 (Cox, Ross and Rubinstein 1979, §2)">
A stock at $S = 50$ will be worth $25$ or $100$ at the end of the period;
borrowing and lending cost $25\%$; a call with strike $50$ expires at the end
of the period, paying $0$ or $50$. Its price $C$ is the unknown.

The hedge: write three calls at $C$ each, buy two shares for $100$, and
borrow $40$, to be repaid with interest as $50$.

| | now | $S^* = 25$ | $S^* = 100$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| write 3 calls | $+3C$ | $0$ | $-150$ |
| buy 2 shares | $-100$ | $+50$ | $+200$ |
| borrow 40 at 25% | $+40$ | $-50$ | $-50$ |
| **total** | $3C - 60$ | $0$ | $0$ |

The position breaks even at expiry in both states, so to admit no arbitrage
it must cost nothing now: $3C - 100 + 40 = 0$, and $C = 20$. If $C = 25$ the
same position brings in $15$ now and owes nothing later; if $C = 15$ the
reverse, buy three calls, short two shares and lend $40$, does the same.

Per call the hedge is $\tfrac23$ of a share and a loan of $13.33$, which
costs $33.33 - 13.33 = 20$. The ratio of three calls to two shares is the
hedge ratio $\tfrac23 = (50 - 0)/(100 - 25)$, larger than the running
market's $\tfrac12$ because this call is at the money and its payoffs are
further apart. The paper's own comment on what the calculation used is the
sentence this course keeps returning to: "we do not need to know the
probability that the stock price will rise or fall. Bulls and bears must
agree on the value of the call, relative to its underlying stock price."
</Example>

<Exercise>
The quarter-year market from the definition's second instance: a stock at
$20$ goes to $22$ or $18$, cash grows by $e^{0.03} = 1.0305$, and a call with
strike $21$ pays $1$ or $0$. Find the share holding, the loan, and the price
of the call. Then a dealer quotes the call at $1.00$: write the three-row
table of the trade against him and the net in both states. Write the share
holding before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** The share holding must make the stock leg's *spread*
between the two states equal the call's spread; the loan then cancels the
stock leg in the state where the call pays nothing.

The call's payoffs differ by $1$ and the stock's by $4$, so the holding is
$\tfrac14$ share, worth $5.50$ on heads and $4.50$ on tails. On tails the
call pays nothing, so the loan must repay exactly $4.50$: borrow
$4.50 / 1.0305 = 4.367$ now. The portfolio costs
$0.25 \times 20 - 4.367 = 5 - 4.367 = 0.633$, and at time one it is worth
$5.50 - 4.50 = 1$ on heads and $4.50 - 4.50 = 0$ on tails: the call.

**Quote 1.00.** The quote is above $0.633$, so sell the call and run the
hedge, depositing the excess $1.00 - 0.633 = 0.367$, which grows to
$0.367 \times 1.0305 = 0.378$.

| | now | $S_1 = 22$ | $S_1 = 18$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| sell one call at 1.00 | $+1.00$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
| buy ¼ share, borrow 4.367 | $-0.633$ | $5.50 - 4.50 = +1$ | $4.50 - 4.50 = 0$ |
| deposit 0.367 | $-0.367$ | $+0.378$ | $+0.378$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $+0.378$ | $+0.378$ |

The net is $(1.00 - 0.633) \times 1.0305 = 0.378$ in both states. The
transferable move: match the spread with shares, cancel the worst state with
the loan, and every other quote is the gap with interest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Aside>
The assumptions the trades used: shares can be subdivided
(half a share is bought); the borrowing rate equals the lending rate; the
stock is bought and sold at the same price, with no bid–offer spread; and
the stock takes exactly two values next period. The first three are the
market assumptions of the forward theorem in per-period form, and the fourth
is new. The first three carry over unchanged into the Black–Scholes model of
the fifth module; the fourth is replaced there by geometric Brownian motion,
and the last section of this lesson explains why the replacement has to be
a model with a single source of randomness. Subdividing shares matters less
than it looks: a desk hedging a hundred of these calls buys fifty shares.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Every quote other than $1.20$ hands the counter-trader

never asks which state is likelier: a seller of the call at $1.20$ who hedges
is indifferent to the coin, and that indifference is what the next section's
multipliers turn out to express.
</KeyFact>

## The general solution: the delta is a slope, and the multipliers that cancel it are probabilities

The half share was verified, not derived. For a general claim the two
equations of the replication have to be solved, and the way the solution is
organised, one subtraction and one weighted sum, is the source of every
pricing formula in the binomial modules.

### Two equations, two unknowns

A *derivative security* in the one-period model is anything that pays a known
amount $V_1(H)$ on heads and a known amount $V_1(T)$ on tails: the call pays
$(S_1 - K)^+$, the put pays $(K - S_1)^+$, the forward pays $S_1 - K$. The
hedger starts with wealth $X_0$, buys $\Delta_0$ shares, and holds the rest,
$X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0$, in the money market, borrowing if it is negative. At time
one the portfolio is worth
$$
X_1 = \Delta_0 S_1 + (1 + r)(X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0),
$$
and replication demands $X_1 = V_1$ in both states:
$$
\Delta_0 S_1(H) + (1 + r)(X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0) = V_1(H), \qquad
\Delta_0 S_1(T) + (1 + r)(X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0) = V_1(T).
$$
Two linear equations, two unknowns $X_0$ and $\Delta_0$; everything else is
known now.

### The delta: subtract, and read a slope

Subtracting the tails equation from the heads equation removes $X_0$ and
everything multiplied by $(1 + r)$:
$$
\Delta_0 = \frac{V_1(H) - V_1(T)}{S_1(H) - S_1(T)}.
$$

<Definition title="delta">
The **delta** of a derivative security in the one-period model is the number
of shares in its replicating portfolio,
$\Delta_0 = \dfrac{V_1(H) - V_1(T)}{S_1(H) - S_1(T)}$: the change in the claim's
payoff per dollar of change in the stock's price across the two states.
</Definition>

The running market gives $\Delta_0 = 3/6 = \tfrac12$, the \$50 market gives
$50/75 = \tfrac23$, and the quarter-year market gives $1/4$. The formula is
a difference quotient: plot the claim's payoff against the stock's, two
points appear, and $\Delta_0$ is the slope of the line through them. A forward
has slope $1$, a claim paying $1$ in both states has slope $0$, and a put has
negative slope, meaning the hedge sells the stock short. In the fifth module
the two points move together, the difference quotient becomes the derivative
$\partial c / \partial S$ of the option's price with respect to the stock's,
and <Ref lesson="black-scholes-pde">the Black–Scholes hedge</Ref> holds
exactly that many shares.

### The multipliers: choose them to make the delta disappear

The delta is one unknown; the price is the other, and it can be found
without substituting the delta back. Multiply the heads equation by a
number $\tilde p$, the tails equation by $\tilde q = 1 - \tilde p$, and add:
$$
(1 + r)X_0 + \Delta_0\bigl[\tilde p\,S_1(H) + \tilde q\,S_1(T) - (1 + r)S_0\bigr]
= \tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T).
$$
This holds for every choice of $\tilde p$. Choose the one that makes the
bracket vanish, so that $\Delta_0$ drops out. The bracket is zero when
$\tilde p\,uS_0 + (1 - \tilde p)\,dS_0 = (1 + r)S_0$, that is,
$\tilde p\,(u - d) = 1 + r - d$:
$$
\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}, \qquad \tilde q = \frac{u - 1 - r}{u - d},
$$
and with that choice
$$
X_0 = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T)\bigr].
$$
In the running market $\tilde p = 0.75 / 1.5 = \tfrac12$ and
$X_0 = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 3 + \tfrac12 \times 0) = 1.20$. In the
quarter-year market $\tilde p = (1.0305 - 0.9)/0.2 = 0.6523$ and
$X_0 = 0.6523 / 1.0305 = 0.633$, both matching the numbers found by hand.

<Theorem title="one-period replication and pricing (Shreve I, (1.1.7)–(1.1.10) and Exercise 1.2)">
In an admissible one-period binomial model let a derivative security pay
$V_1(H)$ on heads and $V_1(T)$ on tails. Then (i) there is exactly one
portfolio $(X_0, \Delta_0)$ of initial wealth and share holding whose time-one
value equals $V_1$ in both states, given by the two formulas above; and (ii)
the market of stock, money market account and derivative security admits no
arbitrage if and only if the derivative's time-zero price is
$$
V_0 = X_0 = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T)\bigr].
$$

<Proof>
The plan: (i) is linear algebra; (ii) has two directions, an explicit
arbitrage at any other price, and at $V_0$ a reduction of every zero-cost
position to one in stock and cash, which the admissibility theorem already
excludes.

**(i) Existence and uniqueness.** The two equations are linear in
$(X_0, \Delta_0)$ with coefficient matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 + r & S_1(H) - (1 + r)S_0 \\ 1 + r & S_1(T) - (1 + r)S_0 \end{pmatrix},
$$
whose determinant is $(1 + r)\,(S_1(T) - S_1(H)) = -(1 + r)(u - d)S_0$,
nonzero because $u \ne d$, $S_0 > 0$ and $r > -1$. So the solution exists and
is unique, and the subtraction and the weighted sum computed it.

**(ii) Any other price is an arbitrage.** Suppose the derivative trades at
$V_0' > V_0$. Sell it, spend $V_0$ of the proceeds on the replicating
portfolio, and deposit $V_0' - V_0$. Cost now: zero. At time one the
portfolio is worth $V_1$, which settles the derivative sold, and the deposit
is worth $(1 + r)(V_0' - V_0) > 0$ in both states. Suppose instead
$V_0' < V_0$. Buy the derivative and take the negative of the replicating
portfolio, selling $\Delta_0$ shares and holding cash $-(X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0)$;
that position brings in $X_0 = V_0$ now and is worth $-V_1$ at time one.
Deposit the difference $V_0 - V_0' > 0$. Cost now: zero; at time one the
derivative and the reversed portfolio cancel and the deposit is worth
$(1 + r)(V_0 - V_0') > 0$ in both states.

**(ii) At $V_0$ there is none.** A zero-cost position holds $\alpha$
derivatives, $a$ shares and cash $b$ with $\alpha V_0 + aS_0 + b = 0$. Each
derivative pays what $\Delta_0$ shares plus cash $X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0$ pay, so
the position's time-one value equals that of $a + \alpha\Delta_0$ shares and
cash $b + \alpha(X_0 - \Delta_0 S_0)$, a position in stock and cash alone whose
cost now is $aS_0 + b + \alpha X_0 = 0$. By the admissibility theorem no
zero-cost position in stock and cash is an arbitrage, so neither is this one.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three comprehension checks. The idea in one sentence: a claim that is a
portfolio in disguise has the portfolio's price, and in two states every
claim is such a portfolio. The step that uses admissibility: only the last,
through the admissibility theorem; part (i) needs $u \ne d$ and nothing
about $1 + r$, so the replication exists even in an inadmissible model, and
the boundary exercise at the end examines what it then prices. The argument
applied to a claim paying $1$ in both states: $\Delta_0 = 0$ and
$X_0 = 1/(1 + r)$, the zero-coupon bond, as it must be.

### The reveal: the multipliers lie in (0, 1), sum to one, and price the stock

From the two preceding lessons one would expect no probability of any kind to
appear in an arbitrage price, and none was put in: $\tilde p$ was introduced
as a multiplier chosen to kill a term. Yet the pricing formula has the exact
shape of a discounted expectation, and the multiplier has every property a
probability of heads would need.

First, $\tilde q = 1 - \tilde p$ by construction. Second, $\tilde p > 0$
exactly when $1 + r > d$, and $\tilde p < 1$ exactly when $1 + r < u$: the two
admissibility inequalities are precisely the statement that $\tilde p$ lies
strictly between $0$ and $1$. Third, the equation that defined $\tilde p$,
rearranged, reads
$$
S_0 = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,S_1(H) + \tilde q\,S_1(T)\bigr]:
$$
under the weights $(\tilde p, \tilde q)$ the stock's average growth is
$1 + r$, the same as cash's. The multipliers can be regarded as probabilities
of heads and tails, and under them the price of every claim, the stock
included, is its discounted average payoff.

  src="./figures/lever.svg"
  alt="A number line of growth factors with d = ½, 1 + r = 1.25 and u = 2 marked; a brace above spans 1 + r − d = 0.75 and a brace below spans u − d = 1.5, giving p-tilde = ½; notes at the ends record that p-tilde tends to 0 as 1 + r approaches d, to 1 as it approaches u, and leaves (0, 1) outside, where the admissibility theorem's trades apply"
  caption="The risk-neutral probability locates the riskless growth between the down and up factors: p̃ is the fraction of the way from d to u, and admissibility is exactly 0 < p̃ < 1."

<Definition title="risk-neutral probabilities">
In an admissible one-period binomial model the **risk-neutral probabilities**
are
$$
\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}, \qquad \tilde q = 1 - \tilde p = \frac{u - 1 - r}{u - d},
$$
the unique weights in $(0, 1)$ under which the stock's expected growth factor
is $1 + r$. The **risk-neutral pricing formula** is
$V_0 = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_1(H) + \tilde q\,V_1(T)]$.
</Definition>

What the multipliers are not is the actual probabilities $p$ and $q$. They
are numbers chosen to make the term multiplying $\Delta_0$ drop out, and the
solution of the two equations is the only place they were needed.

Under the actual probabilities the stock typically satisfies
$S_0 < \frac{1}{1 + r}[p\,S_1(H) + q\,S_1(T)]$, an average growth above
cash's, which is the compensation investors demand for carrying the stock's
risk: in the running market with $p = \tfrac23$ the discounted expected stock
is $(\tfrac23 \times 8 + \tfrac13 \times 2)/1.25 = 4.80$, against a price of
$4$.

The weights that make the stock's average growth equal cash's are read
off the prices $S_0$, $uS_0$, $dS_0$ and $1 + r$ in the same way that
<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the second bookmaker's odds</Ref> were
read off the wagers: they are the odds that make the book balance, and the
book here is the hedge. The price is an expectation; it is not an expected
value.

<KeyFact>
$\tilde p$ is fixed by $u$, $d$ and $r$ and by nothing else, so two traders
who disagree about every probability of the coin agree about $\tilde p$;
every price in the binomial modules is an expectation under $\tilde p$
discounted at $r$, and the actual $p$ enters none of them.
</KeyFact>

the stock goes up and nothing otherwise, and give its replicating portfolio.
(b) In the general admissible model, price a forward contract with delivery

<Solution>

$V_0 = \tfrac13 \times 1 + \tfrac23 \times 0 = \tfrac13$. The delta is
$(1 - 0)/(2 - 0.5) = \tfrac23$ of a share, costing $\tfrac23$; the cash
position is $\tfrac13 - \tfrac23 = -\tfrac13$, a borrowing of $\tfrac13$. Check:
on up, $\tfrac23 \times 2 - \tfrac13 = 1$; on down,

## The refutation: no one was assumed indifferent to risk

The formula's shape invites a reading that its derivation does not support,
and the reading is common enough to be the way the standard textbook
introduces the principle. The correct claim first: the price $1.20$ is the
cost of a hedge that removes the call's risk entirely, and no statement about
any investor's attitude to risk entered the computation.

The misconception, stated once: "risk-neutral valuation means assuming that
investors are risk-neutral, so the price is what the option would be worth in
a world where nobody demanded compensation for bearing risk." Hull's chapter
does introduce the principle with the words "we can make the assumption that
investors are risk-neutral", and then has to add that the world is not
risk-neutral and that the price is nonetheless right for it, which is the
point at which a reader concludes the assumption was made and then excused.

The reading is wrong. Nothing was assumed about investors; $\tilde p$ is the
unique solution of a linear equation in the stock's own prices,
$\tilde p\,uS_0 + (1 - \tilde p)\,dS_0 = (1 + r)S_0$. Risk preferences do act
on prices: how much investors demand for carrying the stock's risk is part of
why $S_0$ is $4$ rather than $3$ or $5$. But once $S_0$, $u$, $d$ and $r$ are
given, the call's value relative to them is fixed by a hedge that carries no
risk to be compensated, and the multipliers that express the hedge's cost as
an average are a fact about those four numbers. The replacement model is the
bookmaker's book: odds read off the wagers balance the book whatever anyone
believes about the horses, and $\tilde p$ read off the prices balances the
hedge whatever anyone believes about the coin.

  src="./figures/p-flat.svg"
  alt="Dollars now against the real-world probability of heads from 0 to 1: a blue line for the discounted expected payoff of the call, 2.4p, rising from 0 to 2.40, and a flat amber line at 1.20 for the arbitrage price; they cross at p = ½, and at p = 0.9 the expectation reads 2.16"
  caption="The arbitrage price is flat in the real-world probability; the discounted expected payoff is a line through it at p = p̃, and every other point on that line is a quote that is sold and hedged."

The discriminating example is Hull's own. In the quarter-year market a
real-world expected return of $16\%$ a year on the stock means a real-world
heads probability $p$ with $22p + 18(1 - p) = 20e^{0.04}$, so $p = 0.7041$;
the call's expected payoff is then $0.7041$, against $0.6523$ under
$\tilde p$. Raise the expected return to $30\%$ and $p$ becomes $0.8894$ and
the expected payoff $0.8894$. The price is $0.633$ in both cases. The extra
expected payoff is what an investor who holds the call unhedged is paid for
carrying its risk, at a discount rate nobody can state, since the call is
riskier than the stock by an amount that depends on the holder; the hedger
carries no risk and needs no rate but $r$. The delayed retrieval of this
point is the third exercise at the end.

### What the actual probability does govern

The actual $p$ is not irrelevant to everything, and the precise scope of its
irrelevance is part of the result. It governs the profit-and-loss
distribution of any position that is not hedged: a buyer of the running
market's call at $1.20$ who believes $p = 0.9$ expects
$0.9 \times 3 = 2.70$ at time one for an outlay worth $1.50$ then, a
favourable bet with a one-in-ten chance of losing the stake, and a bet is
what it stays. It governs whether $u$ and $d$ are a sensible description of
the stock at all, which is a statistical question about the stock's record,
taken up when <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the tree is
refined</Ref> and $u$ and $d$ are chosen to match a measured volatility. What
$p$ does not govern, given $u$, $d$ and $r$, is the price.

<KeyFact>
The one trace the actual probability leaves in a price is the set of states
it makes possible: the admissibility trades needed $p > 0$ and $q > 0$ so
that a gain or a loss could occur, and used nothing else about them. That is
the form the separation keeps for the rest of the course, where
<Ref lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">the change of measure</Ref>
requires the actual and the risk-neutral probabilities to agree on which
events are possible and on nothing else.
</KeyFact>

## Why two states: replication is a spanning condition

The theorem's hypothesis that the stock takes two values, and not three, was
used at exactly one point: two equations in two unknowns have a solution. The
point is worth its own section because it is the reason the binomial model,
and not some richer one, is the discrete model of this course, and because
the same condition reappears in continuous time.

### Two assets span two states

A payoff at time one is a vector with one coordinate per state. In two
states the stock is $(uS_0, dS_0)$ and one dollar of cash is
$(1 + r, 1 + r)$; the two are linearly independent exactly when $u \ne d$,
and two independent vectors span the plane. Every claim $(V_1(H), V_1(T))$ is
a combination $\Delta_0 \times \text{stock} + b \times \text{cash}$, which is
part (i) of the theorem restated: the running market's call, $(3, 0)$, is
$\tfrac12 (8, 2) - 0.8\,(1.25, 1.25)$.

### A third state, and the fence comes back

<Example title="a third state, and the fence comes back">
Let the \$4 stock go to $8$, $4$ or $2$, with cash growing by $1.25$ as
before, and price the call with strike $5$, paying $(3, 0, 0)$. A portfolio
of $a$ shares and $b$ dollars pays $(8a + 1.25b,\ 4a + 1.25b,\ 2a + 1.25b)$.
Matching the two zero states forces $4a + 1.25b = 2a + 1.25b$, so $a = 0$ and
then $b = 0$, and the portfolio pays $0 \ne 3$ in the first state. Matching
the first two states instead gives $a = \tfrac34$, $b = -2.4$, which pays
$1.5 - 3 = -1.5 \ne 0$ in the third. No $(a, b)$ reaches the call.

What survives is the last lesson's method. The cheapest portfolio that pays
at least the call in all three states is $a = \tfrac12$, $b = -0.8$, which
pays $(3, 1, 0)$ and costs $1.20$; the dearest that pays at most the call is
$a = \tfrac34$, $b = -2.4$, which pays $(3, 0, -1.5)$ and costs $0.60$. The
corridor is $[0.60, 1.20]$: sixty cents wide, every price in it consistent
with no arbitrage in stock, cash and call. The third state did not make the
model worse as a description of the stock. It made the call unhedgeable by
stock and cash, and an unhedgeable claim has an interval, not a price.
</Example>

Cox, Ross and Rubinstein state the obstruction in the same terms: with a
third state, "a choice of $\Delta$ and $B$ that would equate the returns in
two states could not in the third", and their closing section turns the
observation into a theorem, that two-state movement each period is necessary
as well as sufficient for options of every maturity and strike to be priced
by arbitrage using stock and bonds alone. The condition is about spanning,
not about realism: a trinomial stock is a legitimate model of a stock, and
it can still be priced once a third traded instrument is added to span the
third direction. With stock and bond only, two states is the exact
requirement.

### What the continuous limit has to meet

The requirement is what the continuous-time model of the fourth and fifth
modules meets in the limit. A stock driven by a single Brownian motion moves,
over an instant, in one of two directions along one axis, so stock and bond
span every instantaneous payoff and the Black–Scholes hedge exists;
<Ref lesson="martingale-representation-ftap">the second fundamental
theorem</Ref> makes that statement precise, and a second, independent source
of randomness in the volatility is exactly what breaks it in the closing
modules.

<KeyFact>
Two states is not a claim about how stocks move but a count: with two traded
assets, exactly two states can be spanned, and a claim that lies outside the
span has a corridor rather than a price, the same corridor no-arbitrage gave
before any model was written down.
</KeyFact>

What is established: a one-period model is admissible exactly when
$d < 1 + r < u$, and each violated inequality is an explicit trade; in an
admissible model every claim is replicated by $\Delta_0 = \frac{V_1(H) -
V_1(T)}{S_1(H) - S_1(T)}$ shares and a money market position, its cost is
the only arbitrage-free price, and that cost is $\frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,
V_1(H) + \tilde q\, V_1(T)]$ with $\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}$ a
number in $(0, 1)$ read off $u$, $d$ and $r$; the actual probability of a
rise moves the expected profit of an unhedged position and nothing else; and
the construction works because two assets span two states.

What is not established is anything beyond one period, or whether quotes
posted by an
exchange obey the module's theorems at all.

<Exercise>
Set $u = 1 + r$ in the running market, so that $u = 1.25$, $d = \tfrac12$,

$\tilde p = (1.25 - 0.5)/(1.25 - 0.5) = 1$ and $\tilde q = 0$: all the weight

$0 + \tfrac43$ deposited grows to $\tfrac53$, and the portfolio pays
$-\tfrac13 \times 5 + \tfrac53 = 0$ on heads and
$-\tfrac13 \times 2 + \tfrac53 = 1$ on tails.

The price is zero for a claim that pays $1$ with probability $q > 0$ and
never pays less than zero. Buying it is an arbitrage: zero cost, no loss, a
gain on tails. Equivalently the replicating portfolio itself, one third of a
share sold short with the proceeds deposited, costs nothing and pays

stock and lend, which the failed inequality $1 + r < u$ was there to exclude.
With $u = 1 + r$ the stock cannot beat cash, so cash is a dominant asset and
the model contains free money before any derivative is added. The pricing
formula does not break; it prices the free money correctly at zero.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "In the running market, if the probability of heads is
$0.9$ rather than $0.5$, the call must be worth more than the $1.20$
computed with $\tilde p = \tfrac12$, because it is far likelier to pay off."
Give the explicit trade in both directions against a quote based on the
claim, then say what does change when $p = 0.9$.

<Solution>
With $p = 0.9$ the discounted expected payoff is
$0.8 \times (0.9 \times 3) = 2.16$. A dealer who quotes $2.16$ is sold the
call: the seller buys half a share and borrows $0.80$ for $1.20$, deposits
the excess $0.96$, delivers $3$ or $0$ from the hedge at time one, and keeps
$0.96 \times 1.25 = 1.20$ in both states. Any quote above $1.20$ is sold the
same way; any quote below is bought, with half a share shorted and $0.80$
deposited, collecting the gap with interest in both states. The trades use
$S_0$, $u$, $d$ and $r$ and never $p$, so they punish the same quotes for
every $p$.

The step that fails in the claim is "must be worth more": the claim computes
an expected payoff and treats it as a price, and the previous two lessons
established that an expectation is a long-run average that enforces nothing
while a hedge enforces a point. What does change at $p = 0.9$ is the
distribution of an unhedged buyer's profit: paying $1.20$ now, worth $1.50$
at time one, for a payoff of $3$ nine times in ten, the buyer expects to
net $1.20$ at time one and loses $1.50$ one time in ten. The stock itself is
a similar bet at that $p$, with expected growth $0.9 \times 8 + 0.1 \times 2 =
7.40$ against $5$ for cash. Both are good bets and neither is an arbitrage;
the price at which the call trades is where a hedger is indifferent, and
that is $1.20$ for every $p$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the price of a dollar delivered in each state directly. Let $\pi_H$
be the time-zero price of a claim paying $1$ on heads and $0$ on tails, and
$\pi_T$ the price of the claim paying $0$ on heads and $1$ on tails. (a) Use
the fact that the money market account and the stock are themselves claims to
write two equations for $(\pi_H, \pi_T)$, solve them, and express the
solution through $\tilde p$ and $\tilde q$. (b) Show that any claim is priced
by $V_0 = \pi_H V_1(H) + \pi_T V_1(T)$, and compute $\pi_H$, $\pi_T$ in the
running market.

<Solution>
**(a)** A dollar of cash pays $(1 + r, 1 + r)$ and costs $1$; the stock pays
$(uS_0, dS_0)$ and costs $S_0$. Each of the two state claims is replicable by
part (i) of the theorem, so by the law of one price a claim's cost is the
sum of its payoffs weighted by the state prices:
$$
1 = (1 + r)\,\pi_H + (1 + r)\,\pi_T, \qquad S_0 = uS_0\,\pi_H + dS_0\,\pi_T.
$$
From the first, $\pi_H + \pi_T = 1/(1 + r)$; from the second,
$u\pi_H + d\pi_T = 1$. Substituting $\pi_T = 1/(1 + r) - \pi_H$ into the
second: $(u - d)\pi_H = 1 - d/(1 + r)$, so
$$
\pi_H = \frac{1 + r - d}{(u - d)(1 + r)} = \frac{\tilde p}{1 + r}, \qquad
\pi_T = \frac{\tilde q}{1 + r}.
$$
The state prices are the risk-neutral probabilities discounted one period.

**(b)** A claim paying $(V_1(H), V_1(T))$ is $V_1(H)$ units of the heads
claim plus $V_1(T)$ units of the tails claim, so its cost is
$\pi_H V_1(H) + \pi_T V_1(T) = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p V_1(H) + \tilde q V_1(T)]$:
the pricing formula again, now read as "a dollar in each state has a price,
and a claim is a bundle of state dollars". In the running market
$\pi_H = \pi_T = 0.5/1.25 = 0.40$: forty cents buys a dollar delivered on
heads, forty cents a dollar on tails, and eighty cents buys a dollar for
certain, which is $1/1.25$. The call costs $0.40 \times 3 + 0.40 \times 0 =
1.20$. This is the route Cox, Ross and Rubinstein call the complete-markets
interpretation, and the state prices per unit of probability return in
<Ref lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">the second module</Ref> as the
state-price density.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

replicate over the first period: $V_0 = 0.8\,(\tfrac12 \times 4.40 +
\tfrac12 \times 0) = 1.76$ and $\Delta_0 = (4.40 - 0)/(8 - 2) = \tfrac{11}{15}
\approx 0.733$.

Check of the first-period hedge: $1.76$ buys $\tfrac{11}{15}$ share for
$2.933$ with a loan of $1.173$, which grows to $1.467$. On heads the shares
are worth $5.867$ and the portfolio $4.40$; on tails they are worth $1.467$
and the portfolio $0$. At the upper node the $4.40$ is reinvested as
$\tfrac{11}{12}$ share for $7.333$ with a loan of $2.933$, growing to
$3.667$: on heads $14.667 - 3.667 = 11$, on tails $3.667 - 3.667 = 0$. The
hedge matches at every node and the share holding changes as the stock
moves.

The pass just performed, from the last date backward one period at a time,
each step a one-period replication, is the algorithm of
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the next lesson</Ref>, and the changing
delta is the dynamic hedge that a static position could not provide.
</Solution>

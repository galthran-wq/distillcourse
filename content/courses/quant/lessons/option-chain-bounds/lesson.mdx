The May 2013 Google option chain — real bid/offer quotes across six strikes
and three expiries — satisfies every bound and parity relation of this module
to within the bid–ask spread, and the residual deviations measure exactly the
frictions the theory assumed away.

This is the closing move of the module's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs. <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">The first lesson</Ref> priced
a forward at $S_0 e^{rT}$ from a loan and a share;
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second</Ref> fenced a European call
into $[\max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT}),\, S_0]$, tied it to the put by
$c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, and proved that an American call on a non-dividend
stock is never exercised early; <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the
third</Ref> collapsed the fence to a point under a two-state model. Every one
of those results was proved under listed market assumptions: no transaction
costs, one rate for borrowing and lending, European exercise, no dividends.
What stands open is whether quotes posted by an actual exchange obey the
theorems, and what the failure of each assumption looks like in dollars. This
lesson tests the bounds and parity on one dated chain, runs parity backwards
to read the chain's own forward and financing rate, and closes the module
with the inventory of frictions that separate a theorem from a trade.
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The next module</Ref> returns to the model
and chains the one-period replication through a tree.

The route: how to read a chain, taught on the chain itself; the bounds
checked at every strike and expiry; the parity residual, its three sources,
and the round trip that fails to collect it; the implied forward and rate at
each expiry; and the friction inventory the module's idealisation left out.

## Reading the chain: two prices per option, one variable in the theory

The theorems of this module speak of a call price $c$ and a put price $p$.
An exchange quotes neither. Before any theorem is tested the artifact has to
be read, and the reading fixes a convention the whole lesson depends on.

### Retrieval: what the module fixed

<Task>
Closed book, before reading on. (1) Write the lower and upper bounds for a
European call and for a European put on a non-dividend stock. (2) Write
put–call parity. (3) State which of an American call and an American put on
a non-dividend stock prices as its European counterpart, and name the
inequality that decides it. Then check against the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) $\max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) \le c \le S_0$ and
$\max(0, Ke^{-rT} - S_0) \le p \le Ke^{-rT}$
(<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the corridor lesson</Ref>).
(2) $c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. (3) The call: $C = c$, because its alive floor
$S - Ke^{-r\tau}$ exceeds its exercise value $S - K$ whenever $r\tau > 0$; the
put's floor $Ke^{-r\tau} - S$ falls below its exercise value $K - S$ by the
same inequality, so $P \ge p$, with strict inequality in general.
</Aside>

The two numbers are a *bid* and an *offer*. A market maker on the exchange
posts both without knowing whether the next order will buy or sell: the bid
is what the market maker pays for the option, so a customer sells at the bid;
the offer is what the market maker charges, so a customer buys at the offer.
The offer always exceeds the bid, and the difference, the *bid–offer spread*,
is the market maker's revenue and the customer's cost of a round trip. For
the September 880 call the bid is 41.00, the offer 41.60 and the spread 0.60.
For the stock the spread is 0.14 on 871, under two hundredths of a percent;
the option spreads run from 1.5% to 6% of the option's own price, and reach
9.5% on the June 820 put.

The theory has one price where the market has two. The convention for the
rest of this lesson, and for every lab in the course that reads a chain: a
theorem is tested at the *mid*, the average of bid and offer, and a trade is
costed at the side of the quote it would actually hit, buying at the offer
and selling at the bid. The September 880 call's mid is 41.30. Mixing the two
silently is the commonest error in this kind of check, and every section
below says which price it is using.

Three further conventions come from the exchange's rulebook. An option
*contract* is on 100 shares, so a price of 41.60 per share is \$4,160 per
contract. An option's last trading day is the third Friday of its expiry
month: June 21, September 20 and December 20, 2013, which from May 8 are 44,
135 and 226 days away. And every stock option listed on the CBOE is American,
exercisable on any trading day up to expiry; Hull's own profit diagrams for
this chain treat the options as European for simplicity, and this lesson
keeps track of where the difference shows.

### A contract bought at the offer, a contract sold at the bid

<Example title="one December 880 call contract (Hull, Figure 1.3a)">
An investor buys one December 880 call contract. The trade fills at the
offer, 56.30 per share, so \$5,630 leaves the account for the right to buy
100 Google shares at \$880 each up to December 20.

If Google is at or below 880 on December 20 the right is worth nothing and
the loss is the \$5,630 paid. If Google is at 1,000, the holder buys 100
shares for \$88,000 and sells them at once for \$100,000, a gain of
\$12,000, or \$6,370 after the premium. The position breaks even at
$880 + 56.30 = 936.30$: the stock has to rise 7.5% from 871 before the call,
held to expiry, returns its cost. Between 880 and 936.30 the call is
exercised and still loses money; the exercise decision and the profit
decision are different comparisons.

The American right changes none of these numbers. By
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the no-early-exercise theorem</Ref> a call
on a non-dividend stock is worth more alive than exercised at every date
before expiry, so a holder who wants out before December sells the call
rather than exercising it, and the profit diagram drawn against $S_T$ is the
whole story.
</Example>

<Exercise>

the bid. Write the cash received, the two outcomes Hull draws (stock above
840; stock at 800), the breakeven, and the obligation the sale creates. Then
say which of the lesson's conventions the American right breaks for this
position, and why. Write the four numbers before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** A sold put is an obligation to buy 100 shares at the
strike, and the premium is the compensation for carrying it.

Cash received: $100 \times 31.00 = 3{,}100$ dollars, at the bid. If Google stays
above 840 through September 20 the put lapses and the \$3,100 is kept. If the
put is exercised with Google at 800, the writer must buy 100 shares for
\$84,000 that are worth \$80,000: a loss of \$4,000, or \$900 after the
premium. Breakeven: $840 - 31.00 = 809.00$, a fall of 7.2% from 871.

The obligation is *assignment*: when a holder exercises, the Options Clearing
Corporation, the clearing house that stands between every buyer and seller of
a listed option, assigns the exercise to a writer of the same option, who
must take the shares at the strike. For a put the American right matters.
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">The corridor lesson</Ref> showed that a
put on a low enough stock is worth more exercised than alive, so the writer
can be assigned before September 20, at the holder's choice, and the diagram
against $S_T$ describes only the case in which the holder waits. The
transferable move: an option's payoff diagram is the holder's; the writer's
is its mirror image plus the premium, and for an American put the writer
does not choose the date.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
Every quantity in this lesson exists twice: at mid, where a theorem's
equality or inequality is tested, and at bid or offer, where a trade's profit
is tested; a violation at mid that disappears at bid and offer is a fact
about the spread, not about the theorem.
</KeyFact>

## Every bound holds at mid, with dollars to spare

The corridor of <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second lesson</Ref>
was proved for a call whose price is a single number, a rate known exactly
and one expiry. The chain supplies seventeen call mids and seventeen put
mids, a rate that has to be looked up, and three clocks.

### The clock and the rate

The rate $r$ in every bound is the rate at which the arbitrageur lends or
borrows over the option's life. For May 2013 a short-term dollar rate of
about a quarter of a percent a year is the right order: three-month
interbank rates were near 0.27%, Treasury bills a few hundredths of a
percent. Take $r = 0.25\%$, continuously compounded, and the three times to
expiry $T = 44/365 = 0.121$, $135/365 = 0.370$ and $226/365 = 0.619$ years.
The discount factors are $e^{-rT} = 0.99970$, $0.99908$ and $0.99845$, so at
the 880 strike the bond paying $K$ costs $879.74$, $879.19$ and $878.64$: a
quarter, four fifths and one and a third dollars less than the strike. Every
result below that depends on $r$ is checked against what a different $r$
would do to it.

### One line in full, then all thirty-four

The June 820 call. Floor:
$S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 871.30 - 820 \times 0.99970 = 871.30 - 819.75 = 51.55$.
Mid: $(56.00 + 57.50)/2 = 56.75$. The mid sits $5.20$ above its floor and
$814.55$ below the upper bound $S_0 = 871.30$. Both inequalities hold. The
same computation at every strike and expiry gives the margin by which each
call mid clears its floor and each put mid clears its own. For the puts the
relevant floor is the American one, $\max(0, K - S_0)$, which exceeds the
European $\max(0, Ke^{-rT} - S_0)$ by the interest on the strike: an American
put quoted below its exercise value would be bought and exercised on the
spot, so the higher floor is the one a quote must clear.

The table has a regularity the theory predicts. In each row the call's
margin and the put's margin nearly coincide: 5.20 against 5.25, 24.84
against 24.55, 55.65 against 54.35. For a strike below the stock,
$c - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT})$ is by parity exactly $p$, so the call's excess over
its floor is the put's price; for a strike above the stock the same identity
makes the put's excess over $K - S_0$ equal to $c$ less the interest on the
strike. The two columns are one fact read from the two sides of parity, and
the boundary exercise at the end follows the call's margin down the strike
ladder to where it vanishes.

### The strike ladder obeys the strike theorems too

The corridor lesson's Remark stated two further inequalities from Merton's
Theorems 4 and 5: a call's price falls with the strike, and by no more than
the discounted strike difference. On the December ladder the drop per \$20
of strike is 12.30, 11.10, 10.10, 9.10 and 7.90 dollars, or 0.615, 0.555,
0.505, 0.455 and 0.395 per dollar of strike, every one inside the theorem's
$[0, e^{-rT}] = [0, 0.998]$. The June ladder is steeper, 0.83 to 0.36 per
dollar, and the theorem holds there too. The drops shrink monotonically down
the ladder, which is the convexity theorem in discrete form; the
forward-reaching exercise at the end differences the ladder a second time
and finds the object the sixth module names.

the stock and hold on the exchange with no model of the stock.
</KeyFact>

## Parity is not exact: the residual and where it comes from

From an identity proved with an equals sign in both columns of a table, one
expects $c - p - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}$ to be zero at every strike. It is not zero
at any strike, and the size and the sign of the residual are the lesson's
second result.

### One line in full: December 880

$c = 55.65$ and $p = 63.05$, so $c - p = -7.40$.
$Ke^{-rT} = 880 \times 0.99845 = 878.64$, so
$S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 871.30 - 878.64 = -7.34$. The residual
$$
\varepsilon \;=\; c - p - S_0 + Ke^{-rT} \;=\; -7.40 + 7.34 \;=\; -0.06
$$
is six cents on a stock at 871. Across the June and December expiries the
residuals run from $-0.01$ to $-0.52$, every one negative; the September
expiry, left to the first exercise, has four positive residuals, one of
them a single cent at the 860 strike, the largest $+0.50$.

### Three sources, measured

The residual has three candidate sources, each a hypothesis of the parity
theorem that the chain does not satisfy, and each can be measured or
excluded.

**Dividends.** Parity with a dividend of present value $D$ reads
$c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}$, so an unaccounted dividend produces a residual
of exactly $-D$. Google paid no dividend in 2013 and had never paid one, so
$D = 0$: this source is removed by fact rather than by estimate.

**American exercise.** The theorem is for European options; every quote is
American. For the calls this costs nothing, since $C = c$ on a non-dividend
stock. For the puts it does: $P \ge p$, so the quoted difference satisfies
$C - P \le c - p$, and the residual computed from American quotes is
$\varepsilon = -(P - p) \le 0$. The premium is bounded. No exact American
parity exists, but the inequalities
$$
S_0 - K \;\le\; C - P \;\le\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT}
$$
hold, Hull's equation (11.7), which the derivation exercise at the end
proves, and they confine the residual to $[-K(1 - e^{-rT}),\, 0]$: at the
880 strike $[-0.27, 0]$ for June, $[-0.81, 0]$ for September and
$[-1.36, 0]$ for December. Every June and December residual is negative and
inside its band, the sign and the size an exercise premium predicts. The
four positive September residuals, $+0.29$, $+0.01$, $+0.27$ and $+0.50$ at
the 820, 860, 900 and 920 strikes, lie outside the band on the side no
exercise premium can reach, and need the third source; the penny at 860 is
inside any rounding of the mids, the other three are not.

**The spread.** Each residual is a difference of three mids, and the trade
that would collect it crosses three spreads. The cost of doing so, the
half-spread of the call plus the half-spread of the put plus the half-spread
of the stock, is 0.72 to 1.07 per share on the June lines, 0.87 to 1.42 on
September and 1.37 to 1.87 on December. Every residual on the chain is
smaller in magnitude than the crossing cost of its own line; the largest,
$-0.52$ at December 920, faces a crossing cost of 1.77.

A fourth quantity is not a friction but an unknown. The residual depends on
$r$ through $Ke^{-rT}$, at the rate
$\partial\varepsilon/\partial r = -KTe^{-rT}$: a quarter of a percent of rate
moves the December 880 residual by $1.36$, more than twenty times the
residual itself. At 2013 rates the assumed $r$ is as large a term in the
residual as anything the chain says, which is the reason the next section
runs the equation the other way.

### The refutation: a failed hypothesis is not a failed market

The correct claim first: on this chain the parity relation holds to within
the bid–offer spread, and the residual has the sign of the one hypothesis of
the theorem that the quotes violate. The common reading of a nonzero
residual is the opposite: "put–call parity is violated by 52 cents on the
December 920 line, so the market is mispricing and the difference can be
collected." That reading is wrong, for a reason the theorem itself states.
Parity's proof compared two portfolios held to expiry; an American put
cannot be held to expiry against its holder's will, so the theorem's
hypothesis fails for the put and the equality is replaced by the inequality
above, which the December 920 residual satisfies: $-0.52$ lies inside
$[-1.42, 0]$. What survives is not a mispricing but a premium, the price of
the put holder's right to exercise early, and the residual measures it. The
discriminating test is the round trip at quoted prices: if the residual were
collectible, selling the dear side and buying the cheap side at bid and
offer would return money in both states, and on the December 920 line that
trade loses 1.25 per share in one direction and 2.30 in the other. The
second exercise at the end returns to this claim.

frictionless trade buys the call, sells the put, shorts the share and lends
the proceeds. Executed leg by leg at the quotes the market actually posts,

Step through the four frames. On frame 3, confirm the cash lent:

The trade loses 0.76 in both states. The difference between $+0.11$ at mid
and $-0.76$ at the quotes is $0.87$, the three half-spreads, and the
arithmetic is the same on every line of the chain: the mid residual is the
frictionless profit, the crossing cost is what the market charges to attempt
it, and on May 8, 2013 the second exceeded the first everywhere. A dealer who
quotes both sides of every option has left no line where a customer, paying
the spread, beats parity.

<Remark title="an executable violation">
For American quotes a parity residual is collectible only if one of the two
inequalities $S_0 - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ fails at the sides of the
quotes a trade would hit. Left side: if
$S_0^{\text{bid}} - c^{\text{offer}} + p^{\text{bid}} > K$, then buying the
call, selling the put, shorting the share and holding $K$ in cash pays the
strike on any assignment, early or at expiry, and banks the excess now; no
rate enters. Right side: if
$K > (S_0^{\text{offer}} + p^{\text{offer}} - c^{\text{bid}})\,e^{rT}$, then
selling the call, buying the put and the share on borrowed money repays the
loan with the strike, received at expiry by exercising the put or on
assignment of the call, and early assignment of a call on a non-dividend
stock only hastens the receipt. A residual outside the American band but
inside the crossing cost fails both tests; on this chain every line fails
both by at least 0.7 per share.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The largest parity residual on the chain, 52 cents at mid, is smaller than
the narrowest round-trip crossing cost, 72 cents: on May 8, 2013 no parity
trade on Google returned money after the spread, and the residuals that
remain are the price of a right the theorem assumed away.
</KeyFact>

## Parity run backwards: the chain's own forward and financing rate

The residual section ended on an unknown: at these rates the assumed $r$
moves the December residual by more than the residual itself, so
$\varepsilon = 0$ is not a test parity can pass or fail without a rate in
hand. Rearranged, the same identity contains no assumed rate at all.

### The implied forward, strike by strike

A long call and a short put at one strike pay $S_T - K$ together, the payoff
of a forward with delivery price $K$; the parity exercise of
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the corridor lesson</Ref> made this
observation. The forward with delivery price $F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$ costs
nothing; the one with delivery price $K$ costs $c - p$; the difference
between the two contracts is a bond paying $F_0 - K$ at $T$. So
$c - p = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}$, which with $F_0 = S_0e^{rT}$ is parity again.
Solved for the two unknowns of the chain, the discount factor and the
forward,
$$
e^{-rT} = \frac{S_0 - c + p}{K}, \qquad
F_0 = S_0\,e^{rT} = \frac{S_0\,K}{S_0 - c + p}.
$$
Nothing on the right is assumed: the stock's mid, the two option mids, the
strike. Each line of the chain returns its own estimate of the forward and
of the rate, and at these rates $F_0$ is $K + c - p$ to within a dime.

### What one dollar of residual is worth in rate

The scatter across strikes within one expiry is the spread's, and it
converts to rate at a fixed exchange rate:
$\partial r/\partial(c - p) \approx 1/(KT)$, which is 0.94 percentage points
per dollar for June, 0.31 for September and 0.18 for December. A residual of
15 cents, ordinary on the June lines, is 0.14 points of June rate and 0.03
points of December rate. The longest expiry therefore pins the financing
rate best, to about a tenth of a point, and the shortest hardly at all: the
same annualisation effect that the currency-forward exercise of
<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the first lesson</Ref> found in the
one-month against the six-month sterling forward.

The American premium enters with a known sign. $P \ge p$ makes
$S_0 - C + P \ge S_0 - c + p$, so the implied discount factor is biased
upward and the implied forward and rate biased downward, by at most the
interest on the strike. December's implied forward is 0.27 below the carry
line, the right sign and a fifth of the allowance 1.36; June's is 0.10
below, inside its allowance 0.27; September's is 0.14 above, a sign no
exercise premium produces and a size inside the crossing cost.

### Agreement with the world

An implied financing rate of 0.16% to 0.29% is the right sign and size:
three-month dollar interbank rates on May 8, 2013 were near 0.27%, and the
rate at which a dealer finances a share position sits near that benchmark
rather than at the Treasury bill's few hundredths of a percent. The chain
agrees with the money market to within the chain's own resolution, and does
so at three expiries at once: the market's forward curve for Google, 871.46,
872.25, 872.38, rises with expiry at the pace $S_0 r$ predicts, about a
dollar over seven months.

<Aside>
The lab version. With many strikes per expiry the per-strike estimates are
combined by regressing $c - p$ on $K$: parity says the line has intercept
$S_0$ and slope $-e^{-rT}$, and its zero crossing is the forward. On six
strikes twenty dollars apart at 2013 rates the slope is useless for the
rate: it comes out $-1.0013$ for June where $-0.9997$ is the truth, because a
spread-sized error of 0.3 over a 100-dollar span is a 0.3% error in the
slope, which is a full percentage point of June rate. The zero crossing is
pinned to a quarter dollar all the same, 871.45, 872.26 and 872.38, the
per-strike means to the cent. <Ref lesson="vol-surface-lab">The
volatility-surface lab</Ref> runs this regression on an S&P 500 chain with
dozens of strikes, where the slope becomes usable, as its
`forward-from-parity` checkpoint.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The chain contains its own forward, $F_0 = S_0K/(S_0 - c + p)$ line by line
with no rate assumed, and on May 8, 2013 it placed Google's December forward
at 872.38 against a spot of 871.30, a carry of 0.20% a year that matches the
money market; every implied-volatility surface in the seventh module begins
by reading this number off the chain.
</KeyFact>

## The friction inventory: where the idealisation departs

The module's theorems were proved under assumptions the market does not
meet, and the point of the chain is to price each departure rather than to
list it.

| assumed | on the chain | size, dollars per share |
|---|---|---|
| one price per instrument, no cost to trade | bid below offer on every instrument | stock 0.14; options 0.50 to 2.30; round-trip crossing cost 0.72 to 1.87, above every parity residual |
| one rate, borrowing and lending alike | a financing rate to be inferred line by line | implied 0.09% to 0.40%; a tenth of a point of December rate is 0.55 in $Ke^{-rT}$ at 880 |
| European exercise | every quote American | calls: zero, by theorem; puts: at most $K(1 - e^{-rT})$, 0.25 to 1.42 at 2013 rates |
| a strike and an expiry for every payoff | six strikes \$20 apart, three expiries, one cell empty | the ladder resolves the payoff to \$20; the June 920 line has no quote |
| no dividends | none paid | 0 |
| any quantity, divisible | contracts of 100 shares; commissions not counted here | a one-contract hedge is 100 shares; commissions add to every crossing cost |

Which results the artifact used: Merton's Theorem 1 and the put bounds for
the thirty-four margins; Theorems 4 and 5 for the ladder; Theorem 12 for the
residual, and Theorem 13 in the form of the American band for its sign;
Theorem 2 for the calls' freedom from an exercise premium; the forward-price
theorem of the first lesson, run through parity, for the implied forward;
and the one-period model of the third lesson in the review exercise.

One number governs how clean the test could be. Three of the theoretical
gaps on the chain, the discount on the strike, the American put's allowance
and the width of the band that an assumed rate leaves the residual in, are
proportional to $r$, and in May 2013 $r$ was a quarter of a percent. At a
five percent rate the December 880 strike would be discounted by 26.83
rather than 1.36, and the American-put allowance would be 26.83 in place of
1.36: a chain quoted at 2013 rates is a near-frictionless test of European
theory on American quotes because the interest that separates the two was
itself nearly zero. <Ref lesson="early-exercise-in-practice">The
early-exercise lesson of the third module</Ref> prices the put's premium in
a regime where it is not.

What is established: on a dated exchange chain every dominance bound of the
module holds at mid with a margin of at least five dollars, and the strike
theorems hold along the ladder; put–call parity holds to within a residual
that never exceeds 52 cents, whose sign is the American put's and whose size
is inside the spread on every line, so no parity trade is collectible; and
parity inverted reads the chain's own forward and financing rate, 0.2% a
year to within a tenth of a point at the longest expiry, in agreement with
the money market. What is not established is a price for any of the
thirty-four options: the module's fences and identities, confirmed by the
market, are fences and identities still.

model, which priced one call at one strike, and chains it through a tree
with enough states to price a ladder.

<Exercise>
Compute the parity residual $c - p - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}$ at mid for all six
September strikes, at $r = 0.25\%$ and $T = 135/365$. Identify the strike
where the residual is largest in magnitude, state which side of parity is
dear there, and test both directions of the round trip at bid and offer.
Then state, in terms of the quotes, what would have counted as a genuine
violation.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Compute $Ke^{-rT}$ once per strike with
$e^{-rT} = 0.99908$; the residual is $(c - p) - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT})$.

$Ke^{-rT}$: 819.24, 839.22, 859.21, 879.19, 899.17, 919.15, so
$S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ is 52.06, 32.08, 12.09, $-7.89$, $-27.87$, $-47.85$. The
mid differences $c - p$ are 52.35, 32.00, 12.10, $-8.00$, $-27.60$, $-47.35$.
Residuals:

| strike | 820 | 840 | 860 | 880 | 900 | 920 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\varepsilon$ | $+0.29$ | $-0.08$ | $+0.01$ | $-0.11$ | $+0.27$ | $+0.50$ |

The largest is $+0.50$ at 920: the call side is dear, or equivalently the
put side is cheap, by fifty cents at mid. A positive residual is outside the
American band $[-0.85, 0]$ on the side no exercise premium can reach, so
only the spread can explain it, and the crossing cost of the 920 line is
$0.40 + 0.95 + 0.07 = 1.42$.

**The trade the residual favours:** sell the call at the bid 24.80, buy the
put at the offer 73.50, buy the share at the offer 871.37. Outlay
$871.37 + 73.50 - 24.80 = 920.07$, borrowed for 135 days to repay
$920.07 \times 1.000925 = 920.92$. At expiry the share is sold for 920 in
either state, by exercising the put or by assignment on the call. Net:
$920 - 920.92 = -0.92$.

**The other direction:** buy the call at 25.60, sell the put at 71.60, short
the share at 871.23. Cash in $917.23$, lent to become $918.08$; pay 920 for a
share at expiry. Net $-1.92$.

**A genuine violation.** Either $S_0^{\text{bid}} - c^{\text{offer}} +
p^{\text{bid}} > 920$, so that the cash from the first direction covers the
strike outright (here it is 917.23, short by 2.77), or
$(S_0^{\text{offer}} + p^{\text{offer}} - c^{\text{bid}})\,e^{rT} < 920$, so
that the second direction's loan is repaid by the strike with money to spare
(here 920.92 against 920, short by 0.92). The transferable move: a residual
is tested by the trade that would collect it, at the quotes it would hit,
and with the American right on the side that cannot be hurt by it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "The December 920 line has $c - p = -47.80$ where parity
requires $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = -47.28$, a violation of 52 cents; the market is
leaving money on the table." Name the hypothesis of the parity theorem that
fails, show that the residual has the sign and a size that the failed
hypothesis predicts, and exhibit the round trip at quoted prices in the
direction the residual favours.

<Solution>
**The hypothesis.** Parity was proved for European options, whose payoffs
are fixed at expiry and nowhere else. The December 920 put is American.
Its holder may exercise before December 20, and
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the corridor lesson</Ref> showed that a
put on a low enough stock is worth more exercised than alive, so $P > p$ in
general. The call is unaffected, $C = c$, because the same lesson proved that
a call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early.

**Sign and size.** With $C = c$ and $P \ge p$,
$C - P - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) = -(P - p) \le 0$: the residual must be negative,
and it is, $-0.52$. Its magnitude is bounded by the American band,
$S_0 - K \le C - P$, that is $\varepsilon \ge -K(1 - e^{-rT}) =
-920 \times 0.001547 = -1.42$. The observed $-0.52$ is inside. The residual
is not a violation of parity; it is a measurement of the early-exercise
premium on the December 920 put, between zero and its maximum.

**The round trip.** A negative residual says the call side is cheap: buy the
call at the offer 39.40, sell the put at the bid 85.50, short the share at
the bid 871.23. Cash in $871.23 - 39.40 + 85.50 = 917.33$, lent for 226 days
to become $917.33 \times 1.001549 = 918.75$; at expiry pay 920 for the share
in either state. Net $-1.25$. Holding the 920 in cash against early
assignment, as the executable-violation Remark requires, is worse still,
since 917.33 is short of 920 before any interest. The reverse direction
loses 2.30. The step that fails in the claim is "parity requires": for an
American put, parity requires an inequality, and the inequality holds.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The June 820 call is the quote closest to its lower bound on the chain.
(a) Compute its margin over the no-arbitrage floor and over Hull's intrinsic
value $S_0 - K$, and account for the difference between the two margins to
the cent. (b) Show from parity that the margin over the floor equals the
June 820 put's price plus the parity residual, and check the numbers.
(c) Let the strike fall toward zero with everything else fixed. State what
happens to the floor, to the upper bound, to the width of the corridor and
to the put, and say why the call's time value must vanish there.

<Solution>
**(a)** Mid $56.75$. Floor $S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 871.30 - 819.75 = 51.55$,
margin $5.20$. Intrinsic value $S_0 - K = 51.30$, margin $5.45$. The
difference, $0.25$, is $K(1 - e^{-rT}) = 820 \times 0.000301 = 0.247$: the
interest on the strike over 44 days, which the no-arbitrage floor counts as
part of the call's floor and Hull's intrinsic value counts as time value.

**(b)** Parity with the residual: $c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT} + \varepsilon$, so
$c - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) = p + \varepsilon$. On the June 820 line
$p = 5.25$ and $\varepsilon = -0.05$, and $5.25 - 0.05 = 5.20$, the margin.
A call's excess over its floor is the price of the put at the same strike,
up to the residual.

**(c)** As $K \to 0$ the floor $S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ rises to $S_0$, the upper
bound is $S_0$ throughout, and the corridor's width $Ke^{-rT}$ shrinks to
zero: both bounds become tight together and meet at the share's price. The
put at strike $K$ pays at most $K$ and so is worth at most $Ke^{-rT}$, which
also goes to zero. By (b) the call's time value is that put, so it vanishes
with it. The reason is the one the corridor lesson gave for the fence's
width: a call is a share plus the right to walk away from paying $K$, and
the right is worth nothing once $K$ is nothing to pay. On the chain the
margin already falls from 15.30 at the 880 strike to 5.20 at 820 as the put
falls from 23.90 to 5.25. The transferable move: a deep-in-the-money call is
a share with a small put attached, and the bounds say so before any model
does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Fit a one-period model to the June expiry. Suppose that on June 21 Google
takes one of two values, $uS_0$ above 900 and $dS_0$ below 880, with
$1 + r = e^{0.0025 \times 44/365} = 1.00030$ over the period. Use the June 880
and 900 call mids, 15.30 and 8.15, to determine $uS_0$, then $\tilde p$,
then $d$. Check admissibility, then price the June 880 put, the June 900 put
and the June 840 put in the fitted model and compare each with its quote.
Decide which kind of problem this is before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** In a two-state world where both strikes lie below
$uS_0$ and above $dS_0$, both calls pay only in the up state, so their ratio
determines $uS_0$ with no probability involved.

**The up state.** By the one-period pricing formula of
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the binomial lesson</Ref>,
$c_{880} = \tilde p\,(uS_0 - 880)/1.0003$ and
$c_{900} = \tilde p\,(uS_0 - 900)/1.0003$, so
$$
\frac{15.30}{8.15} = \frac{uS_0 - 880}{uS_0 - 900}
\;\Rightarrow\; 7.15\,uS_0 = 15.30 \times 900 - 8.15 \times 880 = 6598
\;\Rightarrow\; uS_0 = 922.80,
$$
and $u = 922.80/871.30 = 1.0591$.

**The multiplier.** $\tilde p = 15.30 \times 1.0003/42.80 = 0.358$, so
$\tilde q = 0.642$.

**The down state.** $\tilde p = (1 + r - d)/(u - d)$ rearranges to
$d = (1 + r - \tilde p\,u)/(1 - \tilde p) = (1.0003 - 0.3788)/0.642 = 0.9676$,
so $dS_0 = 843.04$. Admissibility: $0.9676 < 1.0003 < 1.0591$, and both
strikes do lie between 843 and 923, so the setup was consistent.

**Puts in the model.** Each pays only in the down state.
$p_{880} = 0.642 \times (880 - 843.04)/1.0003 = 23.73$ against a quote of
23.90; $p_{900} = 0.642 \times 56.96/1.0003 = 36.58$ against 36.75. Both
within a spread half-width. $p_{840}$: the down state 843.04 is above 840,
so the put pays nothing in either state and the model prices it at $0$,
against a quote of 8.65.

**What kind of problem.** A model-fitting problem with a spanning check
hidden in it. The two-state model has two free parameters and reproduced
the two prices it was fitted to; parity then carried the fit to the two
puts at the same strikes, which is why they came out right. The 840 put is
a different matter: the market pays 8.65 for a claim that pays only if
Google closes below 840, so the market allows states the fitted model does
not contain. A two-state model prices a ladder of thirty-four quotes only
if the world has two states, and the chain says it has more. The
transferable move: count the parameters against the prices, and read a
zero price for a quoted option as a missing state, not a mispricing.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the American band used in the residual section: for American options
on a non-dividend stock with the same strike and expiry,
$$
S_0 - K \;\le\; C - P \;\le\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT}.
$$
For the right inequality use results already proved; for the left,
construct the arbitrage that a violation admits and show it survives early
exercise by the counterparty. Then evaluate both sides on the December 880
line.

<Solution>
**Right inequality.** $C = c$ by the no-early-exercise theorem for calls on
a non-dividend stock, and $P \ge p$ since an American put carries every
right a European one does. So
$C - P \le c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ by European parity.

**Left inequality.** Suppose $C - P < S_0 - K$, that is
$P + S_0 > C + K$. Sell the put for $P$, short the share for $S_0$, buy the
call for $C$, and hold $K$ in cash, lent at $r$. Cash now:
$P + S_0 - C - K > 0$, banked. Two cases.

*The put is exercised against the position at some time $t \le T$.* Pay $K$
for a share, funded from the cash, which has grown to $Ke^{rt} \ge K$; return
the share to close the short. What remains is the call, worth at least
zero, plus $K(e^{rt} - 1) \ge 0$. No loss.

*The put is never exercised.* At $T$, if $S_T > K$ exercise the call, pay
$K$ from the cash, close the short with the share, keep $K(e^{rT} - 1)$. If
$S_T \le K$ the put is exercised at expiry, which is the first case at
$t = T$. No loss either way.

The position cost nothing, banked a positive amount at the outset and never
loses: an arbitrage. Hence $C - P \ge S_0 - K$. The step that makes the
argument American-proof is holding $K$ in cash rather than $Ke^{-rT}$ in a
bond, so that the strike is available whenever the put's holder chooses to
demand it; the price of that readiness is the interest $K(1 - e^{-rT})$,
which is exactly the width of the band.

**December 880.** $S_0 - K = -8.70$, $C - P = 55.65 - 63.05 = -7.40$,
$S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = -7.34$. So $-8.70 \le -7.40 \le -7.34$: inside, six cents
from the European edge and 1.30 from the American one. The transferable
move: when a hypothesis of a theorem fails, the repair is usually an
inequality whose width is the price of the failed hypothesis.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Tabulate the December call mids against strike, 89.15, 76.85, 65.75, 55.65,
46.55, 38.65, and compute the second difference
$$
\frac{c(K + h) - 2c(K) + c(K - h)}{h^2}, \qquad h = 20,
$$
at the four interior strikes 840, 860, 880 and 900. Note the sign of all
four. Then multiply each by $e^{rT}$ and by $h$, and say what the four
numbers add up to and how they compare with one.

<Solution>
Second differences of the mids: at 840,
$89.15 - 2 \times 76.85 + 65.75 = 1.20$; at 860, $1.00$; at 880, $1.00$; at
900, $1.20$. Divided by $h^2 = 400$: $0.0030$, $0.0025$, $0.0025$ and
$0.0030$ per dollar of strike, all positive.

Positivity is the convexity theorem of the corridor lesson in discrete form:
the portfolio long one $(K - h)$-call, short two $K$-calls and long one
$(K + h)$-call, a *butterfly*, pays $\max(0, h - |S_T - K|) \ge 0$ at expiry,
so by the dominance lemma it cannot cost less than zero, and its cost is the
numerator. A negative second difference anywhere on a ladder would be an
arbitrage in three options.

Multiplied by $e^{rT} = 1.0015$ and by $h = 20$: $0.060$, $0.050$, $0.050$
and $0.060$. Each is the discounted-and-rescaled price of a butterfly that
pays about one dollar when Google expires within ten dollars of the centre
strike and nothing otherwise, which is what a bet on the interval
$[K - 10, K + 10]$ costs; the four sum to $0.22$, well under one, because
they cover only the eighty dollars from 830 to 910 and the market places
most of its weight outside it. Read as a density, $e^{rT}\,\partial^2 c /
\partial K^2$ at each strike is the probability per dollar that the
risk-neutral measure assigns to Google expiring there:
<Ref lesson="implied-density">the market-implied distribution</Ref> proves
this identity and integrates the whole curve. The chain has just been read
as a probability distribution, on a twenty-dollar grid, with no model of the
stock and no rate but the one the chain implied.
</Solution>

The module's theorems are claims about limits, and limits can be watched:
sampled quadratic variation pins to $T$ while a smooth path's dies, the
left-endpoint sum of $\int W\,dW$ produces the $-T/2$ correction out of plain
arithmetic, and the Euler scheme's error falls at exactly the order the
calculus predicts.

Everything the lab measures was proved in the four lessons before it. The
scaled random walk with its quadratic variation equal to elapsed time at
every $n$ is <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion" />'s; the theorem
$[W, W](T) = T$, its mean-square proof and the multiplication table
$dW\,dW = dt$ are <Ref lesson="brownian-motion" />'s; the left-endpoint
construction of the Itô integral, with $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 -
\tfrac12 T$ as its first computed value, is <Ref lesson="ito-integral" />'s;
the Itô–Doeblin formula and the closed-form solution of geometric Brownian
motion are <Ref lesson="ito-formula" />'s. Module 4 is the hardest
mathematics in the course, and until now the only module after the binomial
one in which nothing was built.

What the lab contributes is the module's claims as measurements, with the
noise reported. The bench is Higham's SIAM Review assignment — ten MATLAB
programs an undergraduate reproduces — rebuilt in vectorized NumPy with
quadratic variation added as the first instrument: a path constructor, a
quadratic-variation meter that reads any discretely observed path at any
mesh, a stochastic-integral quadrature with a choice of evaluation point, and
an Euler–Maruyama solver. Three studies run on it. The quadratic-variation
table puts four processes on one set of Brownian increments and reads them
at every mesh from four cells to sixteen thousand: $W$ settles at $T$, a
sine dies by the refinement factor per row, a drift of $1.5$ per unit time
leaves no trace, and the log of a geometric Brownian motion settles at
$\sigma^2 T$ — the realized-variance identity the last task uses. The
integral study computes $\int W\,dW$ on one path with the integrand read at
the left endpoint, the midpoint and the right endpoint, and the three sums
converge to three different numbers whose gaps are the sampled quadratic
variation of the cells. The solver study measures Euler–Maruyama's strong
order on geometric Brownian motion the way Higham's `emstrong` does — every
step size consuming block sums of one shared fine path, so the discretization
is the only thing that varies — and the fitted slope lands near $\tfrac12$;
the same study with fresh paths per step size is run beside it, and its slope
is noise. The Itô–Doeblin formula is checked along four hundred paths as a
convergence claim rather than Higham's single $0.0151$, and the discrepancy
falls at the same order $\tfrac12$, for the same reason.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above,
or in any Jupyter you like. Pair the notebook with the code from this page;
each checkpoint submits your function's outputs for verification and the
checklist above tracks what stands. The seven exact checkpoints are the
required set — the walk, the meter, the four-process table, the quadrature,
the Itô–Doeblin sums, the solver and the strong-convergence study; the
written answer and the open estimation task are optional, and partial
completion is a normal way to finish a lab. Expect about four hours of real
work. The definitions are printed above the blanks they motivate — the
sampled sum, the Itô–Doeblin formula, the Euler step; the work is the grid
discipline: reading a fine path at a stride, keeping every resolution on one
set of increments, and vectorizing over a batch of paths so that the studies
report distributions rather than single runs. If a check goes red, the
verdict often names the exact bug — the increments scaled by $\Delta t$
instead of $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, the sum divided by the number of cells, the
right endpoint offered as the left — so read it before rereading your code.
The closing task hands the meter real work: 160 discretely observed price
paths with their own grids and horizons, recorded by a feed that adds an
independent error to every observation, and a volatility to estimate for
each. The identity applied as it stands reads the noise as volatility and
misses the threshold by a factor of two; an estimator that models the feed —
its level read off the covariance of consecutive returns, pooled across the
paths, its bias subtracted or averaged down by a coarser stride — passes
with room, and a calibration bench with the truth known says which route
is better before the holdout sees either.

The bench returns twice. <Ref lesson="discrete-hedging-lab" /> takes the
coupled-refinement discipline and the seeded-study habit into the hedge,
where the order $\tfrac12$ measured here becomes the $1/\sqrt n$ law of the
hedging error; <Ref lesson="realized-implied-smile" /> takes the closing
task's estimator to real prices, where the feed's noise is neither constant
nor independent of the path, the sampled quadratic variation stops
converging as the sampling rate rises, and the theorem's hypotheses, not its
arithmetic, become the question.

Shrinking the binomial period demands a rescaling — steps of size
$1/\sqrt{n}$, $n$ steps per unit time — and under it two things survive the
limit: the central limit theorem hands increments to the normal
distribution, and the walk's quadratic variation stays exactly $t$, path by
path.

This is the fifteenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the first move of its fourth module. The first three modules
built the binomial machine and ran it: <Ref
lesson="multiperiod-binomial">backward induction</Ref> prices every claim on
a tree, <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the risk-neutral
measure</Ref> makes every discounted price a martingale, and <Ref
lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">the pricer lab</Ref> ran that machine at
thousands of steps with up factor $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, a
formula it shipped without deriving. What stands open is what the tree
becomes as the period shrinks and the step count grows, and what the
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$ in the calibration is for. This lesson answers both: the
step size $1/\sqrt n$ is the only one under which the walk's variance at
time $t$ stays finite and nonzero; under it the walk keeps independent
increments, the martingale property, and a *quadratic variation* equal to
$t$ on every single path, a quantity that looks like variance and is not;
the central limit theorem sends the walk's value at any fixed time to the
normal distribution, with a histogram computed bar by bar at $n = 100$
and each bar within one percent of the density; and the same theorem sends the calibrated stock to a lognormal
whose exponent carries a $-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ that nobody put there. <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">The next lesson</Ref> defines the limit object
outright and proves its roughness.

The route: the exponent $\tfrac12$ forced by a variance count; the three
properties of the walk, proved; quadratic variation against variance, with
the asymmetric walk as the discriminating case; $W^{(100)}(0.25)$ by
binomial count against the normal density; and the calibrated binomial
stock's lognormal limit, with the risk-neutral coin tilting by exactly
$1/\sqrt n$.

## The step size is forced: variance $t$ at time $t$ needs steps of $1/\sqrt n$

The tree the lab ran at $n = 40{,}000$ steps is a sequence of models, one
for each $n$, and a sequence has a limit only if its members are scaled to
fit on one axis. The first thing to settle is the scale, and the variance
of the walk settles it without any freedom.

### The symmetric walk on eight tosses

Toss a fair coin and record $X_j = +1$ for a head, $-1$ for a tail. The
*symmetric random walk* is the running sum $M_0 = 0$,
$M_k = X_1 + \cdots + X_k$. On the toss sequence $HHTHTTTH$ the steps are
$+1, +1, -1, +1, -1, -1, -1, +1$ and the walk visits
$1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 0, -1, 0$: eight steps, each of size exactly one, ending
where it began. The walk is the stock tree of <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">the binomial model</Ref> with the prices
stripped off: a head is an up move, a tail a down move, and $M_k$ counts
heads minus tails.

The walk's spread grows with $k$. Its variance is
$\mathrm{Var}(M_k) = \sum_{j=1}^k \mathrm{Var}(X_j) = k$, since each
$X_j$ has mean zero and $X_j^2 = 1$, and the $X_j$ are independent. After
eight tosses the standard deviation is $\sqrt 8 \approx 2.8$; after
$10{,}000$ it is $100$. A walk run for a unit of time at $n$ steps per
unit, with the step size held at one, has variance $n$ at time one and no
limit.

### Three exponents, one survivor

Make the steps smaller as the count grows: steps of size $n^{-\alpha}$,
$n$ per unit time, so that the position at time $t$ is
$n^{-\alpha} M_{nt}$. Its variance is a one-line count,
$$
\mathrm{Var}\bigl(n^{-\alpha} M_{nt}\bigr) = n^{-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{Var}(M_{nt}) = n^{-2\alpha} \cdot nt = t\,n^{1 - 2\alpha}.
$$
The natural first choice, steps of size $1/n$ to match the period $1/n$,
is $\alpha = 1$: the variance at time $t$ is $t/n$, which goes to zero.
The limit exists and is the constant zero, a process with no randomness
in it. Steps shrinking more slowly, $\alpha = \tfrac14$, give variance
$t\sqrt n$, which goes to infinity. Only $\alpha = \tfrac12$ gives a
variance that is finite, nonzero, and the same for every $n$: exactly $t$.

  caption="Steps of size n^(−α) with n steps per unit time: the variance at t = 1 is n^(1−2α), and only α = ½ keeps it finite and nonzero."

The exponent is not a modelling choice. Any scaling that keeps the walk's
spread at time $t$ finite and nonzero must shrink steps like
$1/\sqrt n$, because variance adds across independent steps while the step
size enters squared: $n$ steps of size $s$ have variance $ns^2$, and
$ns^2 = t$ at $n$ steps per unit time forces $s = 1/\sqrt n$. The same
square root is the one the lab's calibration $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$
carries, with $\Delta t = 1/n$; the last section of this lesson derives it
from this count.

<Definition title="scaled symmetric random walk (Shreve II, 3.2.5)">
Fix a positive integer $n$. The **scaled symmetric random walk** is
$$
W^{(n)}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\,M_{nt} \qquad \text{whenever } nt \text{ is an integer},
$$
and, for $t$ between two such points, the linear interpolation between the
values at the nearest points on either side. It takes $n$ steps per unit
of time, each of size $1/\sqrt n$.
</Definition>

The interpolation only makes $W^{(n)}$ a function of every $t$; every
statement below is made at times where $nt$ is an integer, and the
interpolation is never used in a proof.

## Three properties of the walk, and the one measured along a single path

The scaling keeps the spread in place. What it does to the walk's
structure is the next question, and the answer is that the three
properties the walk has at step size one survive scaling unchanged. Two of
them are already familiar from the tree; the third is new, and it is the
property the entire module rests on.

### Independent increments, with variance equal to elapsed time

The change of the walk between two integer times is an *increment*,
$M_\ell - M_k = X_{k+1} + \cdots + X_\ell$ for $k < \ell$. It reads only
the tosses $k + 1$ through $\ell$.

<Theorem title="independent increments (Shreve II, 3.2.2)">
For integers $0 = k_0 < k_1 < \cdots < k_m$, the increments
$M_{k_1} - M_{k_0},\; M_{k_2} - M_{k_1},\; \ldots,\; M_{k_m} - M_{k_{m-1}}$
are independent random variables, each with mean zero and with
$\mathrm{Var}(M_{k_{i+1}} - M_{k_i}) = k_{i+1} - k_i$.

<Proof>
The $i$th increment is a function of the tosses $k_i + 1, \ldots, k_{i+1}$
alone, and the blocks of tosses are disjoint for different $i$. The
tosses are independent, because <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the
coin-toss measure</Ref> assigns every sequence the product
$p^{\#H} q^{\#T}$, so functions of disjoint blocks of tosses are
independent. The mean is $\sum E[X_j] = 0$ over the block. For the
variance, the $X_j$ in the block are independent with
$\mathrm{Var}(X_j) = E[X_j^2] = 1$, so the variances add to the number of
terms, $k_{i+1} - k_i$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Variance accumulates at rate one per unit time: the increment over any
stretch of $\ell - k$ steps has variance $\ell - k$, whatever happened
before $k$.

### The martingale property

The walk is a martingale under the fair coin, and the proof is the one
that made the discounted stock a martingale under $\tilde p$, with the
discount factor gone.

<Theorem title="the symmetric walk is a martingale (Shreve II, 3.2.3)">
For integers $k < \ell$, $E_k[M_\ell] = M_k$, where $E_k$ is conditional
expectation given the first $k$ tosses.

<Proof>
Write $M_\ell = (M_\ell - M_k) + M_k$. By linearity,
$E_k[M_\ell] = E_k[M_\ell - M_k] + E_k[M_k]$. The second term is $M_k$,
because $M_k$ depends only on the first $k$ tosses and is taken out as
known. The first term is $E[M_\ell - M_k] = 0$, because the increment
depends only on tosses after $k$, so the independence property replaces
the conditional expectation by the plain one, and the mean of an
increment is zero by the previous theorem.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof used exactly two facts, "the increment is independent of the
past" and "the increment has mean zero", and the discounted stock's
proof in <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale
lesson</Ref> used the same two with $\tilde p$ supplying the zero mean.
Under an unfair coin, $p \ne \tfrac12$, the increment's mean is
$(\ell - k)(2p - 1) \ne 0$ and the walk is a sub- or supermartingale,
never a martingale.

### Quadratic variation: a sum along one path

The third property is computed differently from the first two. Take the
eight-toss path $HHTHTTTH$ again, list its eight one-step increments
$+1, +1, -1, +1, -1, -1, -1, +1$, square each, and add: eight ones, total
$8$. Do the same on the path $HHHHHHHH$: eight ones, total $8$. On
$TTTTTTTT$, or on any of the $256$ paths of eight tosses, the total is
$8$, because $(\pm 1)^2 = 1$ regardless of the sign.

<Definition title="quadratic variation of the walk (Shreve II, 3.2.4)">
The **quadratic variation** of the symmetric random walk up to time $k$ is
the sum of its squared one-step increments along the path,
$$
[M, M]_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} (M_j - M_{j-1})^2 .
$$
Since every one-step increment is $\pm 1$, $[M, M]_k = k$ on every path.
</Definition>

Four cases fix what the definition measures and what it does not.

- **The canonical case.** The symmetric walk: $[M, M]_k = k$, the same on
  all $2^k$ paths, computed without ever mentioning a probability.
- **A case with a different surface.** The scaled walk $W^{(n)}$ up to a
  time $t$ with $nt$ an integer: $nt$ increments, each $\pm 1/\sqrt n$,
  each squaring to $1/n$, summing to $nt/n = t$. The next section states
  this as a theorem.
- **The degenerate case.** The rescaling with the wrong exponent,
  $\alpha = 1$: steps of size $1/n$, $nt$ of them, squared sum
  $nt/n^2 = t/n$, which goes to zero. A process with steps of order
  $1/n$ over periods of length $1/n$ is a process with a bounded slope,
  and its quadratic variation vanishes in the limit. Quadratic variation
  is the quantity that separates a path with a derivative from one
  without.
- **The near miss.** Drop the square: $\sum_{j=1}^k |M_j - M_{j-1}|$, the
  *first-order variation* of the path, its total length measured
  vertically. For the walk this is also $k$, and the two sums are
  indistinguishable at step size one. For the scaled walk they are not:
  $nt$ increments of absolute size $1/\sqrt n$ sum to $t\sqrt n$, which
  goes to infinity. The clause that separates them is the square, and the
  scaling is what makes the clause bite.

Produce one more case before reading on: a walk with an unfair coin,
$p = 0.7$. Decide which clause of the definition changes, and which
quantity of the walk changes instead.

### Quadratic variation is not variance

The two numbers agree for the symmetric walk, $[M, M]_k = k = \mathrm{Var}(M_k)$,
and the agreement invites the claim that quadratic variation is variance
under another name. The correct statement first: **quadratic variation is
a property of one path**, computed from the realized increments alone;
variance is a property of the ensemble, an average of $M_k^2$ over all
paths weighted by their probabilities. The two agree for the symmetric
walk for one reason only: the squared step $X_j^2 = 1$ is the same on
every path, so the average of $X_j^2$ over paths, which is what variance
adds up, equals $X_j^2$ on any one path, which is what quadratic variation
adds up.

The walk with $p = 0.7$ separates them. Its squared steps are still
$(\pm 1)^2 = 1$, so $[M, M]_k = k$ on every path, unchanged. Its variance
is $\mathrm{Var}(M_k) = k \cdot \mathrm{Var}(X_1) = k\bigl(1 - (2p - 1)^2\bigr) = 4pq\,k = 0.84\,k$,
because the mean $2p - 1 = 0.4$ is no longer zero. Change the coin and
the variance moves; the quadratic variation does not, because the coin
never entered its computation.

The distinction is operational, not philosophical. A variance is an
average over paths that were not realized, and can only be computed
inside a model. A quadratic variation is a sum along the one path that
was realized, and can be computed from a price record: square each tick's
change and add. <Ref lesson="quadratic-variation-lab">The module's
lab</Ref> builds the meter that does exactly this, and the two lessons
before it prove what the meter should read on the limit process: $t$.

<KeyFact>
For the symmetric walk the quadratic variation happens not to depend on
the path; for the processes built from it later in the course, the
stock's above all, it does, and it remains the one quantity a single
realized path determines. Nothing about probabilities enters it, which
is why a change of measure, <Ref lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's
theorem</Ref>, can move a drift but never a volatility.
</KeyFact>

<Example title="quadratic variation of W⁽¹⁰⁰⁾ up to time 1.37 (Shreve II, 3.2.5)">
The scaled walk with $n = 100$ takes steps of size $\tfrac{1}{10}$, one
hundred per unit time. Up to time $1.37$ it has taken $137$ steps. Its
quadratic variation is
$$
[W^{(100)}, W^{(100)}](1.37) = \sum_{j=1}^{137} \Bigl[W^{(100)}\bigl(\tfrac{j}{100}\bigr) - W^{(100)}\bigl(\tfrac{j-1}{100}\bigr)\Bigr]^2
= \sum_{j=1}^{137} \Bigl[\tfrac{1}{10} X_j\Bigr]^2 = 137 \cdot \tfrac{1}{100} = 1.37 .
$$
Every term is $\tfrac{1}{100}$ whichever way the coin fell, so the sum is
the elapsed time, on this path and on all $2^{137}$ others. The
first-order variation on the same stretch is
$\sum_{j=1}^{137} \tfrac{1}{10}|X_j| = 13.7$, ten times the elapsed time,
and it would be $\sqrt{n}$ times the elapsed time at any other $n$.
</Example>

## The scaled walk keeps every property, and hands $[W, W](t) = t$ to the limit unchanged

Scaling multiplies the walk by $1/\sqrt n$ and relabels the time axis. A
constant multiple of a martingale is a martingale, independent blocks of
tosses stay independent, and squared increments scale by $1/n$; the three
properties survive with the variances and the quadratic variation
converted into units of time.

<Theorem title="properties of the scaled walk (Shreve II, 3.2.5)">
Let $0 \le s < t$ be such that $ns$ and $nt$ are integers. Then:

1. For $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_m$ with every $nt_i$ an integer, the
   increments $W^{(n)}(t_{i+1}) - W^{(n)}(t_i)$ are independent, with
   mean zero and variance $t_{i+1} - t_i$.
2. $E_{ns}\bigl[W^{(n)}(t)\bigr] = W^{(n)}(s)$: the scaled walk is a
   martingale with respect to the information in the tosses.
3. $[W^{(n)}, W^{(n)}](t) = \sum_{j=1}^{nt} \bigl[W^{(n)}(\tfrac{j}{n}) - W^{(n)}(\tfrac{j-1}{n})\bigr]^2 = t$
   on every path.

<Proof>
(1) The increment $W^{(n)}(t_{i+1}) - W^{(n)}(t_i)$ is $1/\sqrt n$ times
$M_{nt_{i+1}} - M_{nt_i}$, an increment of the walk over
$n(t_{i+1} - t_i)$ tosses. Independence and zero mean carry over from
the walk's increments; the variance is
$\tfrac{1}{n} \cdot n(t_{i+1} - t_i) = t_{i+1} - t_i$.
(2) $E_{ns}[M_{nt}] = M_{ns}$ by the walk's martingale property, and
dividing both sides by $\sqrt n$ gives the claim.
(3) Each one-step increment is $X_j/\sqrt n$, whose square is $1/n$;
there are $nt$ of them.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Property 3 is the one to hold on to. For every $n$, before any limit is
taken, the scaled walk's quadratic variation over $[0, t]$ is exactly $t$
on every path. Whatever object the walks converge to, they approach it
carrying quadratic variation $t$ the whole way. The limit does not create
the fact that the next lesson writes $dW\,dW = dt$; the limit inherits it.
By contrast, property 1 says only that the *variance* of an increment is
$t - s$, a statement about the average over paths, and the two statements
are about as different as two statements with the same number can be.

<KeyFact>
The length of the scaled path over $[0, t]$ is $t\sqrt n$ at every
refinement, on every path, so the object the walks converge to has no
length while keeping quadratic variation $t$; a path with a derivative
has the opposite pair, finite length and zero quadratic variation, and
no continuous path has finite length together with a nonzero quadratic
variation. <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">The next
lesson</Ref> proves both halves for the limit itself.
</KeyFact>

## The distribution at a fixed time: $W^{(100)}(0.25)$ by count, then the normal by theorem

The three properties are exact at every $n$. What the limit adds is a
distribution: at a fixed time $t$, the value $W^{(n)}(t)$ is a rescaled
count of heads, and the count of heads in many tosses is where the normal
distribution comes from. The claim is checked first at $n = 100$ by
counting, and only then stated as a theorem.

### One bar computed

Fix $t = 0.25$ and $n = 100$. Then $W^{(100)}(0.25) = M_{25}/10$ is
generated by $25$ tosses. $M_{25}$ is heads minus tails, and with an odd
number of tosses it is an odd integer between $-25$ and $25$, so
$W^{(100)}(0.25)$ takes the $26$ values
$-2.5, -2.3, \ldots, -0.1, 0.1, \ldots, 2.3, 2.5$, spaced $0.2$ apart.

<Example title="P{W⁽¹⁰⁰⁾(0.25) = 0.1} (Shreve II, 3.2.6)">
The value $0.1$ requires $M_{25} = 1$: thirteen heads and twelve tails in
$25$ tosses. The number of such sequences is $\binom{25}{13} = 5{,}200{,}300$,
out of $2^{25} = 33{,}554{,}432$ equally likely sequences, so
$$
P\bigl\{W^{(100)}(0.25) = 0.1\bigr\} = \binom{25}{13}\Bigl(\tfrac12\Bigr)^{25} = \frac{5{,}200{,}300}{33{,}554{,}432} = 0.1550 .
$$
Draw this probability as a histogram bar centred at $0.1$ with width $0.2$,
the spacing of the lattice; its area is the probability, so its height is
$0.1550/0.2 = 0.775$.

The random variable $W^{(100)}(0.25)$ has mean $0$ and variance $0.25$.
The normal density with that mean and variance is
$$
\varphi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 0.25}}\,e^{-x^2/(2 \cdot 0.25)} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-2x^2},
$$
with peak $\varphi(0) = 0.798$. At the bar's centre,
$\varphi(0.1) = 0.798 \times e^{-0.02} = 0.798 \times 0.980 = 0.782$,
and the normal's probability of the bar's footprint $[0, 0.2]$ is
approximately width times density, $0.2 \times 0.782 = 0.156$. The exact
count gave $0.155$: $0.155$ against $0.156$, a relative gap under one
percent, and the sign of the gap, bar below curve, is the flatness the
whole histogram shows below.
</Example>

The practical consequence, which is the form in which the next four
lessons use the limit, is that an expectation of a function of
$W^{(n)}(t)$ can be computed with an integral against the density instead
of a sum over $2^{nt}$ sequences: for a bounded continuous $g$,
$$
E\,g\bigl(W^{(100)}(0.25)\bigr) \approx \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)\,\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-2x^2}\,dx .
$$

### The central limit theorem for the scaled walk

The [central limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem)
is the statement that the approximation becomes exact as $n \to \infty$.
The version below is the one this course needs, and its proof runs
through the *moment-generating function*, the expectation $E\,e^{uX}$ as
a function of the real parameter $u$, which turns the sum of independent
steps into a product.

<Theorem title="central limit theorem for the scaled walk (Shreve II, Theorem 3.2.1)">
Fix $t > 0$. As $n \to \infty$ through values with $nt$ an integer, the
distribution of $W^{(n)}(t)$ converges to the normal distribution with
mean zero and variance $t$: for every $x$,
$P\{W^{(n)}(t) \le x\} \to \int_{-\infty}^{x} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}} e^{-y^2/2t}\,dy$.

<Proof>
Goal: show the moment-generating function of $W^{(n)}(t)$ converges to
that of $N(0, t)$; the passage from moment-generating functions to
distributions is the one fact the proof takes from outside, and it is
named at the end. Roadmap: compute the normal's function; write the
walk's as a power of $\cosh$; squeeze its logarithm.

**The normal's function.** For $X \sim N(0, t)$, completing the square in
the exponent,
$$
E\,e^{uX} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{ux}\,\frac{e^{-x^2/2t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,dx
= e^{u^2 t/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-(x - ut)^2/2t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,dx = e^{u^2 t/2},
$$
since the last integrand is the $N(ut, t)$ density and integrates to one.

**The walk's function.** $W^{(n)}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^{nt} X_j$
with independent $X_j = \pm 1$, so
$$
\varphi_n(u) = E\,e^{uW^{(n)}(t)} = \prod_{j=1}^{nt} E\,e^{uX_j/\sqrt n}
= \Bigl(\tfrac12 e^{u/\sqrt n} + \tfrac12 e^{-u/\sqrt n}\Bigr)^{nt}
= \bigl(\cosh(u/\sqrt n)\bigr)^{nt},
$$
using independence to turn the expectation of a product into a product
of expectations. It remains to show $nt \log\cosh(u/\sqrt n) \to u^2 t/2$.

**The squeeze.** For every real $x$,
$1 + \tfrac{x^2}{2} \le \cosh x \le e^{x^2/2}$: the lower bound keeps the
first two terms of the all-positive series
$\cosh x = \sum_k x^{2k}/(2k)!$, and the upper bound compares that series
term by term with $e^{x^2/2} = \sum_k x^{2k}/(2^k k!)$, using
$(2k)! \ge 2^k k!$. Taking logarithms and using
$\log(1 + y) \ge y - y^2/2$ for $y \ge 0$ (the difference has derivative
$y^2/(1 + y) \ge 0$ and vanishes at $0$),
$$
\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{8} \le \log\cosh x \le \frac{x^2}{2}.
$$
Put $x = u/\sqrt n$ and multiply by $nt$:
$$
\frac{u^2 t}{2} - \frac{u^4 t}{8n} \le nt\,\log\cosh\frac{u}{\sqrt n} \le \frac{u^2 t}{2}.
$$
Both ends tend to $u^2 t/2$, so $\log\varphi_n(u) \to u^2 t/2$ and
$\varphi_n(u) \to e^{u^2 t/2}$ for every real $u$.

**The fenced step.** If the moment-generating functions of a sequence of
random variables converge, on an open interval around $0$, to the
moment-generating function of $X$, then the distributions converge to the
distribution of $X$ ([Curtiss 1942](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177731541);
Billingsley, *Probability and Measure*, §30, in the method-of-moments form).
This course states it and does not prove it.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

After the proof: the idea in one sentence is that independence makes the
generating function a product, the product a power, and the power's
logarithm $nt$ times a quantity of order $1/n$ whose leading coefficient
is $u^2/2$. The step that would fail for the wrong exponent is the
squeeze: with steps of size $n^{-\alpha}$ the middle term is
$nt\log\cosh(u\,n^{-\alpha})$, of order $t\,n^{1 - 2\alpha}u^2/2$, which
tends to $0$ or $\infty$ unless $\alpha = \tfrac12$.

<Remark title="the fence: what the theorem does and does not say">
Three things are outside the theorem, and the module names them here
because every later lesson stands on the same fence.

1. **Generating functions to distributions.** The last step of the proof
   is a cited theorem. Its proof is complex analysis or measure theory;
   the course uses it as stated.
2. **One time, not a path.** The theorem is about $W^{(n)}(t)$ for one
   fixed $t$. The same proof, run on the increments of the scaled-walk theorem (each
   a smaller scaled walk), shows that any finite list
   $\bigl(W^{(n)}(t_1), \ldots, W^{(n)}(t_m)\bigr)$ converges to
   independent normal increments. It says nothing about the whole
   random function $t \mapsto W^{(n)}(t)$ converging to a whole random
   function. That stronger statement is true: it is
   [Donsker's invariance principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donsker%27s_theorem)
   (1951), which puts a distribution on the space of continuous paths and
   proves convergence there. Its proof needs a compactness argument on
   path space that this course does not carry.
3. **The limit object.** Nothing above constructs a process with
   continuous paths and independent normal increments. <Ref
   lesson="brownian-motion">The next lesson</Ref> writes down that
   process's defining properties, names its existence as the module's
   central fenced fact, and proves everything else from the definition.
</Remark>

<Exercise>
Compute $P\{W^{(64)}(0.5) = 0.25\}$ by binomial count and compare it with
the normal approximation, bin width times density. State first which
values $W^{(64)}(0.5)$ can take and why $0.125$ is not one of them.

<Solution>
Strategy: identify the underlying walk and the number of heads the value
requires, then count; the normal supplies the density of the right
variance at the bar's centre.

**The lattice.** $W^{(64)}(0.5) = M_{32}/8$. With $32$ tosses, $M_{32}$
is an even integer between $-32$ and $32$, so the values are even
multiples of $\tfrac18$: $0, \pm 0.25, \pm 0.5, \ldots, \pm 4$, spaced
$0.25$ apart. The value $0.125$ would need $M_{32} = 1$, impossible with
an even number of tosses.

**The count.** $0.25 = M_{32}/8$ needs $M_{32} = 2$: seventeen heads and
fifteen tails. $\binom{32}{17} = 565{,}722{,}720$ and
$2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296$, so the probability is $0.1317$.

**The approximation.** The variance of $W^{(64)}(0.5)$ is $0.5$, so the
density is $\varphi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-x^2}$ and
$\varphi(0.25) = 0.5642 \times e^{-0.0625} = 0.5642 \times 0.9394 = 0.530$.
Bin width times density: $0.25 \times 0.530 = 0.1325$. The gap to the
exact $0.1317$ is $0.6\%$ of the value; at $n = 100$ it was $0.9\%$. The
transferable move: the normal approximates the probability of a bin of
the lattice, never of a point, and the bin's width is the lattice spacing
$2/\sqrt n$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The calibrated stock: $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ sends $\log S$ to the normal and $S$ to the lognormal

The walk is the tree without prices. Putting the prices back turns the
central limit theorem into a statement about the stock, and the
statement has one feature that is invisible at any finite $n$: a
$-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ in the exponent of the limit, which the risk-neutral
coin manufactures out of the very quadratic variation of the last three
sections.

### Why $\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and not $\sigma\,\Delta t$

<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">The binomial model</Ref> moves the stock
to $uS$ or $dS$ each period with $d < 1 + r < u$ and never says how large
$u$ and $d$ are. Build the model with $n$ periods per unit time, so a
period is $\Delta t = 1/n$, and take the down factor to be the reciprocal
of the up factor, $d_n = 1/u_n$, so that an up followed by a down returns
the stock to where it was and the tree recombines on a lattice. Write
$u_n = e^{a_n}$. After $nt$ periods with $H$ heads and $T$ tails, and
with $M_{nt} = H - T$,
$$
\log\frac{S_n(t)}{S_0} = H\,a_n - T\,a_n = a_n\,M_{nt}.
$$
The log of the stock is a multiple of the walk. Under the fair coin its
variance is $a_n^2 \cdot nt$, and the requirement that it be $\sigma^2 t$,
a variance that accumulates at a fixed rate $\sigma^2$ per unit time
whatever the refinement, forces $a_n^2 n = \sigma^2$:
$$
u_n = e^{\sigma/\sqrt n} = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d_n = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
$$
This is the calibration <Ref lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">the lab's
`crr_tree`</Ref> ships, and the count that forced it is the count of the
first section: the log-return per period is $\pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$
because $n$ steps of size $s$ have variance $ns^2$, and
$\sigma\,\Delta t$ would have given a log-stock with variance
$\sigma^2 t/n \to 0$, a stock with no randomness in the limit. Shreve's
§3.2.7 uses $u_n = 1 + \sigma/\sqrt n$, $d_n = 1 - \sigma/\sqrt n$
instead; the two differ by $O(1/n)$ per period and reach the same limit.

Interest is compounded continuously from here on: one dollar in the money
market grows to $e^{r/n}$ over a period and $e^{rt}$ over $nt$ periods,
whatever $n$ is, which is the property the tree needs so that refining
it does not change the riskless return over a fixed calendar time. The
admissibility condition $d_n < e^{r/n} < u_n$ reads
$-\sigma/\sqrt n < r/n < \sigma/\sqrt n$, which holds as soon as
$n > (r/\sigma)^2$; for $r = 0.05$ and $\sigma = 0.2$ that is every
$n \ge 1$.

### The risk-neutral coin tilts by $1/\sqrt n$

The risk-neutral probability of the tree is the algebra of <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">one-period replication</Ref> with the
continuous-compounding growth factor in place of $1 + r$:
$$
\tilde p_n = \frac{e^{r/n} - d_n}{u_n - d_n} = \frac{e^{r/n} - e^{-\sigma/\sqrt n}}{e^{\sigma/\sqrt n} - e^{-\sigma/\sqrt n}} .
$$
For $\sigma = 0.2$, $r = 0.05$ and $n = 4$: $u_4 = e^{0.1} = 1.10517$,
$d_4 = 0.90484$, $e^{r/4} = 1.01258$, and
$\tilde p_4 = 0.10774/0.20033 = 0.5378$. At $n = 16$ the value is
$0.5188$, at $n = 64$ it is $0.5094$, at $n = 1024$ it is $0.5023$: the
coin tends to fair, and the excess over $\tfrac12$ halves each time $n$
quadruples, which is the signature of $1/\sqrt n$.

<Theorem title="the risk-neutral coin of the calibrated tree">
As $n \to \infty$,
$$
\tilde p_n = \frac12 + \frac{r - \tfrac12\sigma^2}{2\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt n} + O\Bigl(\frac1n\Bigr),
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
2\tilde p_n - 1 = \frac{r - \tfrac12\sigma^2}{\sigma\sqrt n} + O\Bigl(\frac1n\Bigr).
$$

<Proof>
Expand numerator and denominator in powers of $1/\sqrt n$. Numerator:
$$
e^{r/n} - e^{-\sigma/\sqrt n}
= \Bigl(1 + \frac rn\Bigr) - \Bigl(1 - \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2n} - \frac{\sigma^3}{6n^{3/2}}\Bigr) + O\Bigl(\frac{1}{n^2}\Bigr)
= \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Bigl[1 + \frac{r - \tfrac12\sigma^2}{\sigma\sqrt n} + \frac{\sigma^2}{6n}\Bigr] + O\Bigl(\frac{1}{n^2}\Bigr).
$$
Denominator: $2\sinh(\sigma/\sqrt n) = \frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt n}\bigl[1 + \frac{\sigma^2}{6n}\bigr] + O(n^{-5/2})$.
Dividing, the common factor $\sigma/\sqrt n$ cancels, the $\sigma^2/6n$
terms cancel to the order kept, and
$\tilde p_n = \tfrac12\bigl[1 + \frac{r - \sigma^2/2}{\sigma\sqrt n} + O(1/n)\bigr]$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Figure
  src="./figures/ptilde-tilt.svg"
  alt="The quantity (p-tilde minus one half) times the square root of n plotted against n on a log scale for sigma 0.2 and r 0.05, decreasing from 0.0775 at n = 1 toward the predicted constant 0.075"
  caption="(p̃ₙ − ½)·√n for σ = 0.2, r = 0.05: the exact coin approaches the predicted (r − ½σ²)/(2σ) = 0.075, the remaining gap shrinking like 1/n."
/>

The tilt is of order $1/\sqrt n$, and the exercise on the unfair coin
shows why it must be: a fixed tilt $2p - 1 = c$ gives the scaled walk a
mean $c\sqrt n\,t$ that explodes, while a tilt of order $1/\sqrt n$ gives
a mean of order $t$ that survives. The risk-neutral coin is exactly as
unfair as the scaling tolerates.

### Where the $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ comes from: a squared step that is not random

The numerator of the tilt, $r - \tfrac12\sigma^2$, has a derivation that
is shorter than the expansion and shows what the half is. Let
$Y = \log(S_{k+1}/S_k) = \pm\sigma/\sqrt n$ be one period's log-return.
The identity that defines $\tilde p_n$ is that the stock's risk-neutral
mean growth is the riskless growth,
$\tilde E[S_{k+1}/S_k] = \tilde E[e^Y] = e^{r/n}$, the one-period form of
the fact that <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the
discounted stock is a $\tilde P$-martingale</Ref>. Expand
$e^Y = 1 + Y + \tfrac12 Y^2 + O(n^{-3/2})$ and take the risk-neutral mean:
$$
1 + \tilde E[Y] + \tfrac12\,\tilde E[Y^2] + O(n^{-3/2}) = 1 + \frac rn + O(n^{-2}).
$$
Now $Y^2 = \sigma^2/n$ on both branches: the squared log-return is not a
random variable at all, and $\tilde E[Y^2] = \sigma^2/n$ under any coin.
Solving,
$$
\tilde E[Y] = \frac{r - \tfrac12\sigma^2}{n} + O(n^{-3/2}),
$$
and over $nt$ periods the log of the stock has risk-neutral mean
$(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,t + O(n^{-1/2})$. The mean growth rate of the
stock is $r$; the mean growth rate of its logarithm is $r$ less half the
variance rate, because $e^Y$ is convex and the convexity term
$\tfrac12 Y^2$ is a quadratic variation, deterministic on the tree and
equal to $\sigma^2$ per unit time.

<Example title="the calibrated tree at n = 4, σ = 0.2, r = 0.05, over one year">
The per-period log-return is $Y = \pm 0.1$ with
$\tilde p_4 = 0.5378$. Its mean is
$(2\tilde p_4 - 1)(0.1) = 0.0756 \times 0.1 = 0.00756$, against the
prediction $(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)/n = (0.05 - 0.02)/4 = 0.0075$. Its
variance is $\tilde E[Y^2] - (\tilde E Y)^2 = 0.01 - 0.0000572 = 0.009943$,
against $\sigma^2/n = 0.01$. Over four periods the log of the stock has
mean $0.0302$ and variance $0.0398$, against the limit's $0.03$ and
$0.04$. The mean is above its limit by $0.0002$, which is the $O(1/n)$
remainder of the theorem, and the variance is below its limit by the
square of the mean, $4 \times 0.00756^2$: the tilt spends a little of
the step's variance on drift.

The five terminal prices are
$100\,e^{0.1 k}$ for $k = -4, -2, 0, 2, 4$: $67.0, 81.9, 100.0, 122.1, 149.2$,
with risk-neutral probabilities $\binom{4}{j}\tilde p_4^{\,j}(1 - \tilde p_4)^{4 - j}$.
Their discounted mean is $100$ exactly, because the discounted stock is a
martingale at every $n$, not only in the limit.
</Example>

### The lognormal limit

The log of the stock is $\sigma/\sqrt n$ times a walk whose coin is
$\tilde p_n$. The central limit theorem's proof runs on it with one
change, the coin, and the tilt of the coin is what puts the mean into the
limit.

<Theorem title="lognormal limit of the calibrated binomial model (Shreve II, Theorem 3.2.2 and Exercise 3.8; CRR §5)">
Let $S_n(t) = S_0\,u_n^{H}d_n^{T}$ be the stock of the tree with
$u_n = e^{\sigma/\sqrt n}$, $d_n = 1/u_n$ and risk-neutral coin
$\tilde p_n$, after $nt$ periods. As $n \to \infty$, the distribution of
$\log\bigl(S_n(t)/S_0\bigr)$ under $\tilde P$ converges to the normal
distribution with mean $(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t$ and variance
$\sigma^2 t$. Consequently the distribution of $S_n(t)$ converges to that
of
$$
S(t) = S_0 \exp\Bigl\{\sigma W(t) + \bigl(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)t\Bigr\}, \qquad W(t) \sim N(0, t).
$$

<Proof>
Goal: the moment-generating function of
$L_n = \log(S_n(t)/S_0) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} M_{nt}$, where $M$ is now
the walk with head probability $\tilde p_n$, converges to that of
$N\bigl((r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t, \sigma^2 t\bigr)$, which by the
completing-the-square computation of the previous proof is
$\exp\{(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t\,u + \tfrac12\sigma^2 t\,u^2\}$. Then the
fenced step of that proof finishes.

**Product form.** With $a = u\sigma/\sqrt n$, independence gives
$$
\varphi_n(u) = E\,e^{uL_n} = \bigl(\tilde p_n e^{a} + \tilde q_n e^{-a}\bigr)^{nt}
= \bigl(\cosh a + (2\tilde p_n - 1)\sinh a\bigr)^{nt},
$$
using $\tilde p_n e^a + \tilde q_n e^{-a} = \tfrac12(e^a + e^{-a}) + (\tilde p_n - \tilde q_n)\tfrac12(e^a - e^{-a})$.

**The base to order $1/n$.** $\cosh a = 1 + \tfrac{u^2\sigma^2}{2n} + O(n^{-2})$
and $\sinh a = \tfrac{u\sigma}{\sqrt n} + O(n^{-3/2})$. By the coin
theorem, $2\tilde p_n - 1 = \frac{r - \sigma^2/2}{\sigma\sqrt n} + O(1/n)$,
so
$(2\tilde p_n - 1)\sinh a = \frac{(r - \sigma^2/2)\,u}{n} + O(n^{-3/2})$.
The base is therefore
$$
1 + \frac{c(u)}{n} + O(n^{-3/2}), \qquad c(u) = \bigl(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)u + \tfrac12\sigma^2 u^2 .
$$

**The logarithm.** $\log(1 + y) = y + O(y^2)$, so
$\log\varphi_n(u) = nt\bigl[\frac{c(u)}{n} + O(n^{-3/2})\bigr] = t\,c(u) + O(n^{-1/2}) \to t\,c(u)$,
which is the target. The fenced step converts convergence of
$\varphi_n$ into convergence of the distribution of $L_n$.

**From $\log S$ to $S$.** For $s > 0$,
$P\{S_n(t) \le s\} = P\{L_n \le \log(s/S_0)\}$ exactly, because
$\exp$ is increasing; the right side converges to
$P\{\sigma W(t) + (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t \le \log(s/S_0)\}$, which is
$P\{S(t) \le s\}$. No further theorem is needed.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

A random variable of the form $c\,e^X$ with $X$ normal is called
*lognormal*; its logarithm is normal. Here $\log S(t)$ is normal with mean
$\log S_0 + (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t$ and variance $\sigma^2 t$. Two
consequences fix the shape. The **median** of $S(t)$ is
$S_0 e^{(r - \sigma^2/2)t}$, the exponential of the median of the log.
The **mean** is $S_0 e^{rt}$: by the moment-generating function at
$u = \sigma$, $E\,e^{\sigma W(t)} = e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$, which cancels the
$-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ exactly and leaves $E[S(t)] = S_0 e^{rt}$. The
discounted stock has mean $S_0$ in the limit as on every tree: the
martingale property of module 2 survives the passage to continuous time
intact. For $S_0 = 100$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $r = 0.05$, $t = 1$ the median
is $103.0$ and the mean $105.1$; the density is skewed right, its peak
below the median and its mean above.

<Remark title="the limit is a choice, not a consequence of refinement (CRR §5)">
Refining the tree does not by itself produce the lognormal. Cox, Ross and
Rubinstein's §5 exhibits a second scaling of the same binomial model:
hold $u$ fixed, let $d_n = e^{\zeta/n}$ and give the up move probability
$\lambda/n$. Each period the stock almost always drifts by the small
factor $d_n$ and occasionally, with probability shrinking like $1/n$,
jumps by the fixed factor $u$. The number of jumps by time $t$ converges
to a Poisson variable with mean $\lambda t$, the log of the stock to a
Poisson multiple of $\log u$ plus a drift, and the option price to a
different closed formula. Both limits come from the same two-state
period; what selects the normal is the choice that both moves shrink like
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$, and the condition in the central limit theorem that
selects it is that no single period's move stays large relative to the
accumulated standard deviation.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The $-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ in the exponent of the limit is the convexity
correction $\tfrac12 Y^2$ of a log-return whose square is deterministic:
$\sigma^2\,\Delta t$ per period, path by path. <Ref lesson="ito-formula">The
Itô formula</Ref> recomputes the same term as $\tfrac12 f''(S)\,dS\,dS$
with $f = \log$, and <Ref lesson="black-scholes-formula">the
Black–Scholes formula</Ref> carries it as the gap between its two
arguments $d_1$ and $d_2$.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
The physical process the limit is named after was seen before it was
computed. Robert Brown, a botanist, reported in 1827 that pollen grains
suspended in water jitter without cease under the microscope. Louis
Bachelier's 1900 thesis at the Sorbonne, *Théorie de la spéculation*,
modelled bond prices on the Paris bourse as the limit of a random walk and
obtained the normal spread growing like $\sqrt t$, five years before
Einstein's 1905 paper derived the same law, mean squared displacement
proportional to elapsed time, from the accumulated kicks of molecules on a
suspended particle, and Jean Perrin's measurements of 1908 confirmed it
and settled the existence of atoms. The
[MinuteLabs Brownian-motion simulation](https://labs.minutelabs.io/Brownian-Motion/)
shows the mechanism directly: a large particle among many small ones, with
a slider that reveals the small ones, and a trace that is the scaled walk
of this lesson in two dimensions. Its page links the MinutePhysics
account of Einstein's argument.
</Aside>

What is established: with $n$ steps per unit time, the walk's spread at
time $t$ is finite and nonzero only for steps of size $1/\sqrt n$, the
exponent being forced by variance adding across independent steps; the
scaled walk $W^{(n)}(t) = M_{nt}/\sqrt n$ has independent increments
with variance equal to elapsed time, is a martingale, and has quadratic
variation exactly $t$ on every path at every $n$, a sum along one
realized path that coincides with the variance for the symmetric walk
and diverges from it the moment the coin is unfair; its first-order
variation is $t\sqrt n$ on every path; at any fixed time its distribution
converges to $N(0, t)$, verified bar by bar at $n = 100$ to within one
percent and proved through the moment-generating function with one cited step;
and the calibrated stock with $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ and the
risk-neutral coin $\tilde p_n = \tfrac12 + \frac{r - \sigma^2/2}{2\sigma\sqrt n} + O(1/n)$
converges to the lognormal
$S_0\exp\{\sigma W(t) + (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t\}$, the $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$
being the convexity term of a log-return whose square is deterministic.
What is not established is the limit object itself: a process
$W(t)$ with continuous paths, independent increments $W(t) - W(s) \sim N(0, t - s)$,
and quadratic variation $t$, defined for all $t$ at once and not one time
at a time. <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">The next lesson</Ref> defines
Brownian motion by those properties, marks its existence as the module's
central fenced fact, proves the martingale property and $[W, W](T) = T$
from the definition, shows the first-order variation is infinite, and
fixes the meaning of $dW\,dW = dt$.

<Exercise>
Rescale the walk with steps of size $n^{-\alpha}$, $n$ per unit time, so
that the position at time $t$ is $W_\alpha^{(n)}(t) = n^{-\alpha}M_{nt}$.
For $\alpha = 1$ and $\alpha = \tfrac14$, compute
$\mathrm{Var}\bigl(W_\alpha^{(n)}(1)\bigr)$ and
$[W_\alpha^{(n)}, W_\alpha^{(n)}](1)$ as functions of $n$, and describe
the limit distribution of $W_\alpha^{(n)}(1)$ in each case. Then show that
$\alpha = \tfrac12$ is the only exponent for which the moment-generating
function proof of the central limit theorem produces a nondegenerate
limit, naming the line of the proof that fails otherwise.

<Solution>
**Variance and quadratic variation.** Both are the same count:
$n$ increments, each squaring to $n^{-2\alpha}$, so
$\mathrm{Var} = [W_\alpha, W_\alpha](1) = n^{1 - 2\alpha}$. For
$\alpha = 1$ this is $1/n$; for $\alpha = \tfrac14$ it is $\sqrt n$.

**The limits.** $\alpha = 1$: $W_1^{(n)}(1) = M_n/n$ has mean $0$ and
variance $1/n \to 0$, so by Chebyshev
$P\{|W_1^{(n)}(1)| > \varepsilon\} \le 1/(n\varepsilon^2) \to 0$: the
limit distribution is the point mass at $0$. (This is the weak law of
large numbers for the fraction of heads.) $\alpha = \tfrac14$:
$W_{1/4}^{(n)}(1) = n^{1/4}\,(M_n/\sqrt n)$, a factor $n^{1/4} \to \infty$
times a variable that converges to $N(0, 1)$; for any fixed $x$,
$P\{|W_{1/4}^{(n)}(1)| \le x\} = P\{|M_n/\sqrt n| \le x n^{-1/4}\} \to 0$,
so no probability remains on any bounded interval and there is no limit
distribution.

**The proof's failing line.** The generating function is
$\bigl(\cosh(u\,n^{-\alpha})\bigr)^{n}$, and the squeeze gives
$$
\frac{u^2}{2}\,n^{1 - 2\alpha} - \frac{u^4}{8}\,n^{1 - 4\alpha} \le \log\varphi_n(u) \le \frac{u^2}{2}\,n^{1 - 2\alpha}.
$$
For $\alpha > \tfrac12$ both ends go to $0$: $\varphi_n \to 1$, the
generating function of the constant $0$. For $\alpha < \tfrac12$ the
upper bound goes to $\infty$ for every $u \ne 0$ and so does the lower
bound, so $\varphi_n(u) \to \infty$, which is the generating function of
nothing. Only $\alpha = \tfrac12$ makes the exponent $1 - 2\alpha$ vanish
and leaves $u^2/2$. The transferable move: a scaling exponent is found by
asking which power of $n$ the second moment carries, and the answer is
the unique exponent that makes it $n^0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague states: "Quadratic variation is a fancy name for variance. The
lesson's own theorem says $[M, M]_k = k = \mathrm{Var}(M_k)$, and the
scaled version says $[W^{(n)}, W^{(n)}](t) = t = \mathrm{Var}(W^{(n)}(t))$;
two quantities that agree for every $k$, every $n$ and every $t$ are the
same quantity." Refute the claim with a computation, name the object each
quantity is a function of, and state the one feature of the symmetric
walk that produces the coincidence.

<Solution>
**The counterexample.** Take the walk with $p = 0.7$. Its steps are
still $\pm 1$, so $[M, M]_k = \sum (\pm 1)^2 = k$ on every path. Its
variance is $k\,\mathrm{Var}(X_1)$ with
$\mathrm{Var}(X_1) = E[X_1^2] - (E X_1)^2 = 1 - 0.4^2 = 0.84$, so
$\mathrm{Var}(M_k) = 0.84\,k \ne k$. Two quantities that disagree on this
walk are not the same quantity, and the agreement on the symmetric walk
is a coincidence of that walk, not an identity.

**What each is a function of.** $[M, M]_k$ is a function of one
path $\omega = \omega_1\omega_2\cdots$: a random variable, computed from
the realized increments by squaring and adding, with no probability in
the computation. $\mathrm{Var}(M_k) = E[M_k^2] - (E M_k)^2$ is a number,
a function of the measure: an average of $M_k^2$ over all $2^k$ paths
weighted by $p^{\#H}q^{\#T}$. The first can be read off a price record;
the second exists only inside a model.

**The coincidence.** For the symmetric walk, $E[M_k] = 0$ so
$\mathrm{Var}(M_k) = E[M_k^2] = E\bigl[\sum_j X_j^2 + \sum_{i \ne j} X_i X_j\bigr] = \sum_j E[X_j^2]$,
the cross terms having mean zero by independence. Since $X_j^2 = 1$ is
the same on every path, $E[X_j^2] = X_j^2$ and the variance equals the
path sum. Both ingredients are needed: zero mean kills the cross terms,
and the deterministic squared step makes the average of the squares equal
to the squares. The unfair coin breaks the first; a walk with random step
sizes would break the second, and for it $[M, M]_k$ would differ from
path to path while $\mathrm{Var}(M_k)$ stayed one number. The claim fails
at the step "agree for every parameter": the parameters listed never
included the coin.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two martingale checks, one from the walk and one from the tree. (a) Show
that $M_k^2 - k$ is a martingale under the fair coin, and deduce that
$\bigl(W^{(n)}(t)\bigr)^2 - t$ is a martingale for the scaled walk at
times where $nt$ is an integer. (b) On the calibrated tree with
$u_n = e^{\sigma/\sqrt n}$, $d_n = 1/u_n$ and per-period growth
$e^{r/n}$, show that $e^{-rk/n}S_k$ is a martingale under $\tilde p_n$ and
is not a martingale under the fair coin, and compute the fair-coin ratio
$E[e^{-r/n}S_{k+1}/S_k]$ for $\sigma = 0.2$, $r = 0.05$, $n = 4$.

<Solution>
**(a) The walk.** Write $M_{k+1} = M_k + X_{k+1}$. Then
$$
E_k[M_{k+1}^2] = E_k[M_k^2 + 2M_k X_{k+1} + X_{k+1}^2] = M_k^2 + 2M_k\,E[X_{k+1}] + E[X_{k+1}^2] = M_k^2 + 0 + 1,
$$
taking $M_k$ out as known and using independence of $X_{k+1}$ from the
first $k$ tosses. Hence $E_k[M_{k+1}^2 - (k + 1)] = M_k^2 + 1 - k - 1 = M_k^2 - k$:
the martingale clause. For the scaled walk, divide by $n$ and read $k = nt$:
$E_{nt}\bigl[(W^{(n)}(t + \tfrac1n))^2 - (t + \tfrac1n)\bigr] = (W^{(n)}(t))^2 - t$,
and the property between any two lattice times follows by iterating one
step at a time with the tower property.

**(b) The tree, risk-neutral coin.** By construction
$\tilde p_n u_n + \tilde q_n d_n = e^{r/n}$, the identity that defined
$\tilde p_n$. So
$\tilde E_k[e^{-r(k+1)/n}S_{k+1}] = e^{-r(k+1)/n}S_k(\tilde p_n u_n + \tilde q_n d_n) = e^{-rk/n}S_k$,
taking $S_k$ out as known: the discounted stock is a $\tilde P$-martingale
at every $n$, by <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the same
argument as on the \$4 tree</Ref>.

**The fair coin.** Under $p = \tfrac12$,
$E[e^{-r/n}S_{k+1}/S_k] = e^{-r/n}\cdot\tfrac12(u_n + d_n) = e^{-r/n}\cosh(\sigma/\sqrt n)$.
For $\sigma = 0.2$, $r = 0.05$, $n = 4$: $\cosh(0.1) = 1.005004$ and
$e^{-0.0125} = 0.987578$, product $0.99252 \ne 1$. The discounted stock
is a supermartingale under the fair coin: it drifts down by $0.75\%$ per
period in conditional mean, because the fair coin gives the log-return
mean zero while the riskless rate asks for $r/n - \sigma^2/2n = 0.0075$
per period. The transferable move: "$\tilde p$ makes the discounted stock
a martingale" is the same identity at every refinement, and the fair
coin's failure is the drift $r - \tfrac12\sigma^2$ that the limit theorem
puts in the exponent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Four quantities about the walk; decide which kind of problem each part
is before starting. (a) A tick record of $W^{(400)}$ over $[0, 2]$ lists
$800$ consecutive increments, each $+0.05$ or $-0.05$. From the record
alone, give $\sum(\Delta W)^2$ and $\sum|\Delta W|$, and state whether
$\mathrm{Var}\bigl(W^{(400)}(2)\bigr)$ is a third number the record can
give. (b) Give $E\bigl[\bigl(W^{(400)}(2)\bigr)^2\bigr]$ under the fair
coin and under the coin $p = 0.6$. (c) Let $\tau$ be the first $k$ with
$|M_k| = 3$ for the symmetric walk. Compute $E[M_\tau]$ and $E[\tau]$.
(d) Let $\tau_n$ be the first lattice time at which
$|W^{(n)}(t)| = 0.3$, for $n$ with $0.3\sqrt n$ an integer. Compute
$E[\tau_n]$ and say what it does as $n \to \infty$.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** (a) is a sum along one path: no coin, no model. (b) is
an ensemble average, which under the fair coin happens to equal the first
number of (a). (c) is an optional-sampling problem from module 3, and (d)
is (c) rescaled, not a fresh computation.

**(a)** Each squared increment is $0.05^2 = 1/400$, so
$\sum(\Delta W)^2 = 800/400 = 2$; each absolute increment is $0.05$, so
$\sum|\Delta W| = 40 = 2\sqrt{400}$. Both are read off the record with no
reference to a coin. The variance is not: it is an average over the
$2^{800}$ records that were not written, and it needs the coin, which the
record does not reveal. The record is consistent with every $p$.

**(b)** Fair coin: $E[W^2] = \mathrm{Var}(W) = 800 \times \tfrac{1}{400} = 2$,
the same number as (a)'s first, for the reason of the quadratic-variation
section: $X_j^2 = 1$ on every path, so the average of $X_j^2$ over paths
is $X_j^2$ on any one. Under $p = 0.6$: $E[W] = 800 \times 0.2 \times 0.05 = 8$,
$\mathrm{Var}(W) = 800 \times \tfrac{1}{400}(1 - 0.2^2) = 1.92$, so
$E[W^2] = 1.92 + 64 = 65.92$. The path sum of (a) is still $2$ on every
path of this coin too.

**(c)** $\tau$ is a stopping time: $\{\tau = k\}$ is decided by the first
$k$ tosses. By <Ref lesson="stopping-times">optional sampling</Ref> on
the first $N$ tosses, for any $N$, the stopped walk $M_{k \wedge \tau}$
is a martingale, so $E[M_{k \wedge \tau}] = 0$ for every $k$. Now
$|M_{k \wedge \tau}| \le 3$, and $\tau < \infty$ with probability one
(from any point of $\{-2, \ldots, 2\}$ three heads in a row force a hit,
and a run of three heads occurs eventually), so letting $k \to \infty$
gives $E[M_\tau] = 0$; with $M_\tau = \pm 3$ this says
$P\{M_\tau = 3\} = \tfrac12$. For $E[\tau]$ apply the same theorem to the
martingale $M_k^2 - k$ of the previous exercise:
$E[M_{k \wedge \tau}^2] = E[k \wedge \tau]$ for every $k$. As
$k \to \infty$ the left side tends to $E[M_\tau^2] = 9$ (bounded by $9$,
convergent on every path) and the right side increases to $E[\tau]$, so
$E[\tau] = 9$. The passage to the limit in $k$ is the one step module 3's
finite-horizon theorem does not supply.

**(d)** $|W^{(n)}(t)| = 0.3$ is $|M_{nt}| = 0.3\sqrt n$, so
$\tau_n = \tau'/n$ with $\tau'$ the walk's first hit of $\pm 0.3\sqrt n$.
The argument of (c) with $3$ replaced by $a = 0.3\sqrt n$ gives
$E[\tau'] = a^2 = 0.09\,n$, hence $E[\tau_n] = 0.09$ for every admissible
$n$. It does nothing as $n \to \infty$: the expected time to move a
distance $0.3$ is $0.3^2$ time units at every refinement, the
$\text{distance}^2 = \text{time}$ count of the first section read as a
hitting time. The transferable move: before computing, ask whether the
quantity is a function of one path (sum it), of the measure (average it),
or of a stopping rule (stop a martingale and take the limit in the
horizon).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Cox, Ross and Rubinstein calibrate to a general drift. For a time
horizon $t$ divided into $n$ periods, they set
$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{t/n}}$, $d = 1/u$ and a coin
$q = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu/\sigma)\sqrt{t/n}$, with the aim that
$\log(S^*/S)$, the log-return over the horizon, has mean tending to
$\mu t$ and variance tending to $\sigma^2 t$. Compute the exact mean and
variance of $\log(S^*/S)$ for finite $n$, verify the two limits, show
that $q \to \tfrac12$, and identify which $\mu$ reproduces the lesson's
risk-neutral coin to leading order.

<Solution>
**Setup.** With $j$ up moves in $n$ periods,
$\log(S^*/S) = j\log u + (n - j)\log d = j\log(u/d) + n\log d$, and
$j$ is binomial with mean $nq$ and variance $nq(1 - q)$. Here
$\log(u/d) = 2\sigma\sqrt{t/n}$ and $\log d = -\sigma\sqrt{t/n}$.

**Mean.**
$E\log(S^*/S) = 2\sigma\sqrt{t/n}\cdot nq - n\sigma\sqrt{t/n} = n\sigma\sqrt{t/n}\,(2q - 1)$.
With $2q - 1 = (\mu/\sigma)\sqrt{t/n}$ this is
$n\sigma\sqrt{t/n}\cdot(\mu/\sigma)\sqrt{t/n} = \mu t$, exactly, for
every $n$.

**Variance.**
$\mathrm{Var}\log(S^*/S) = [\log(u/d)]^2\,nq(1 - q) = 4\sigma^2\,\tfrac tn\cdot n\,q(1 - q) = 4\sigma^2 t\,q(1 - q)$.
Since $q(1 - q) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac14(2q - 1)^2 = \tfrac14 - \tfrac14(\mu^2/\sigma^2)(t/n)$,
$$
\mathrm{Var}\log(S^*/S) = \sigma^2 t - \mu^2 t\cdot\frac tn = \Bigl(\sigma^2 - \mu^2\frac tn\Bigr)t \to \sigma^2 t .
$$
The variance is short of its limit by exactly the square of the
per-period mean times the number of periods, the same accounting as in
the lesson's $n = 4$ example.

**The coin.** $q - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12(\mu/\sigma)\sqrt{t/n} \to 0$:
the coin tends to fair at rate $1/\sqrt n$, and the tilt per period is
what carries the drift. Comparing with the lesson's
$\tilde p_n = \tfrac12 + \frac{r - \sigma^2/2}{2\sigma}\frac{1}{\sqrt n} + O(1/n)$
at $t = 1$: the two agree to leading order exactly when
$\mu = r - \tfrac12\sigma^2$, the risk-neutral drift of the log. CRR's
$q$ matches the mean exactly at every $n$ and the risk-neutral coin
matches the growth $e^{r/n}$ exactly at every $n$; they differ by
$O(1/n)$ and have the same limit. The transferable move: any coin of the
form $\tfrac12 + c/\sqrt n$ gives the scaled walk a limiting drift of
$2c\sigma$ per unit time and leaves its variance rate at $\sigma^2$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The central limit theorem with numbers attached. Simulate $10{,}000$
scaled walks at $n = 10$ and at $n = 100$, record $W^{(n)}(1)$ for each,
and estimate the skewness $E[Z^3]/(\mathrm{Var}\,Z)^{3/2}$ and the excess
kurtosis $E[Z^4]/(\mathrm{Var}\,Z)^2 - 3$ of the sample. Before running,
derive the exact values of both for $W^{(n)}(1) = M_n/\sqrt n$, and state
what sampling error a $10{,}000$-draw estimate carries, so that you can
say which departures from normality the simulation can detect and which
it cannot.

<Solution>
**Exact skewness.** $M_n$ and $-M_n$ have the same distribution (swap
heads and tails), so every odd moment is zero: skewness is exactly $0$
at every $n$. A sample estimate deviates from $0$ only by sampling error.

**Exact kurtosis.** Expand $M_n^4 = \bigl(\sum_j X_j\bigr)^4$ as a sum
over ordered quadruples $(a, b, c, d)$ of indices of
$X_a X_b X_c X_d$. A quadruple with any index appearing an odd number of
times has mean zero by independence and $E X_j = 0$. What remains: all
four equal, $n$ quadruples, each with mean $E[X_j^4] = 1$; and two
distinct indices each appearing twice, $3n(n - 1)$ quadruples (three ways
to pair four positions, $n(n - 1)$ ordered choices of the two values),
each with mean $E[X_a^2]E[X_b^2] = 1$. So
$E[M_n^4] = n + 3n(n - 1) = 3n^2 - 2n$, and with $\mathrm{Var}(M_n) = n$,
$$
\text{excess kurtosis of } W^{(n)}(1) = \frac{3n^2 - 2n}{n^2} - 3 = -\frac 2n :
$$
$-0.2$ at $n = 10$, $-0.02$ at $n = 100$, and $0$ in the limit. The
binomial is *platykurtic*: lighter-tailed than the normal, with the
deficit shrinking like $1/n$, which is the same flatness the histogram
task read off at $n = 100$.

**Sampling error.** For $N$ independent draws from a distribution close
to normal, the standard error of the sample skewness is about
$\sqrt{6/N} = 0.024$ and of the sample excess kurtosis about
$\sqrt{24/N} = 0.049$ at $N = 10{,}000$. Agreement means: within two
standard errors of the exact value.

**What one run gives.** With a fixed seed, $n = 10$: skewness $0.03$,
excess kurtosis $-0.16$; $n = 100$: skewness $0.00$, excess kurtosis
$+0.05$. Both skewness estimates are within two standard errors of $0$.
The $n = 10$ kurtosis is within one standard error of $-0.2$ and four
standard errors from $0$: the simulation detects the departure from
normality. The $n = 100$ kurtosis is within two standard errors of both
$-0.02$ and $0$: at this $n$ the departure is below the resolution of
$10{,}000$ draws, which is what "converges in distribution" looks like in
a sample. The transferable move: to report that a simulation confirms a
limit, state the exact finite-$n$ value, the standard error, and which of
the two the data can distinguish.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take one simulated path of $W^{(1000)}$ on $[0, 1]$. Compute its
first-order variation $\sum_i |W(t_{i+1}) - W(t_i)|$ and its quadratic
variation $\sum_i (W(t_{i+1}) - W(t_i))^2$ over the partitions of mesh
$\tfrac{1}{10}$, $\tfrac{1}{100}$ and $\tfrac{1}{1000}$. Before
simulating, compute the expected first-order variation at each mesh
exactly, and state how the two sums behave as the mesh is refined.

<Solution>
**Expected first-order variation.** At mesh $1/m$ with $m$ dividing
$1000$, each cell contains $k = 1000/m$ steps of size $1/\sqrt{1000}$,
so a cell's increment is $M_k/\sqrt{1000}$ for a fresh walk $M$ of $k$
steps, and the expected sum is $m\,E|M_k|/\sqrt{1000}$. For even $k$,
$E|M_k| = k\binom{k}{k/2}2^{-k}$. Mesh $\tfrac1{10}$: $k = 100$,
$E|M_{100}| = 100 \times 0.0796 = 7.96$, expected sum
$10 \times 7.96/31.62 = 2.52$. Mesh $\tfrac1{100}$: $k = 10$,
$E|M_{10}| = 10 \times 252/1024 = 2.46$, expected sum
$100 \times 2.46/31.62 = 7.78$. Mesh $\tfrac1{1000}$: every increment
is exactly $\pm 1/\sqrt{1000}$, so the sum is $1000/\sqrt{1000} = 31.62$
on every path, no expectation needed. Each tenfold refinement multiplies
the length by about $\sqrt{10}$: the normal approximation gives
$E|N(0, 1/m)| = \sqrt{2/(\pi m)}$ per cell and $\sqrt{2m/\pi}$ in total,
growing like $\sqrt m$ without bound.

**One path.** With a fixed seed: first-order variation $2.53$, $8.73$,
$31.62$ at the three meshes; quadratic variation $0.71$, $1.13$,
$1.000$. The quadratic sum fluctuates around $1$ at the coarse meshes
(each cell's squared increment has mean $1/m$ but is random) and pins to
exactly $1$ at the walk's own mesh. The first-order sum does not settle
anywhere: it grows with every refinement.

**What this is the doorstep of.** The limit process inherits both
behaviours: its quadratic variation over $[0, T]$ is $T$, and its
first-order variation over any interval is infinite, so a Brownian path
has no length and no derivative. <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">The next
lesson</Ref> proves both from the definition of Brownian motion, and
<Ref lesson="ito-integral">the lesson after it</Ref> builds an integral
against that path anyway, using the finite quadratic variation in place
of the infinite length.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

The actual measure $P$ and the risk-neutral measure $\tilde P$ disagree about
probabilities but agree about which events are impossible, and one random
variable $Z = \tilde P/P$ carries the entire disagreement — price with
$\tilde E$, or price with $E[Z \cdot{}]$: same number.

This is the eighth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the fourth move of its second module. <Ref
lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The previous lesson</Ref> assembled
the replication constants into the measure $\tilde P$, proved that every
discounted price is a $\tilde P$-martingale, and priced every claim as
$V_n = \tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^{N - n}]$; what it said about $\tilde P$ in
relation to the actual coin $P$ was only that the two agree on which paths
are possible. This lesson makes the relation an object: the ratio
$Z(\omega) = \tilde P(\omega)/P(\omega)$ on every path, with $E[Z] = 1$; the
translation $\tilde E[Y] = E[ZY]$ and its conditional form; the process
$Z_n = E_n[Z]$, a $P$-martingale that grows by a fixed factor per toss; and
the state-price density $\zeta_n = Z_n/(1 + r)^n$, which prices a dollar
delivered in one leaf and turns the risk-neutral formula into an expectation
under the actual coin. The machinery is the discrete original of which <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</Ref> is the continuous copy;
<Ref lesson="crr-arbitrage-trade">the next lesson</Ref> closes the module by
cashing the module's results on the trade in the 1979 paper that made the
binomial model standard.

The route: the eight ratios on the \$4 tree and the three properties they
satisfy; the translation identity with the \$5 call priced twice; the
Radon–Nikodym process and the conditional translation lemma; state prices
and what they say about insurance; and the exact shape that survives the
passage to continuous time.

## Z = P̃/P: eight ratios carry the whole disagreement

The pricing formula of the previous lesson takes an average with the
weights $\tilde P(\omega)$. Any average with one set of weights is an average
with another set of weights of a rescaled variable, and the rescaling
factor, path by path, is the object this section computes.

### The eight ratios

The eight paths of the three-period tree carry two sets of weights. Under
the actual coin, $P(\omega) = p^{\#H}q^{\#T}$ with $p = \tfrac23$: $\tfrac{8}{27}$
for $HHH$, $\tfrac{4}{27}$ for each path with two heads, $\tfrac{2}{27}$ for
each path with one, $\tfrac{1}{27}$ for $TTT$. Under the risk-neutral coin
every path weighs $\tilde p^3 = \tfrac18$. Dividing the second weight by the
first, path by path,
$$
Z(HHH) = \frac{1/8}{8/27} = \frac{27}{64}, \quad
Z(HHT) = Z(HTH) = Z(THH) = \frac{27}{32}, \quad
Z(HTT) = Z(THT) = Z(TTH) = \frac{27}{16}, \quad
Z(TTT) = \frac{27}{8}.
$$
In decimals: $0.42$, $0.84$, $1.69$, $3.38$. The ratio is below one on the
paths the actual coin favours and above one on the paths it does not, and it
runs from $0.42$ to $3.38$, a factor of eight, which is the ratio of the
heaviest actual weight to the lightest.

The ratio factorises toss by toss. Each head contributes
$\tilde p/p = \tfrac{1/2}{2/3} = \tfrac34$ and each tail
$\tilde q/q = \tfrac{1/2}{1/3} = \tfrac32$, so
$$
Z(\omega) = \Bigl(\frac{\tilde p}{p}\Bigr)^{\#H(\omega)}\Bigl(\frac{\tilde q}{q}\Bigr)^{\#T(\omega)},
\qquad Z(HTT) = \tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac32 = \tfrac{27}{16}.
$$
Two numbers, $\tfrac34$ and $\tfrac32$, generate all eight values; a
$20$-period tree has $2^{20}$ paths and the same two numbers.

### The definition and its boundary

<Definition title="Radon–Nikodym derivative on a finite sample space (Shreve I, Definition 3.2.4)">
Let $P$ and $\tilde P$ be two probability measures on a finite sample space
$\Omega$, each assigning positive probability to every element of $\Omega$.
The **Radon–Nikodym derivative of $\tilde P$ with respect to $P$** is the
random variable
$$
Z(\omega) = \frac{\tilde P(\omega)}{P(\omega)}, \qquad \omega \in \Omega .
$$
Two measures that assign positive probability to exactly the same
outcomes are called **equivalent**.
</Definition>

On a finite space the derivative is a quotient, and the name is inherited
from the general theorem that this lesson's starred section states. The
requirement that both measures be positive everywhere is the equivalence
clause, and it is what makes the quotient exist. Four cases fix the
boundary.

- **The canonical case.** The \$4 tree with $p = \tfrac23$ and
  $\tilde p = \tfrac12$: the eight values above.
- **A case with a different surface.** The same tree, the same $\tilde P$,
  but an actual coin with $p = 0.6$. Then $Z(TTT) = \tfrac{1/8}{0.064} = 1.95$
  in place of $3.38$, and every other value changes too. The risk-neutral
  measure is fixed by $u$, $d$ and $r$ alone; the derivative is not, because
  it is a ratio and the denominator is the actual coin. $Z$ is a property of
  the pair of measures, never of $\tilde P$ by itself.
- **The degenerate case.** $p = \tilde p$, which on the \$4 tree means
  $p = \tfrac12$. Then $Z \equiv 1$, and $E[ZY] = E[Y]$ for every $Y$: the
  actual coin already makes the discounted stock a martingale, the stock
  carries no risk premium, and risk-neutral pricing is actual-expectation
  pricing. <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">The coin-toss lesson's</Ref>
  gap between the discounted expected payoff $1.60$ and the price $1.20$ of
  the one-period call is the gap between $p = \tfrac23$ and $\tfrac12$, and
  it closes exactly when $Z$ is constant.
- **The near miss.** Take $Z'$ equal to $\tfrac87 Z$ on the seven paths
  other than $TTT$ and $0$ on $TTT$. Then $Z' \ge 0$ and
  $E[Z'] = \tfrac87 \cdot \tfrac78 = 1$, so $P'(\omega) = Z'(\omega)P(\omega)$
  is a probability measure, assigning $\tfrac17$ to seven paths and $0$ to
  the eighth. The clause it violates is positivity: $P'$ and $P$ are not
  equivalent. The consequence is not cosmetic. A claim paying $1$ on $TTT$
  has $E[Z' \cdot \mathbb 1_{TTT}] = 0$, so a formula using $P'$ prices it at
  zero, and a buyer who pays nothing collects a dollar with actual
  probability $\tfrac{1}{27}$: the "price" is a free lottery ticket, which is
  to say an arbitrage.

### Three properties, proved as finite sums

<Theorem title="the Radon–Nikodym derivative on a finite space (Shreve I, Theorem 3.1.1)">
Let $P$ and $\tilde P$ be probability measures on a finite sample space
$\Omega$ with $P(\omega) > 0$ and $\tilde P(\omega) > 0$ for every $\omega$,
and let $Z = \tilde P/P$. Then (i) $Z > 0$ on every outcome; (ii) $E[Z] = 1$;
(iii) for every random variable $Y$ on $\Omega$,
$$
\tilde E[Y] = E[ZY].
$$

<Proof>
Roadmap: each property is one sum over $\Omega$, and in each the product
$Z(\omega)P(\omega)$ under the sum is replaced by $\tilde P(\omega)$, which
turns a $P$-average of $Z$ times a variable into a $\tilde P$-average of the
variable.

**(i)** $Z(\omega)$ is a quotient of two positive numbers.

**(ii)** $E[Z] = \sum_{\omega} Z(\omega)P(\omega) = \sum_{\omega} \frac{\tilde P(\omega)}{P(\omega)}P(\omega) = \sum_{\omega} \tilde P(\omega) = 1$,
the last equality because $\tilde P$ is a probability measure.

**(iii)** $E[ZY] = \sum_{\omega} Z(\omega)Y(\omega)P(\omega) = \sum_{\omega} Y(\omega)\tilde P(\omega) = \tilde E[Y]$,
by the same substitution with $Y(\omega)$ carried along.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two checks on the proof. Property (ii) is (iii) at $Y \equiv 1$, so the
theorem has one substantive line, and it is the substitution. The place
where positivity of $P$ is used is the existence of the quotient; the place
where positivity of $\tilde P$ is used is (i), and (i) is what the near miss
above lacked. On the binomial tree both positivities are supplied by the
admissibility inequalities $0 < d < 1 + r < u$ of <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period lesson</Ref>, which force
$0 < \tilde p < 1$, together with the standing assumption $0 < p < 1$ on the
actual coin: equivalence of $P$ and $\tilde P$ is not an extra hypothesis
of the model but a consequence of the one it already has.

<KeyFact>
A change of measure on the tree is one positive random variable with mean
one, and equivalence is the statement that it is positive everywhere. Every
formula of this lesson, and every formula of <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</Ref>, is a consequence of
$\tilde P(\omega) = Z(\omega)P(\omega)$ and nothing else.
</KeyFact>

## Translation: the risk-neutral average is an actual average of Z·Y

Property (iii) is the mechanical core of the lesson. Term by term, the
risk-neutral expectation of $Y$ is a sum of $Y$ against the new weights; substituting the definition
of $Z$ turns each new weight into the old weight times a factor, and the
factor can be read as part of the variable instead of part of the measure.

### The \$5 call, priced twice

<Example title="two accountings of 2.304">
The three-period \$5 call on the \$4 tree pays $V_3 = 27$ on $HHH$, $3$ on
each of $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$, and $0$ on the four paths ending at $2$ or
$0.5$. <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The pricing
formula</Ref> gives
$$
V_0 = \tilde E\Bigl[\frac{V_3}{1.25^3}\Bigr] = 0.512 \times \bigl(\tfrac18 \cdot 27 + 3 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot 3\bigr) = 0.512 \times 4.5 = 2.304 .
$$
The same price, computed under the actual coin with $Z$ carried along:

| $\omega$ | $V_3$ | $P(\omega)$ | $Z(\omega)$ | $Z \cdot P$ | $V_3 \cdot Z \cdot P$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $HHH$ | $27$ | $8/27$ | $27/64$ | $1/8$ | $3.375$ |
| $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$ | $3$ each | $4/27$ | $27/32$ | $1/8$ | $0.375$ each |
| $HTT$, $THT$, $TTH$ | $0$ | $2/27$ | $27/16$ | $1/8$ | $0$ |
| $TTT$ | $0$ | $1/27$ | $27/8$ | $1/8$ | $0$ |

The last column sums to $3.375 + 3 \times 0.375 = 4.5$, and
$$
V_0 = E\Bigl[\frac{Z\,V_3}{1.25^3}\Bigr] = 0.512 \times 4.5 = 2.304 .
$$
The two computations are the same eight products grouped differently: in
the first the factor $\tfrac18$ is a probability, in the second it is
$Z(\omega)P(\omega)$ with the two factors written out. Nothing about
$\tilde P$ is used in the second beyond the values of $Z$; the actual coin
and the eight ratios suffice.
</Example>

The second accounting does not compute the discounted expected payoff
under the actual coin, which would be
$0.512 \times (\tfrac{8}{27} \cdot 27 + 3 \cdot \tfrac{4}{27} \cdot 3) = 0.512 \times \tfrac{252}{27} = 4.78$,
more than twice the price. It computes the expected payoff of the
re-weighted claim $Z V_3$, and $Z$ is below one exactly on the paths where
the call pays. The factor $Z$ is where the whole difference between
expectation and price lives.

<Remark title="notation ledger: three symbols, one measure change">
$Z$, spoken "the Radon–Nikodym derivative of $\tilde P$ with respect to
$P$", is a random variable on $\Omega$, positive with $P$-mean one; it
converts $P$-averages into $\tilde P$-averages by multiplication. $Z_n$,
defined in the next section, is its estimate given $n$ tosses. $\zeta_n$,
defined after that, is $Z_n$ discounted. The common error is to write
$\tilde E[Y] = E[Y]/Z$ or $E[Y/Z]$: the factor multiplies, and it multiplies
inside the expectation. The direction is also fixed: $Z = \tilde P/P$ takes
$P$ to $\tilde P$, and $1/Z$ takes $\tilde P$ back, since
$\tilde E[Y/Z] = E[Z \cdot Y/Z] = E[Y]$.
</Remark>

## The Radon–Nikodym process Zₙ = Eₙ[Z]: a P-martingale built by multiplication

The translation identity handles expectations at time zero. The pricing
formula needs conditional expectations at every node, and translating those
requires knowing how much of the measure change has happened by time $n$.
The object that records it is the conditional expectation of $Z$ given the
first $n$ tosses.

### Node by node

Start from the leaves, where $Z_3 = Z$, and average backward with the
actual coin. At $HH$, one toss remains:
$Z_2(HH) = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{27}{64} + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac{27}{32} = \tfrac{18}{64} + \tfrac{18}{64} = \tfrac{9}{16}$.
At $HT$ and $TH$: $\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{27}{32} + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac{27}{16} = \tfrac98$.
At $TT$: $\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{27}{16} + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac{27}{8} = \tfrac94$.
One date earlier, $Z_1(H) = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{9}{16} + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac98 = \tfrac{6}{16} + \tfrac{6}{16} = \tfrac34$
and $Z_1(T) = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac98 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac94 = \tfrac32$; at the root,
$Z_0 = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac32 = 1$, which is $E[Z]$ as
the theorem requires.

The values at the nodes are not new numbers. $Z_1(H) = \tfrac34 = \tilde p/p$,
$Z_2(HT) = \tfrac98 = \tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac32$, and in general $Z_n$ at a node
is the product of the per-toss factors along the path to it, which is the
ratio of the block probabilities $\tilde P(A)/P(A)$ for the block
$A = [\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$: at $H$, $\tfrac{1/2}{2/3} = \tfrac34$. The
second closing exercise proves this in general. Along any path the
process therefore moves by multiplication, $\tfrac34$ on every head and
$\tfrac32$ on every tail, and the two factors have actual-coin mean
$\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac32 = 1$, which is why averaging
the children returns the parent.

### The theorem: any estimated random variable is a martingale

<Theorem title="the Radon–Nikodym derivative process is a P-martingale (Shreve I, Theorem 3.2.1 and Definition 3.2.4)">
Let $Z$ be any random variable on the $N$-toss space and define
$Z_n = E_n[Z]$ for $n = 0, \ldots, N$. Then $Z_0, \ldots, Z_N$ is a martingale
under $P$. In particular, for $Z = \tilde P/P$ the process
$Z_n = E_n[Z]$, the **Radon–Nikodym derivative process**, is a
$P$-martingale with $Z_0 = 1$ and $Z_N = Z$.

<Proof>
By the tower property of <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">conditional
expectation</Ref>, for $n \le N - 1$,
$E_n[Z_{n+1}] = E_n\bigl[E_{n+1}[Z]\bigr] = E_n[Z] = Z_n$. The process is
adapted because $E_n[Z]$ is constant on each block of $\mathcal F_n$. For
the Radon–Nikodym derivative, $Z_0 = E_0[Z] = E[Z] = 1$ by property (ii) of
the previous theorem, and $Z_N = E_N[Z] = Z$ because nothing is unrevealed
at time $N$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The theorem is <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the second
boundary case of the martingale definition</Ref> with $Z$ in the role of
the estimated variable, and its proof is the same line. What is specific to
the Radon–Nikodym derivative is the multiplicative structure, which the
theorem does not state: on the binomial tree, with the actual coin
independent across tosses,
$$
Z_{n+1} = Z_n \cdot \frac{\tilde p}{p}\,\mathbb 1_{\{\omega_{n+1} = H\}} + Z_n \cdot \frac{\tilde q}{q}\,\mathbb 1_{\{\omega_{n+1} = T\}},
$$
a fixed random factor per toss, of mean one, applied to the running
product. A positive martingale that moves by multiplying with independent
mean-one factors is the discrete form of an exponential martingale, and
the last section returns to it.

### The conditional translation lemma

<Theorem title="conditional translation (Shreve I, Lemma 3.2.6, the case m = N)">
Under the conditions of the definition, for every random variable $Y$ on
the $N$-toss space and every $n = 0, \ldots, N$,
$$
\tilde E_n[Y] = \frac{1}{Z_n}\,E_n[Z\,Y] .
$$

<Proof>
Roadmap: write both sides at a fixed node as sums over continuations;
factor $Z$ along the path into the part already realised and the part
still to come; recognise the realised part as $Z_n$.

Fix a node $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$. By the definition of $\tilde E_n$,
$$
\tilde E_n[Y](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} \tilde p^{\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}\,\tilde q^{\#T(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}\,Y(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N).
$$
By the per-toss factorisation, $Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)$ is the product
of the factors over the first $n$ tosses, which is $Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$,
and the factors over the remaining tosses, which is
$(\tilde p/p)^{\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}(\tilde q/q)^{\#T(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}$.
Hence each risk-neutral continuation weight can be written
$$
\tilde p^{\#H}\tilde q^{\#T} = p^{\#H}q^{\#T} \cdot \frac{Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)}{Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)},
$$
with $\#H$ and $\#T$ counting the continuation only. Substituting, and
taking the constant $1/Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$ outside the sum,
$$
\tilde E_n[Y](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \frac{1}{Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} p^{\#H}q^{\#T}\,Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)\,Y(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)
= \frac{1}{Z_n}\,E_n[ZY](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n),
$$
the remaining sum being the definition of $E_n[ZY]$ at the node.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two checks on the proof. The step that uses the binomial structure is the
factorisation of $Z$ into a realised part and a future part; on a general
finite space the same lemma holds with $Z_n = E_n[Z]$ defined as a block
average, and the proof runs through block probabilities instead of per-toss
factors. The case $n = 0$ is property (iii), since $Z_0 = 1$; the case
$n = N$ reads $Y = ZY/Z$, which is true. Between those ends the division by
$Z_n$ removes the part of the measure change already spent on the path
to the node, leaving only the change over the tosses still to come.

= 5.0625 + 0.5625 + 0.5625 + 0 = 6.1875 .
$$
Dividing by $Z_1(H) = \tfrac34$ gives $8.25$, and dividing by $1.25^2$ gives
$5.28$. The number $8.25$ is $\tilde E_1[V_3](H) = \tfrac14(27 + 3 + 3 + 0)$,
reached without a single risk-neutral weight.
</Example>

## State prices: the price of a dollar delivered in one leaf

The conditional lemma applied to the pricing formula gives a third form of
the price, and reading its ingredients gives the one place in the course
where the actual coin enters a price: not as a forecast, but as the
denominator of a ratio.

### From the pricing formula to the state-price density

<Definition title="state-price density and state price (Shreve I, Definition 3.1.3 and Theorem 3.2.7)">
In an $N$-period binomial model with actual measure $P$ and risk-neutral
measure $\tilde P$, let $Z_n$ be the Radon–Nikodym derivative process. The
**state-price density process** is
$$
\zeta_n = \frac{Z_n}{(1 + r)^n}, \qquad n = 0, \ldots, N,
$$
and for a path $\omega$ the number $\zeta_N(\omega)P(\omega)$ is the
**state price** of $\omega$.
</Definition>

<Theorem title="three forms of the price (Shreve I, Theorem 3.2.7)">
For a derivative security paying $V_N$ at time $N$, and every
$n = 0, \ldots, N$,
$$
V_n = \tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{V_N}{(1 + r)^{N - n}}\Bigr]
= \frac{1}{Z_n}\,E_n\Bigl[\frac{Z\,V_N}{(1 + r)^{N - n}}\Bigr]
= \frac{1}{\zeta_n}\,E_n[\zeta_N V_N] .
$$

<Proof>
The first equality is <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the
risk-neutral pricing formula</Ref>. The second is the conditional
translation lemma applied to $Y = V_N/(1 + r)^{N - n}$, the constant
discount passing through $E_n$ by linearity. The third multiplies numerator
and denominator by $(1 + r)^{-n}$: $Z_n/(1 + r)^n = \zeta_n$ and
$Z/(1 + r)^N = \zeta_N$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

At time zero the third form reads
$V_0 = E[\zeta_N V_N] = \sum_{\omega} V_N(\omega)\,\zeta_N(\omega)P(\omega)$:
the price is the payoff in each leaf times that leaf's state price, summed.
The name is earned by the simplest claim there is. A *digital* on $\omega$
pays $1$ if the tosses come out $\omega$ and $0$ otherwise; the sum has one
term, and its price is $\zeta_N(\omega)P(\omega) = \tilde P(\omega)/(1 + r)^N$.
On the \$4 tree that is $\tfrac18 \times 0.512 = 0.064$ for every leaf: a
dollar delivered in any one of the eight states costs $6.4$ cents today,
and any claim is a portfolio of digitals, priced by adding.

### Reading the state prices against the odds

The state price has three factors, and each answers a different question.
The discount $1/(1 + r)^N = 0.512$ is the time value of money. The actual
probability $P(\omega)$ is how often the leaf occurs. The remaining factor
$Z(\omega)$ is everything else, and on the \$4 tree it runs from $0.42$ on
$HHH$ to $3.38$ on $TTT$.

<Task>
Read the pair for $TTT$: discounted probability
$\tfrac{1}{27} \times 0.512 = 0.019$, state price $0.064$, ratio

Ranked by the actual odds, a dollar in $HHH$ should cost eight times a
dollar in $TTT$; the market charges the same for both. Under $p = \tfrac23$
the stock's expected growth is $\tfrac23 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1.5$
per period against the money market's $1.25$, so holding the stock is
rewarded on average, and the leaves where that reward fails to arrive are
the leaves where a dollar is worth most. A claim that pays in the down
states is insurance, and the state prices say that insurance costs more
than its odds: on this tree, $1.7$ times in the one-head leaves and $3.4$
times in the crash leaf. The factor $Z$ is the price of risk, in the
literal sense that it is the multiplier the market applies to a leaf's
probability before paying for a dollar there. The same multiplier, read
off real option prices rather than a tree, is the reason implied variance
sits persistently above realised variance, which <Ref
lesson="realized-implied-smile">the variance-risk-premium lesson</Ref>
measures.

<KeyFact>
$Z \equiv 1$ exactly when $p = \tilde p$, which on the tree is the equation
$pu + qd = 1 + r$: the stock's actual expected return is the riskless rate
and it carries no risk premium. Only then is every state price its
discounted odds. A premium in either direction pushes $Z$ above one on the
leaves where the premium fails to arrive and below one where it pays, and
the split is invisible from prices: a chain of calls on $S_N$ identifies
the state price of each terminal level, the sum of $\tilde P(\omega)/(1 + r)^N$
over the paths ending there, and nothing more, which is why <Ref
lesson="implied-density">the implied-density lesson</Ref> recovers
$\tilde P$ from a call-price curve and never $P$. Factoring a state price
into odds times $Z$ needs the actual coin, and no price quotes it.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Radon–Nikodym as a theorem

On the finite tree the derivative is a quotient and the construction is
the proof. In general the same two statements hold, one cheap and one
deep, and both are stated here so that <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">module 6</Ref> can cite them.

<Theorem title="a positive mean-one Z builds an equivalent measure (Shreve II, Theorem 1.6.1)">
Let $(\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$ be a probability space and $Z$ an almost
surely positive random variable with $E[Z] = 1$. Then
$\tilde P(A) = \int_A Z\,dP$ defines a probability measure on
$(\Omega, \mathcal F)$ equivalent to $P$, and for every random variable
$X \ge 0$, $\tilde E[X] = E[ZX]$ and $E[X] = \tilde E[X/Z]$.
</Theorem>

The proof is the finite one with sums replaced by integrals, plus a
monotone-convergence argument for countable additivity that the finite
case does not need. The converse, that every equivalent $\tilde P$ arises
this way, is the Radon–Nikodym theorem proper (Shreve II, Theorem 1.6.7):
for equivalent $P$ and $\tilde P$ there is an almost surely positive $Z$
with $E[Z] = 1$ and $\tilde P(A) = \int_A Z\,dP$ for all $A$. Its proof is
measure theory the course does not carry, and this is one of the fenced
facts the course's continuous half names without proving. On a finite
space it is a one-line quotient. The finite proofs of this lesson are the
honest half of a pair whose other half is cited.

## The discrete rehearsal: re-weight paths, never rewrite them

Every formula in this lesson has a continuous twin, and the structural
fact that survives the passage is the multiplicative ladder of the
Radon–Nikodym process. On the \$4 tree the ladder has an exact closed form,
and that form, not merely the rule of multiplying by a mean-one factor, is
what Girsanov's theorem states.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ladder-kernel.svg"
  alt="Left, the Radon–Nikodym process along the path T H T as a step function, 1 then 3/2 then 9/8 then 27/16, with the per-toss factor on each rise; right, a sample path of the exponential martingale Z of t equals exp of minus theta W of t minus one half theta squared t, fluctuating around the level one"
  caption="The discrete ladder and its limit shape: Z grows by a mean-one factor per toss on the tree and by a mean-one factor per dt in continuous time; module 6 takes the limit."
/>

### The exact shape of the ladder

Along any path, $\log Z_n = \#H \log\tfrac34 + \#T \log\tfrac32$. Writing
$M_n = \#H - \#T$ for the walk driven by the tosses and $B_n = M_n - \tfrac{n}{3}$
for the same walk with its actual-coin drift $p - q = \tfrac13$ per step
removed, so that $B_n$ is a $P$-martingale started at zero,
$$
\log Z_n = -\theta B_n - \kappa n, \qquad \theta = \tfrac12 \ln 2 \approx 0.347,
\quad \kappa = \ln\bigl(\tfrac23 \cdot 2^{-1/3} + \tfrac13 \cdot 2^{2/3}\bigr) \approx 0.057,
$$
an identity that holds exactly at every node, as substituting $\#H = (n + M_n)/2$
and $\#T = (n - M_n)/2$ verifies. Two features of this shape are the whole
content of the continuous theorem. The exponent is *linear in the driving
martingale*, with a negative coefficient because the actual coin favours
heads and the change of measure has to tilt against it. The drift term
$-\kappa n$ is exactly what makes $e^{-\theta B_n}$ a martingale again:
$E[e^{-\theta(B_{n+1} - B_n)}] = e^{\kappa}$, so the correction cancels
the growth that the convexity of the exponential adds. In continuous time,
where the driving martingale is Brownian motion $W$ and the per-step
variance is $dt$, the correction is $\tfrac12\theta^2 t$ and the twin reads
$$
Z(t) = \exp\bigl(-\theta W(t) - \tfrac12 \theta^2 t\bigr),
$$
with $\theta = (\alpha - r)/\sigma$ the excess return per unit of
volatility. On this tree $\kappa = 0.057$ against $\tfrac12\theta^2 = 0.060$,
and the gap closes as the step shrinks. The refinement that closes it holds
the \$4 tree's growth rates fixed and packs $1/h$ tosses into a unit of
time: $u = 2^{\sqrt h}$, $d = 1/u$, the money market growing by $1.25^h$
and the stock's actual expectation by $1.5^h$ per toss, so that
$\sigma = \ln 2$, $\alpha = \ln 1.5$ and $r = \ln 1.25$ in continuous
units and $h = 1$ is the tree of this lesson. Both $\kappa$ and
$\tfrac12\theta^2$ are then proportional to $h$, and per unit of time they
converge to the same number, $\tfrac12\bigl((\alpha - r)/\sigma\bigr)^2 = 0.035$:
from $0.057$ and $0.060$ at one toss, to $0.0356$ and $0.0357$ at sixteen.

  src="./figures/kappa-vs-theta.svg"
  alt="Two curves against the number of tosses per unit of time on a doubling axis from 1 to 256: the blue curve one half theta squared over h starting at 0.060 and the amber curve kappa over h starting at 0.057, both falling and merging onto a dashed line at 0.035 labelled as one half of the squared excess return per unit volatility"
  caption="The drift of log Z per unit of time as the tree is refined: κ and ½θ² start 0.003 apart on the one-toss tree and meet at Girsanov's ½θ² as the step shrinks."

### What the ladder cannot change

What the ladder cannot change is as important as what it can. The change
of measure multiplies weights; it does not move a single node, so $u$, $d$
and every price on every path are the same under $P$ and $\tilde P$. The
multiplicative factor tilts the frequency of heads from $\tfrac23$ to
$\tfrac12$, which is the tree's drift, and leaves the size of each step,
which is the tree's volatility, untouched. Girsanov's theorem is that
sentence for Brownian motion: an equivalent change of measure moves the
drift and cannot move the quadratic variation, because quadratic variation
is computed path by path and equivalent measures share their paths.

What is established: the Radon–Nikodym derivative $Z = \tilde P/P$ is a
positive random variable of $P$-mean one, on the \$4 tree the product of
$\tfrac34$ per head and $\tfrac32$ per tail; $\tilde E[Y] = E[ZY]$ for every
$Y$, and $\tilde E_n[Y] = E_n[ZY]/Z_n$ at every node, so that the \$5 call's
$2.304$ and the lookback's $1.376$ are actual-coin expectations of the
re-weighted payoff; $Z_n = E_n[Z]$ is a $P$-martingale that grows by a
mean-one factor per toss, equal at each node to the ratio of block
probabilities; the state-price density $\zeta_n = Z_n/(1 + r)^n$ gives
$V_n = E_n[\zeta_N V_N]/\zeta_n$, the state price $\zeta_N(\omega)P(\omega)$ is
the price of a dollar in leaf $\omega$, $6.4$ cents in every leaf of the
\$4 tree, and where the stock carries a risk premium the down leaves cost
more than their odds; and the ladder has the exact shape
$\exp(-\theta B_n - \kappa n)$ whose continuous twin is Girsanov's kernel.
The module's machinery is now complete: replication, the coin-toss space,
martingales, the pricing formula, and the change of measure as an object.
<Ref lesson="crr-arbitrage-trade">The next lesson</Ref> cashes it on the
trade in Cox, Ross and Rubinstein's 1979 paper: a call worth $34.065$
quoted at $36$, hedged tick by tick to a profit of $1.935$ on every one of
the eight paths.

the per-toss factors, as the block-ratio reading requires.

with $Z$: $0.36 \times \tfrac{25}{36} \times 11 / 1.5625 = 0.25 \times 11 / 1.5625 = 1.76$,
the multiperiod lesson's price. The transferable move: $Z(\omega)P(\omega)$
is $\tilde P(\omega)$ term by term, so the two accountings never differ and
the actual coin cancels out of the price.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Prove two statements the lesson used without proof. (a) For every $n$ and
every node, $Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \tilde P(A)/P(A)$ where
$A = [\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$ is the block of paths beginning with those

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Both follow from property (iii) applied to well-chosen
$Y$ and the tower and taking-out properties of <Ref

**(a)** Let $Y = \mathbb 1_A$, which depends only on the first $n$ tosses.
Then $\tilde E[Y] = \tilde P(A)$. On the other hand
$E[ZY] = E[E_n[ZY]] = E[Y E_n[Z]] = E[Y Z_n]$, using the tower property and
then taking out the time-$n$ quantity $Y$. Since $Z_n$ is constant on $A$,
$E[Y Z_n] = Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)P(A)$. Property (iii) equates the
two: $\tilde P(A) = Z_n(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)P(A)$, which is the claim.
(This is Shreve's Lemma 3.2.5 read at an indicator.)

**(b)** The same chain, run at a node. For $Y$ depending on the first $m$
tosses, $E_n[ZY] = E_n[E_m[ZY]] = E_n[Y E_m[Z]] = E_n[Z_m Y]$, by the tower
property with $n \le m$ and taking out $Y$, which is known at time $m$.
Substituting into the lemma proved in the body,
$\tilde E_n[Y] = E_n[ZY]/Z_n = E_n[Z_m Y]/Z_n$. The transferable move: the
full derivative $Z$ can always be replaced by its estimate at the latest
time the variable reads, because the tosses after that time average out
of $Z$ by the tower property.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Since $E[Z] = 1$ and $Z > 0$, the process $Z_n$ must
dip to $1$ or below somewhere on every path; otherwise the average could
not come out to one." Use the computed tree, name the property $Z_n$
actually has, and state what the constant-mean property does and does not
say about individual paths.

<Solution>
**The counterexample.** On the path $TTT$ the process reads
$Z_0 = 1$, $Z_1 = \tfrac32$, $Z_2 = \tfrac94$, $Z_3 = \tfrac{27}{8}$: it rises
at every toss and never returns to $1$. On $HHH$ it reads
$1, \tfrac34, \tfrac{9}{16}, \tfrac{27}{64}$, falling at every toss. Neither
path is balanced on its own.

**The property $Z_n$ has.** It is a $P$-martingale, so
$E[Z_n] = Z_0 = 1$ for every $n$: the *average over paths* at each date is
one. The average at date $3$ is
$\tfrac{8}{27} \cdot \tfrac{27}{64} + \tfrac{12}{27} \cdot \tfrac{27}{32} + \tfrac{6}{27} \cdot \tfrac{27}{16} + \tfrac{1}{27} \cdot \tfrac{27}{8} = 1$,
with the falling paths and the rising paths compensating across the
ensemble, not along any single path.

**The failing step.** The claim converts a statement about an expectation
into a statement about every realisation. A martingale has constant mean
and unconstrained individual paths; the discounted stock of <Ref
lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the previous lesson</Ref> is the
same shape, a $\tilde P$-martingale that rises on every head and falls on
every tail. What is true is weaker and still useful: since $Z_n > 0$ and
$E[Z_n] = 1$, on any date at least one path has $Z_n \le 1$ and at least one
has $Z_n \ge 1$, unless $Z_n \equiv 1$. The transferable move: "martingale"
constrains the cross-section at each date, never the time series along a
path.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show that the discounted stock $S_n/(1 + r)^n$ being a $\tilde P$-martingale
is equivalent to $\zeta_n S_n$ being a $P$-martingale, where
$\zeta_n = Z_n/(1 + r)^n$. Verify the $P$-martingale statement numerically
at the root of the \$4 tree, then state the corresponding fact for the
discounted wealth of a self-financing strategy.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** The conditional lemma in the form of the second
exercise, $\tilde E_n[Y] = E_n[Z_{n+1} Y]/Z_n$ for $Y$ known at time
$n + 1$, converts each one-step clause into the other.

**The equivalence.** Take $Y = S_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}$, which depends on the
first $n + 1$ tosses. Then
$$
E_n[\zeta_{n+1} S_{n+1}] = E_n\Bigl[Z_{n+1}\,\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr]
= Z_n\,\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr].
$$
If the discounted stock is a $\tilde P$-martingale the right side is
$Z_n S_n/(1 + r)^n = \zeta_n S_n$, so $\zeta S$ is a $P$-martingale.
Conversely, if $E_n[\zeta_{n+1} S_{n+1}] = \zeta_n S_n$, dividing the display
by $Z_n > 0$ gives the $\tilde P$-martingale clause for the discounted
stock. Positivity of $Z_n$ is used in the converse, and only there.

**At the root.** $\zeta_1 = Z_1/1.25$ is $0.6$ at $H$ and $1.2$ at $T$, and
$E[\zeta_1 S_1] = \tfrac23 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 8 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1.2 \cdot 2 = 3.2 + 0.8 = 4 = \zeta_0 S_0$.
Under the actual coin the stock's average is $6$, not $4$; the factor
$\zeta_1$, low on heads and high on tails, brings it back to the current
price.

**Wealth.** By <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the wealth
theorem</Ref> the discounted wealth $X_n/(1 + r)^n$ of any self-financing
strategy is a $\tilde P$-martingale, so by the same argument $\zeta_n X_n$ is
a $P$-martingale, and $X_0 = E[\zeta_N X_N]$: every self-financing strategy's
starting wealth is the state-price value of its terminal wealth. The
transferable move: "discounted, under $\tilde P$" and "multiplied by
$\zeta$, under $P$" are two homes for one identity, and the second is the
one that survives when there is no tree.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the one-period \$4 tree ($u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$), find
the head probability $\hat p$ under which the *undiscounted* stock is a
martingale, $\hat E[S_1] = S_0$, and its derivative $\hat Z = \hat P/P$ with
$p = \tfrac23$. Then use $\hat P$ without discounting to "price" the \$5 call
and the money market account, compare with the true prices $1.20$ and $1$,
and explain from the two results why the martingale measure the theory
needs is the one for *discounted* prices.

<Solution>
**$\hat p$.** $8\hat p + 2(1 - \hat p) = 4$ gives $\hat p = \tfrac13$, and
$\hat Z = \tfrac{1/3}{2/3} = \tfrac12$ on heads, $\tfrac{2/3}{1/3} = 2$ on
tails, with $E[\hat Z] = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = 1$ and
$\hat Z > 0$: a legitimate equivalent measure.

**The call.** $\hat E[(S_1 - 5)^+] = \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = 1.00$, against the
price $1.20$. This is the "underpriced at $1.00$" preset of <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period lesson's demo</Ref>, and the
counter-trade there collects $0.20$ compounded in both states.

**The money market.** One dollar lent today returns $1.25$ in both states,
and $\hat E[1.25] = 1.25$, against its price of $1$. The recipe misprices the
riskless asset itself by the factor $1 + r$, and it does so because it
treats a sure $1.25$ tomorrow as worth $1.25$ today.

**Why discounting.** A pricing measure must make *every* primary asset's
price a martingale in *one* unit of account. Measuring in dollars, $\hat P$
levels the stock but leaves the money market growing at $25\%$; no measure
can level both, because one dollar today and one dollar tomorrow are
different goods. Measuring in units of the money market account, the
account is the constant $1$, a martingale under every measure, and the
stock's discounted price is levelled by exactly one coin, $\tilde p = \tfrac12$.
The transferable move: "martingale" is a statement about a process in a
chosen unit, and the risk-neutral measure is the one attached to the
riskless asset as the unit; <Ref lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's
theorem</Ref> constructs it in continuous time with the same discounting
built in.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $Y$ depend only on toss $n + 1$, taking the value $y_H$ if it is a head
and $y_T$ if a tail. Compute $E_n\bigl[(Z_{n+1}/Z_n)\,Y\bigr]$ on the \$4
tree with $p = \tfrac23$ and compare it with $\tilde E_n[Y]$. Then write the
one-step factor $Z_{n+1}/Z_n$ in the form $e^{-\theta \Delta B - c}$ with
$\Delta B = B_{n+1} - B_n$ the centred step of the last section, and read off
$\theta$ and $c$.

<Solution>
**The computation.** $Z_{n+1}/Z_n$ is $\tfrac34$ on a head and $\tfrac32$ on
a tail, independent of the first $n$ tosses, so by the independence property
$$
E_n\Bigl[\frac{Z_{n+1}}{Z_n}\,Y\Bigr] = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac34\,y_H + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac32\,y_T = \tfrac12\,y_H + \tfrac12\,y_T = \tilde E_n[Y].
$$
One step of the measure change is one multiplication by a mean-one factor
that reads only the next toss; the whole change is the product of $N$ such
factors, which is the per-toss factorisation of $Z$.

**The exponential form.** The centred step is $\Delta B = \tfrac23$ on a
head and $-\tfrac43$ on a tail. With $\theta = \tfrac12 \ln 2$ and
$c = \kappa \approx 0.0566$: on a head,
$e^{-\theta \cdot 2/3 - \kappa} = 2^{-1/3}e^{-\kappa} = 0.794 \times 0.945 = 0.75$;
on a tail, $e^{\theta \cdot 4/3 - \kappa} = 2^{2/3}e^{-\kappa} = 1.587 \times 0.945 = 1.5$.
So the one-step factor is $e^{-\theta \Delta B - \kappa}$ exactly, with the
$\kappa$ chosen so that its $P$-mean is one.

**Where this lands.** The continuous version of this per-step factor is
$e^{-\theta\,dW - \frac12 \theta^2 dt}$, Girsanov's kernel: a change of
measure over an infinitesimal step is a multiplication by a mean-one
exponential of the Brownian increment, and the product over $[0, t]$ is
$\exp(-\theta W(t) - \tfrac12\theta^2 t)$. <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">The Girsanov lesson</Ref> proves that under the
measure this kernel builds, $W(t) + \theta t$ is a Brownian motion without
drift, which is the continuous form of "the coin's head frequency moves
from $\tfrac23$ to $\tfrac12$".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

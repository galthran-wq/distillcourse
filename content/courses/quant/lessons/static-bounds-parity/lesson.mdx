Before any model of how the stock moves, no-arbitrage alone pins a European
call between $\max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT})$ and $S_0$ — and ties calls to puts by
an exact identity that holds for any distribution whatsoever. This lesson
builds the one proof device that delivers every such statement, a payoff
table comparing two portfolios, and runs it four times: for the call's
bounds, for the put's, for put–call parity, and for the theorem that an
American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early.

This is the second move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs. <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">The previous lesson</Ref>
established the ground rule for payoffs that are lines in $S_T$: a forward is
manufactured from a loan and a share, so its price is $S_0 e^{rT}$ with no
forecast in it, and any other quote is an arbitrage. What stood open was every
payoff with a corner, the call's $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ above all, which no
buy-and-hold position in stock and bond reproduces. This lesson establishes
how much no-arbitrage says about such a payoff without any model of the
stock: an interval for its price, an exact relation between the call and the
put, and a theorem about when the right to exercise early is worth nothing.
What it cannot deliver is a number for the call itself, and
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the next lesson</Ref> adds the smallest
model that can.

The route: the dominance lemma and the table that proves it; the call's
corridor, with the arbitrage that punishes each edge, worked in dollars
before it is stated in symbols; put–call parity as the same table with an
equality in every column; the early-exercise asymmetry between calls and
puts; and the honest accounting of what an interval and a relation leave
undetermined.

## The dominance table: a portfolio that never pays less never costs less

Every result in this lesson is one instance of a single comparison, so the
comparison comes first, in general form, with its proof. It uses only the
definition of arbitrage from the last lesson and the market assumptions of
the forward-price theorem there.

### Retrieval: what the last lesson fixed

<Task>
Closed book, before reading on. (1) State the three clauses that make a
strategy an arbitrage. (2) Write the forward price of a non-dividend stock
and name the trade that punishes a dealer quoting above it. (3) Write the
payoff at $T$ of a European call and of a European put with strike $K$.
Then check your answers against the fold below.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) Zero cost now, $X_0 = 0$; no loss in any state,
$P(X_T < 0) = 0$; gain in some state, $P(X_T > 0) > 0$
(<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage" id="arbitrage-defined-zero-cost-no-loss-a-chance-of-gain">the
definition</Ref>). (2) $F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$; against a quote $K > F_0$, sell the
forward, borrow $S_0$, buy the share, deliver it at $T$ and keep
$K - S_0 e^{rT}$. (3) Call: $\max(S_T - K, 0)$. Put: $\max(K - S_T, 0)$.
</Aside>

The market assumptions carry over unchanged: the stock pays no dividends
before $T$, it can be bought, sold and sold short in any amount at $S_0$
without cost, and cash can be borrowed or lent in any amount at the
continuously compounded rate $r$, so that a *zero-coupon bond* paying $K$ at
$T$ costs $Ke^{-rT}$ now. The notation for the rest of the module: $c$ and
$p$ are the prices now of a European call and put with strike $K$ and expiry
$T$; $C$ and $P$ are the prices of their American counterparts, exercisable
at any time up to $T$; $S_T$ is the stock price at expiry, about which
nothing is assumed beyond $S_T \ge 0$.

### The lemma and its arbitrage

Take two portfolios of traded assets, A costing $a$ now and B costing $b$
now, and write $A_T$ and $B_T$ for their values at $T$, both functions of the
state of the world. Compare them column by column.

<Figure
  src="./figures/dominance-table.svg"
  alt="A two-row table with portfolios A and B as rows and cost-now, Sₜ ≤ K and Sₜ > K as columns; a brace under the expiry columns reads Aₜ ≥ Bₜ in every column, with an arrow to the conclusion a ≥ b and the arbitrage that enforces it"
  caption="The dominance table: if A's expiry entries are never below B's, A's cost is never below B's; otherwise buy A, sell B, and lend the difference."
/>

<Theorem title="dominance lemma">
Suppose $A_T \ge B_T$ in every state of the world. Then $a \ge b$. If
$A_T = B_T$ in every state, then $a = b$.

<Proof>
The proof mode is direct: assume the conclusion fails and exhibit the
arbitrage.

Suppose $a < b$. Buy A for $a$, sell B for $b$, and lend the difference
$b - a > 0$ at rate $r$. The position costs $a - b + (b - a) = 0$ now, so
clause 1 holds. At $T$ it is worth
$$
A_T - B_T + (b - a)\,e^{rT} \;\ge\; (b - a)\,e^{rT} \;>\; 0
$$
in every state, using the hypothesis $A_T - B_T \ge 0$. So the position never
loses (clause 2) and gains with probability one (clause 3). It is an
arbitrage, which contradicts the assumption that none exists. Hence
$a \ge b$.

If $A_T = B_T$ in every state, the hypothesis holds in both directions, so
$a \ge b$ and $b \ge a$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Task>
Before opening the fold: (1) state the idea of the proof in one sentence;
(2) name the step that uses the lending assumption, and say what survives
if no riskless asset exists; (3) name the step that uses the distribution
of $S_T$.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) A portfolio that is cheaper now and never worse later is free
money, so it cannot exist. (2) Lending parks the difference $b - a$
somewhere that returns at least $b - a$ at $T$; without a riskless asset
the argument still gives $a \ge b$ by holding the cash, and the strict
positivity at $T$ comes from the cash itself. (3) None. The distribution of
$S_T$ appears nowhere, because "in every state" is a statement about which
states are possible, not how likely they are.
</Aside>

The lemma's second sentence is the *law of one price*, and the forward
theorem of the last lesson was its first application: the forward and the
loan-plus-share have the same payoff, so they have the same cost, which is
zero when $K = S_0 e^{rT}$. The rest of this lesson fills the table's rows
with options and reads the inequalities off.

<KeyFact>
The dominance table never asks how likely a state is, only whether it is
possible; two traders who disagree about every probability of $S_T$ but
agree that both $S_T \le K$ and $S_T > K$ can happen agree about every bound
in this lesson.
</KeyFact>

## The call's corridor: between max(0, S₀ − Ke⁻ʳᵀ) and S₀

The table's first use is the lower bound in the opening sentence. The
computation comes first in dollars, with a quote that violates the bound and
the trade that punishes it; the theorem then names what was computed.

### The lower bound in dollars: a \$3 call that must cost \$3.71

<Example title="a call quoted below its floor">
A non-dividend stock trades at $S_0 = 20$; the one-year rate is $r = 10\%$;
a one-year European call with strike $K = 18$ is quoted at $c = 3.00$. The
bond paying $K = 18$ in one year costs $18e^{-0.10} = 16.29$ now, and
$S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 20 - 16.29 = 3.71$. The quote is $0.71$ below that
number.

Buy the call and short the share: cash in now is $20 - 3 = 17$, lent for one
year to become $17e^{0.10} = 18.79$ at $T$. Then the table, with the short
share and the loan folded into one row:

| | now | $S_T \le 18$ | $S_T > 18$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| long call at 3.00 | $-3$ | $0$ | $S_T - 18$ |
| short share, lend 17 at 10% | $+3$ | $18.79 - S_T$ | $18.79 - S_T$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $18.79 - S_T \ge 0.79$ | $+0.79$ |

If $S_T > 18$, exercise the call, pay $18$ for a share, return it to the
lender, and keep $18.79 - 18 = 0.79$. If $S_T \le 18$, abandon the call, buy
the share in the market for $S_T$, return it, and keep $18.79 - S_T$, which is
at least $0.79$ and larger the lower the stock falls. The worst state pays
$0.79 = (3.71 - 3.00)\,e^{0.10}$: the shortfall below the floor, with a
year's interest.
</Example>

Nothing in the trade depends on where the stock goes. The call was bought
for less than the cost of manufacturing something the call dominates, and
the difference is collected in every state.

### The theorem (Merton 1973, Theorem 1)

<Theorem title="lower bound for a European call (Merton 1973, Theorem 1)">
Under the market assumptions above, a European call on a non-dividend stock
satisfies
$$
c \;\ge\; \max\bigl(0,\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\bigr).
$$
No assumption on the distribution of $S_T$ is used.

<Proof>
The proof mode is the dominance table. Two portfolios:

- **A**: one call plus one zero-coupon bond paying $K$ at $T$. Cost now:
  $c + Ke^{-rT}$.
- **B**: one share. Cost now: $S_0$.

| | now | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: call + bond | $c + Ke^{-rT}$ | $0 + K = K$ | $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ |
| B: share | $S_0$ | $S_T$ | $S_T$ |

In the first expiry column $A_T = K \ge S_T = B_T$; in the second
$A_T = S_T = B_T$. So $A_T \ge B_T$ in every state, and the lemma gives
$c + Ke^{-rT} \ge S_0$, that is $c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. Separately $c \ge 0$,
because the call's payoff is never negative and a negative price would be
an arbitrage by itself: buy the call, receive cash now, never pay anything.
Together: $c \ge \max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT})$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof is Merton's own, from the 1973 paper that assembled every
model-free property of options into one place, in warrant notation: his
$f(S, \tau; E)$ is $c$, his exercise price $E$ is $K$, and his $E$ bonds at
$P(\tau)$ each are the bond paying $K$. His Assumption 1, that a rationally
priced option is neither a dominant nor a dominated security, is exactly the
dominance lemma, and his portfolios A and B are the rows above.
The bound in the Example is this theorem at $S_0 = 20$, $K = 18$: portfolio A
pays $\max(S_T, 18)$, the share pays $S_T$, and the quote of $3.00$ priced A
at $19.29$ against a share at $20$.

### The upper bound and the put's corridor

The upper bound needs a one-line table. A share pays $S_T$ at $T$; a call
pays $\max(S_T - K, 0) \le S_T$. So the share dominates the call and
$c \le S_0$: a call quoted above the share is sold, the share is bought, and
the trade never loses. Together the two bounds fence the call into a
corridor.

The corridor is old. The only figure in Black and Scholes's 1973 paper, the
one that derived the formula the fifth module reaches, draws exactly these
two edges: line A at $45°$ for the upper bound, line B kinked at the exercise
price for the lower, and three dotted curves between them for options of
successively shorter maturity. Their text states the two bounds in words
and then observes that the curve of an option's value normally lies below
the $45°$ line and is convex, which is as far as the paper goes before it
introduces a model of the stock.

The put's corridor is the mirror image, and its proof is the paired exercise
below. A put pays at most $K$, at time $T$, so it cannot be worth more than
the bond that pays $K$ at $T$ for certain: $p \le Ke^{-rT}$. And a put plus a
share pays $\max(S_T, K) \ge K$, so it dominates that bond:
$p + S_0 \ge Ke^{-rT}$. Together,
$$
\max\bigl(0,\; Ke^{-rT} - S_0\bigr) \;\le\; p \;\le\; Ke^{-rT}.
$$

<Exercise>
Prove both put bounds by constructing the two dominance tables yourself: for
the lower bound, rows "put + share" and "bond paying $K$"; for the upper
bound, rows "bond paying $K$" and "put". Then apply the lower bound to a
six-month European put with $K = 40$ on a stock at $S_0 = 37$ with
$r = 5\%$, quoted at $p = 1.00$, and write the arbitrage table in dollars.
Write both tables before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** The put's payoff is $\max(K - S_T, 0)$; adding a share
turns it into $\max(K, S_T)$, which is a bond's payoff or better.

**Lower bound.**

| | now | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C: put + share | $p + S_0$ | $(K - S_T) + S_T = K$ | $0 + S_T = S_T$ |
| D: bond paying $K$ | $Ke^{-rT}$ | $K$ | $K$ |

Column one: equal. Column two: $S_T > K$. So $C_T \ge D_T$ in every state and
$p + S_0 \ge Ke^{-rT}$. With $p \ge 0$ (a negative price is an arbitrage on
its own), $p \ge \max(0, Ke^{-rT} - S_0)$.

**Upper bound.**

| | now | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| D: bond paying $K$ | $Ke^{-rT}$ | $K$ | $K$ |
| put | $p$ | $K - S_T$ | $0$ |

The bond's entries are never smaller, so $Ke^{-rT} \ge p$.

**In dollars.** $Ke^{-rT} = 40e^{-0.025} = 39.01$, so the floor is
$39.01 - 37 = 2.01$, and $p = 1.00$ sits $1.01$ below it. Borrow $38$ for six
months, buy the put and the share; the loan repays $38e^{0.025} = 38.96$.

| | now | $S_T \le 40$ | $S_T > 40$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| long put at 1.00 | $-1$ | $40 - S_T$ | $0$ |
| long share | $-37$ | $S_T$ | $S_T$ |
| borrow 38 at 5% | $+38$ | $-38.96$ | $-38.96$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $+1.04$ | $S_T - 38.96 > 1.04$ |

Below the strike, exercise the put, sell the share for $40$, repay the loan,
keep $1.04$. Above it, abandon the put and sell the share for more. The
transferable move: a put is completed into a bond by adding the share, and
the bound is the bond's price.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Intrinsic value, redefined

The usual name for $\max(0, S_0 - K)$ is the call's *intrinsic value*, the
amount collected by exercising it now, and the usual statement is that a
call must sell for at least its intrinsic value. The theorem says more:
the floor is $\max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT})$, which exceeds $\max(0, S_0 - K)$
whenever $r > 0$ and $T > 0$, because the strike need not be paid until $T$
and $Ke^{-rT}$ is the present cost of paying it then. Merton's remark that
the discounted version "makes more sense" as the definition of intrinsic
value is not cosmetic: the gap between the two, $K(1 - e^{-rT})$, is the
quantity that decides the early-exercise question two sections on.

<Remark title="monotone and convex in the strike (Merton 1973, Theorems 4–5)">
The same table with three expiry columns, $S_T \le K_1$, $K_1 < S_T \le K_2$
and $S_T > K_2$, proves that for $K_1 < K_2$ the call price falls with the
strike but by no more than the discounted strike difference,
$0 \le c(K_1) - c(K_2) \le (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$ (Theorem 5), and that $c$ is
a convex function of $K$ (Theorem 4). The convexity is what
<Ref lesson="implied-density">the market-implied distribution</Ref> later
differentiates twice to read a probability density off a row of call quotes;
it is an arbitrage fact, proved by this table, before it is a fact about any
model. The strike-spread inequality is the derivation exercise at the end.
</Remark>

## Put–call parity: two portfolios, one payoff, one price

The bounds used the table with an inequality in one column and an equality
in the other. The identity in the opening sentence is the case with an
equality in both, and the lemma's second sentence then returns an equation.

### The identity

The two portfolios are the ones already built: portfolio C, a share plus a
put, from the put's lower bound, and portfolio A, a call plus a bond paying
$K$, from the call's. Each was shown to pay $\max(S_T, K)$.

<FigureSequence
  name="parity"
  alt="Three frames: two portfolio boxes, a share plus a put and a call plus a bond paying K, are assembled; their expiry payoffs are drawn on one axis and coincide as the hockey stick max(Sₜ, K); the cost equation S0 + p = c + Ke⁻ʳᵀ appears with each term coloured by its portfolio"
  caption="Put–call parity in three steps: assemble the two portfolios, watch their expiry payoffs coincide in every state, and equate their costs."
/>

<Task>
Step through the three frames. On frame 2, read the payoff of each
portfolio in each of the two states off the plot and confirm the two
hockey sticks coincide at every $S_T$, including at $S_T = K$ exactly. On
frame 3, identify which term of the cost equation belongs to which box.
</Task>

<Theorem title="put–call parity (Merton 1973, Theorem 12)">
For European options with the same strike $K$ and expiry $T$ on a
non-dividend stock, under the market assumptions above,
$$
c + Ke^{-rT} \;=\; p + S_0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
c - p \;=\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT}.
$$
No assumption on the distribution of $S_T$ is used.

<Proof>
| | now | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: call + bond paying $K$ | $c + Ke^{-rT}$ | $0 + K = K$ | $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ |
| C: put + share | $p + S_0$ | $(K - S_T) + S_T = K$ | $0 + S_T = S_T$ |

The expiry entries agree in both columns, so $A_T = C_T$ in every state. By
the second sentence of the dominance lemma the costs agree:
$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$. Both options are European, so neither portfolio
can be altered before $T$ by an exercise decision, and only the expiry
column matters.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The identity holds for every distribution of $S_T$, every volatility, every
belief. It is also the last time this lesson uses an equality: the bounds
fenced each option separately, and parity says the two fences are one
fence, viewed from the call's side and from the put's.

### The arbitrage in dollars

<Example title="a put and a call that disagree by $0.99">
Stock at $S_0 = 31$, three-month rate $r = 10\%$, strike $K = 30$; a
three-month European call is quoted at $c = 3.00$ and the matching put at
$p = 2.25$. The two sides of parity:
$$
c + Ke^{-rT} = 3 + 30e^{-0.025} = 3 + 29.26 = 32.26,
\qquad
p + S_0 = 2.25 + 31 = 33.25.
$$
Portfolio C is dear by $0.99$. Buy the cheap side and sell the dear side:
buy the call, sell the put, short the share. Cash in now:
$-3 + 2.25 + 31 = 30.25$, lent for three months to become
$30.25e^{0.025} = 31.02$.

| | now | $S_T \le 30$ | $S_T > 30$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| long call at 3.00 | $-3$ | $0$ | $S_T - 30$ |
| short put at 2.25 | $+2.25$ | $-(30 - S_T)$ | $0$ |
| short share, lend 30.25 | $+31$ | $31.02 - S_T$ | $31.02 - S_T$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $+1.02$ | $+1.02$ |

Below the strike the put is exercised against the position and a share is
bought for $30$; above it the call is exercised and a share is bought for
$30$. Either way one share arrives for $30$, closes the short, and
$31.02 - 30 = 1.02$ remains. The profit is the parity gap of $0.99$ with three
months' interest.
</Example>

$31 + 1 - 3 = 29$, borrowed at $10\%$ for three months, repaying
$29e^{0.025} = 29.73$.

| | now | $S_T \le 30$ | $S_T > 30$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| short call at 3.00 | $+3$ | $0$ | $-(S_T - 30)$ |
| long put at 1.00 | $-1$ | $30 - S_T$ | $0$ |

| **net** | $0$ | $+0.27$ | $+0.27$ |

Below the strike the put sells the share for $30$; above it the call is
exercised against the position and the share goes for $30$. Either way
$30 - 29.73 = 0.27$ remains. The transferable move: parity violations are

</Solution>

### What parity carries

Parity is the single most-used identity in the rest of the course, and its
uses are of three kinds. It transfers results: once the call's bounds are
known, the put's follow by substitution, since
$p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT} \le S_0 - S_0 + Ke^{-rT} = Ke^{-rT}$ reproduces the
put's upper bound from the call's, which is Merton's Corollary 1 to his
Theorem 12. It transfers prices: when
<Ref lesson="black-scholes-formula">the Black–Scholes formula</Ref> delivers
$c$, the put needs no second derivation, and when
<Ref lesson="implied-volatility">implied volatility</Ref> is backed out of
quotes, parity is why the call and the put at one strike must return the
same number. And it transfers structure: the long call and the short put at
the same strike, taken together, pay $S_T - K$, which is a forward's payoff,
and the review exercise at the end derives the forward price of the last
lesson from that observation.

<Aside>
Parity as a statement about a firm. A company financed by zero-coupon debt
with face value $K$ due in five years and by equity, paying no dividends,
has assets worth $A_T$ at the debt's maturity. If $A_T > K$ the shareholders
repay the debt and keep $A_T - K$; if $A_T \le K$ they default and the
bondholders take the assets. So equity is a call on the firm's assets with
strike $K$: it is worth $\max(A_T - K, 0)$. The bondholders receive
$\min(A_T, K) = K - \max(K - A_T, 0)$: a riskless bond less a put on the
assets with strike $K$. The firm's value is the sum of its claims,
$A_0 = c + [Ke^{-rT} - p]$, which rearranges to $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + A_0$: put–call
parity, with the firm's assets as the underlying. The credit-risk models
built on this reading are outside this course.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Parity is a relation between two unknowns, not a value for either: knowing
$c - p$ exactly leaves $c$ anywhere in its corridor, and the bounds and
parity together are still one interval and one line, not a point.
</KeyFact>

## Early exercise: the call is worth more alive, the put is not

Exchange-traded stock options are almost all American, and the results so
far are for European ones. The gap closes for calls, by a two-line
consequence of the lower bound, and stays open for puts, for a reason the
same inequality makes visible.

### The call: killing it burns the interest on the strike

An American call gives every right a European one does and more, so
$C \ge c$ by the lemma applied to the two options, and $C \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$
follows from the European bound. A holder deep in the money, with $S_0 = 70$
against $K = 40$ and a month to run, is commonly tempted to exercise and
bank the $30$. The temptation is the misconception this section refutes:
exercising early is never optimal for a call on a non-dividend stock, and
the reason is arithmetic, not a bet on the stock.

<Theorem title="no early exercise for calls (Merton 1973, Theorem 2)">
Under the market assumptions above, with $r > 0$ and $T > 0$, an American
call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised before expiry, and its
price equals the European call's: $C = c$.

<Proof>
At any time before expiry, with the stock at $S$ and time $\tau > 0$ left,
compare the call's two fates. Kept alive, it is worth at least
$\max(0, S - Ke^{-r\tau})$ by the lower bound applied at that moment.
Exercised, it pays $S - K$. Two cases, by whether exercise pays anything.

**Case $S > K$.** Since $r\tau > 0$, $Ke^{-r\tau} < K$, so
$$
S - Ke^{-r\tau} \;>\; S - K,
$$
and the alive floor strictly exceeds the exercise value. A holder who wants
out sells the call for at least its alive value instead of exercising it.

**Case $S \le K$.** Exercise pays $S - K \le 0$, at best nothing, so the right
goes unused; the call kept alive is worth at least $0$, which is all that
declining to exercise yields. For $S \le Ke^{-r\tau}$ the alive floor and the
value of the unused right are both $0$, so there is no strict gap there, and
none is needed: exercise offers nothing to give up.

In neither case does exercise beat holding, and wherever exercise would pay
anything it is strictly worse. So the exercise right is never used, and an
American call is a European call with an unused clause: $C = c$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The refutation in full. The correct claim: a deep-in-the-money call is sold,
not exercised. The misconception: exercising locks in a gain that waiting
might lose. Why it fails: exercise pays $K$ now for a share; holding the
call pays $K$ at expiry, so the holder keeps the interest on $K$ for the
remaining life, $K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, and additionally keeps the right to
walk away if the stock falls below $K$ before then. The replacement model
is the wedge in the intrinsic-value figure: for a call in the money, the
floor an alive call sits on lies strictly above the exercise value by
exactly that interest. The
discriminating case: at $S_0 = 70$, $K = 40$, $r = 10\%$, one month,
exercise pays $30.00$ and the alive floor is
$70 - 40e^{-0.10/12} = 30.33$; a buyer of the call pays at least $30.33$,
and the holder who wants the stock buys it a month later for $40$ having
kept the $40$ invested meanwhile.

<Remark title="the perpetual warrant paradox (Merton 1973, Theorem 3)">
Push $T \to \infty$ in the lower bound. A bond paying $K$ at infinity costs
nothing, so $c \ge S_0$; with $c \le S_0$ from the upper bound, a perpetual
call on a non-dividend stock is worth exactly the stock, whatever the strike
and whatever the distribution of returns. Merton records in a footnote that
the few perpetual warrants actually outstanding do not sell at the stock
price, and names the reason: the theorem assumes no payouts to the stock for
the life of the contract, and no real stock goes forever without them. A
theorem's hypotheses are the part that says where it applies.
</Remark>

### The put: the same inequality runs the other way

From the symmetry of the payoffs one would expect the put to behave the same
way, so that an American put on a non-dividend stock would also price as
European. It does not. The put's European floor is $Ke^{-rT} - S_0$ and its
exercise value is $K - S_0$; the same inequality $Ke^{-rT} < K$ now puts the
floor *below* the exercise value. For the call, the discounting of the
strike works for the holder who waits, because waiting defers a payment.
For the put, waiting defers a receipt, and the holder who exercises now
receives $K$ now instead of at most $K$ at $T$.

The extreme case settles it. Take $K = 10$ and a stock that has fallen to a
few cents. Exercising now collects nearly $10$, which can be invested at $r$;
waiting can never collect more than $10$, since the stock cannot fall below
zero, and collects it later. Immediate exercise is strictly better, and so
an American put is exercised early whenever the stock is low enough, at a
level $S^*$ that depends on $K$, $r$, the time left and the model of the
stock. Merton's Theorem 13 states the condition without a model: early
exercise of the put has positive probability whenever there is positive
probability that the call at the same strike will be worth less than the
interest on the strike, $c < K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, which by parity is exactly
the event $p < K - S$, a European put worth less than its own exercise
value.

Two consequences follow, and both are stated rather than proved here.
First, $P > p$ in general: the American put carries a genuine early-exercise
premium even without dividends, and no exact American parity exists, only
the inequalities $S_0 - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$, whose right-hand
side is European parity with $P \ge p$ substituted. Second, computing $P$
and $S^*$ requires a model of the stock and an optimisation over exercise
rules; that is the work of
<Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the American-options module</Ref>, where
the binomial version of the call theorem is proved again by conditional
Jensen's inequality, and the put's boundary is computed.

<KeyFact>
One inequality, $Ke^{-rT} < K$, drives both halves of the asymmetry: it
places the call's floor above its exercise value, so a call is never killed
early, and the put's floor below its exercise value, so a put sometimes is.
</KeyFact>

### The hypotheses are the theorem

Every result above assumed no dividends, and one substitution repairs all
of them when dividends are known in advance. Let $D$ be the present value of
the dividends paid over the option's life. A share bought now delivers the
dividends as well as $S_T$, so in every table the share's row is worth $D$
more than a claim on $S_T$ alone; replacing $S_0$ by $S_0 - D$ throughout
restores every bound and parity:
$$
c \ge \max\bigl(0,\; S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\bigr), \qquad
p \ge \max\bigl(0,\; D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0\bigr), \qquad
c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}.
$$
The one result the substitution does not save is the call theorem: with a
dividend ahead, the alive floor becomes $S - D - Ke^{-r\tau}$, which can fall
below $S - K$, and exercising immediately before an ex-dividend date can be
optimal. <Ref lesson="early-exercise-in-practice">Early exercise in real
markets</Ref> takes that case up with the tools of the third module.

## What arbitrage alone delivers: an interval and a relation, not a price

The module's opening claim was that a derivative's price is enforced, not
estimated. For the forward the enforcement was complete: one number. For
the call, everything above is enforced at the same standard, and the tally
of what has been enforced is honest about its shape.

### The tally: three results, no number

For the one-year call with $K = 18$ on the stock at $20$, no-arbitrage
delivers the corridor $3.71 \le c \le 20$, a band sixteen dollars wide, and
every price inside it is consistent with the absence of a static arbitrage
in stock, bond and call. Parity delivers $c - p = 3.71$ exactly, which pins
the put once the call is known and pins nothing before. The early-exercise
theorem delivers $C = c$, which says the American premium is zero without
saying what $c$ is. Three results, each proved for every distribution of
$S_T$, and no number for the call among them.

### Why the fence has width: a line cannot match a corner

The reason is the one the last lesson named: the table compares the call
with portfolios that dominate it or are dominated by it, and among static
portfolios of stock and bond none reproduces the corner in
$\max(S_T - K, 0)$. Dominance from above and from below can only fence a
payoff that nothing matches, and the corridor's two edges are the extreme
fences themselves. The upper edge is the share: among positions
$a$ shares plus a bond paying $b$, the cheapest whose payoff $aS_T + b$
is at least $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ for every $S_T \ge 0$ has $a = 1$, $b = 0$
(any $a < 1$ falls below the corner for large $S_T$, any $b < 0$ below it at
$S_T = 0$). The lower edge is a share less the bond paying $K$, the dearest
position whose payoff is at most the corner in every state. The width of
the fence is the gap between their costs, $S_0 - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) = Ke^{-rT}$
when the floor is positive: at $S_0 = 20$, the $16.29$ the bond costs.

<Task>
On the left panel, confirm that the line $S_T$ lies on or above the corner
at every $S_T \ge 0$ and the line $S_T - K$ on or below it, and read the
two costs off. On the right, check that $\tfrac12 S_T - 1$ passes through
both dots, then price it: half a share at $S_0 = 4$ less a bond paying $1$
when cash grows by $1.25$ over the period. The answer, $1.20$, is what the
last exercise derives from the lemma alone.
</Task>

What closes the corridor is a restriction on the states, not on the
probabilities. If $S_T$ can take only two values, one on each side of $K$,
a line through two points matches the corner exactly, the cheapest
dominating portfolio and the dearest dominated portfolio are the same
portfolio, and the corridor collapses to its cost. The forward-reaching
exercise at the end computes that collapse for the stock at $4$ that the
course's introduction promised, and finds $1.20$.
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">The next lesson</Ref> makes the two-state
restriction a model, names the matching portfolio's share count the delta,
and proves that the collapse is a theorem.

What is established: the dominance table turns "never pays less" into "never
costs less" by an explicit arbitrage; a European call lies in
$[\max(0, S_0 - Ke^{-rT}),\, S_0]$ and a European put in
$[\max(0, Ke^{-rT} - S_0),\, Ke^{-rT}]$; $c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$ exactly;
an American call on a non-dividend stock is a European call, and an American
put is not; and all of it holds for every distribution of $S_T$. What is not
established is a price for the call, because the fence around a corner has
width. The next lesson's two-state model is the smallest world in which it
has none.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A non-dividend stock trades at $S_0 = 31$; the three-month rate is
$r = 10\%$; a three-month European call with $K = 30$ is quoted at
$c = 0.75$. Decide which bound the quote violates and by how much, then
write the arbitrage table and the profit in each state.

<Solution>
The bond paying $30$ in three months costs $30e^{-0.025} = 29.26$, so the
lower bound is $\max(0, 31 - 29.26) = 1.74$. The quote is $0.99$ below the
floor; the upper bound $c \le 31$ is not in question.

Buy the call, short the share, lend the difference $31 - 0.75 = 30.25$ at
$10\%$ for three months: it becomes $30.25e^{0.025} = 31.02$.

| | now | $S_T \le 30$ | $S_T > 30$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| long call at 0.75 | $-0.75$ | $0$ | $S_T - 30$ |
| short share, lend 30.25 | $+0.75$ | $31.02 - S_T$ | $31.02 - S_T$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $31.02 - S_T \ge 1.02$ | $+1.02$ |

Above the strike, exercise, pay $30$, return the share, keep $1.02$. Below
it, buy the share for $S_T \le 30$ and keep at least $1.02$. The worst-state
profit is $(1.74 - 0.75)e^{0.025} = 1.02$, the shortfall with interest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "An American put on a non-dividend stock, like the
American call, should never be exercised early, because waiting always
preserves the option's remaining value." Break it with an explicit case,
then name the step of the call theorem's proof that has no counterpart for
the put, and state Merton's condition for early exercise in terms of the
put's own price.

<Solution>
**The case.** $K = 10$, $r = 5\%$, one year to run, and the stock at
$S = 0.05$. Exercising now collects $9.95$, which invested at $5\%$ is
$10.46$ at expiry. Waiting collects $\max(10 - S_T, 0) \le 10$ at expiry,
since $S_T \ge 0$, and $10 < 10.46$. Waiting is strictly worse in every
state, so the claim is false.

**The step.** The call theorem compared the alive floor
$S - Ke^{-r\tau}$ with the dead value $S - K$ and used $Ke^{-r\tau} < K$ to
conclude alive $>$ dead. The put's alive floor is $Ke^{-r\tau} - S$ and its
dead value is $K - S$; the same inequality $Ke^{-r\tau} < K$ now gives
floor $<$ dead. The comparison that made the call's proof work is exactly
the comparison that fails for the put: discounting the strike helps a
holder who will pay it and hurts a holder who will receive it. Waiting does
preserve the put's optionality; what it costs is the interest on $K$, and
for a low enough stock the optionality is worth less than the interest.

**Merton's condition.** Early exercise has positive probability whenever
$c < K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$ can occur, the call being worth less than the
interest on the strike. By parity,
$p = c - S + Ke^{-r\tau} < K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) - S + Ke^{-r\tau} = K - S$:
the condition says the European put would be worth less than its exercise
value, which an American put cannot be, so its holder exercises.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set $r = 0$ and run every result of this lesson again. State the call and
put corridors, the parity identity, and what becomes of the two
early-exercise conclusions; for each result, say whether $r > 0$ was a
hypothesis it needed.

<Solution>
With $r = 0$ a bond paying $K$ at $T$ costs $K$ now, so $Ke^{-rT} = K$
everywhere.

**Corridors.** $\max(0, S_0 - K) \le c \le S_0$ and
$\max(0, K - S_0) \le p \le K$. The tables are unchanged; neither bound used
the sign of $r$, only the bond's price.

**Parity.** $c - p = S_0 - K$. Unchanged in form; $r$ was never a hypothesis.

**The call.** Alive floor $S - K$, dead value $S - K$: alive $\ge$ dead, no
longer strictly. Exercising early never gains, so $C = c$ still holds, but
the "strictly more alive" of the proof is lost. The theorem needed $r > 0$
only for strictness.

**The put.** Parity with $r = 0$ gives $p = c - S_0 + K \ge K - S_0$ since
$c \ge 0$, and $p \ge 0$, so $p \ge \max(0, K - S_0)$: the European put now
sits on or above its exercise value everywhere. Early exercise gains
nothing, $P = p$, and the asymmetry between calls and puts disappears.
Merton's condition confirms it: $K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) = 0$, and $c < 0$ is
impossible. The put's early exercise needed $r > 0$ as its entire engine.

The transferable observation: in this lesson $r > 0$ is a hypothesis of
exactly one phenomenon, the put's early-exercise premium, and everything
else survives at $r = 0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Combine a long European call and a short European put with the same strike
$K$ and expiry $T$. Write the package's payoff at $T$, identify the contract
from <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the previous lesson</Ref> that
has the same payoff, and use parity to find the strike at which the package
costs nothing. Then repeat with a known dividend of present value $D$.

<Solution>
Payoff: $\max(S_T - K, 0) - \max(K - S_T, 0)$. If $S_T > K$ this is
$(S_T - K) - 0$; if $S_T \le K$ it is $0 - (K - S_T)$. In both cases
$S_T - K$: the payoff of a long forward with delivery price $K$. The corner
of the call and the corner of the put cancel.

By parity the package costs $c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. It costs nothing when
$K = S_0 e^{rT}$, which is the forward price
$F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$ of the previous lesson, now obtained from the option
identity rather than from the loan-and-share construction. The two
derivations agree because both are the law of one price applied to the
payoff $S_T - K$.

With dividends, $c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}$, and the zero-cost strike is
$K = (S_0 - D)e^{rT}$, matching the dividend-adjusted forward price of the
previous lesson's exercise. The move that transfers: any payoff that is a
line in $S_T$ has a forward price, and parity is how options are combined
into lines.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On May 8, 2013, with Google stock quoted at bid $871.23$ and offer $871.37$,
the Chicago Board Options Exchange's quotes for the September 2013 options at strike $880$ were: call
bid $41.00$, offer $41.60$; put bid $48.80$, offer $49.80$. The December
$880$ quotes were: call $55.00$ / $56.30$, put $62.40$ / $63.70$. Google
paid no dividend. Using mid-prices, back out the interest rate implied by
parity for each expiry and judge whether the two agree. Then check whether
either direction of the parity trade clears the bid–offer spread for the
September pair, stating what rate would be needed for it to do so. State
what would count as agreement with the world.

<Solution>
**Mid-price parity.** Mid stock $871.30$. September: $c = 41.30$,
$p = 49.30$, $c - p = -8.00$, so $Ke^{-rT} = S_0 - (c - p) = 879.30$ and
$e^{-rT} = 879.30/880 = 0.99920$, $rT = 0.00080$. The September expiry was
September 20, 135 days or $T = 0.370$ years ahead, giving $r = 0.22\%$.
December: $c = 55.65$, $p = 63.05$, $c - p = -7.40$, $Ke^{-rT} = 878.70$,
$rT = 0.00148$; December 20 was 226 days or $T = 0.619$ years ahead, giving
$r = 0.24\%$. The two expiries imply the same rate to within a few
hundredths of a percent, which is the first test passed: one rate explains
both pairs.

**The spread.** Direction one, buy A and sell C: buy the call at the offer
$41.60$, sell the put at the bid $48.80$, short the stock at the bid
$871.23$; cash in $878.43$, lent to $T$; pay $880$ for a share at $T$ in
either state. Profit only if $878.43e^{rT} > 880$, that is $rT > 0.00179$,
$r > 0.48\%$ for $T = 0.370$. Direction two, sell A and buy C: sell the
call at $41.00$, buy the put at $49.80$ and the stock at $871.37$; outlay
$880.17$ financed at $r$; receive $880$ at $T$. Profit only if
$880.17e^{rT} < 880$, impossible for $r \ge 0$. Neither direction clears the
spread at any plausible 2013 rate; the quotes are consistent with every
$r$ between $0$ and $0.48\%$, and the mid-implied $0.22\%$ sits inside that
band.

**Agreement with the world.** Short-term dollar rates in May 2013 were a
few tenths of a percent (three-month interbank rates near $0.27\%$, Treasury
bills lower), so an implied rate of $0.2$ to $0.25\%$ is the right sign and
size. One correction is expected: the options are American, and with no
dividend $C = c$ but $P \ge p$, so $C - P \le c - p$ and the implied
$Ke^{-rT}$ is biased upward, the implied rate biased downward, by the put's
early-exercise premium, which is small for a near-the-money put four months
from expiry at rates this low.
<Ref lesson="option-chain-bounds">The module's last lesson</Ref> runs this
check across the whole chain.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the strike-spread inequality of the Remark: for European calls on the
same stock and expiry with $K_1 < K_2$,
$$
0 \;\le\; c(K_1) - c(K_2) \;\le\; (K_2 - K_1)\,e^{-rT}.
$$
Use a dominance table with three expiry columns. Then evaluate the bound for
$K_1 = 18$, $K_2 = 20$, $r = 10\%$, $T = 1$.

<Solution>
**Left inequality.** The $K_1$-call pays $\max(S_T - K_1, 0) \ge
\max(S_T - K_2, 0)$ in every state, so it dominates the $K_2$-call and
$c(K_1) \ge c(K_2)$.

**Right inequality.** Portfolio A: one $K_2$-call plus a bond paying
$K_2 - K_1$ at $T$, costing $c(K_2) + (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$. Portfolio B: one
$K_1$-call, costing $c(K_1)$.

| | $S_T \le K_1$ | $K_1 < S_T \le K_2$ | $S_T > K_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: $K_2$-call + bond | $0 + (K_2 - K_1)$ | $0 + (K_2 - K_1)$ | $(S_T - K_2) + (K_2 - K_1) = S_T - K_1$ |
| B: $K_1$-call | $0$ | $S_T - K_1$ | $S_T - K_1$ |

Column one: $K_2 - K_1 > 0$. Column two: $K_2 - K_1 \ge S_T - K_1$ since
$S_T \le K_2$. Column three: equal. So $A_T \ge B_T$ everywhere and
$c(K_2) + (K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT} \ge c(K_1)$.

**Numbers.** $(20 - 18)e^{-0.10} = 1.81$: the $18$-call can cost at most
$1.81$ more than the $20$-call, less than the $2$ difference in strikes,
because the extra $2$ of strike is paid at expiry. The transferable move is
the third column: a table has as many expiry columns as the payoffs have
regions, and the lemma reads across all of them.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A stock at $S_0 = 4$ will be worth either $8$ or $2$ at the end of one
period, and cash grows by the factor $1 + r = 1.25$ over the period. A call
with $K = 5$ pays $3$ in the up state and $0$ in the down state. (a) Find
the cheapest static portfolio of $a$ shares and $b$ dollars of cash whose
value at $T$ is at least the call's in both states, and conclude an upper
bound for $c$ from the dominance lemma. (b) Find the dearest static
portfolio whose value at $T$ is at most the call's in both states, and
conclude a lower bound. (c) Compare the two bounds.

<Solution>
A portfolio of $a$ shares and $b$ dollars costs $4a + b$ now and is worth
$8a + 1.25b$ up and $2a + 1.25b$ down.

**(a) Cheapest dominating portfolio.** Minimise $4a + b$ subject to
$8a + 1.25b \ge 3$ and $2a + 1.25b \ge 0$. The two constraints give
$b \ge 2.4 - 6.4a$ and $b \ge -1.6a$; the first is the binding one when
$a \le 0.5$ and the second when $a \ge 0.5$. For $a \le 0.5$ the cost is at
least $4a + 2.4 - 6.4a = 2.4 - 2.4a$, decreasing in $a$; for $a \ge 0.5$ it
is at least $4a - 1.6a = 2.4a$, increasing. The minimum is at $a = 0.5$,
$b = -0.8$, cost $2 - 0.8 = 1.20$, where both constraints bind: the
portfolio pays exactly $3$ up and exactly $0$ down. By the lemma,
$c \le 1.20$.

**(b) Dearest dominated portfolio.** Maximise $4a + b$ subject to
$8a + 1.25b \le 3$ and $2a + 1.25b \le 0$. The same algebra with the
inequalities reversed gives cost at most $2.4a$ for $a \le 0.5$ and at most
$2.4 - 2.4a$ for $a \ge 0.5$; the maximum is again at $a = 0.5$, $b = -0.8$,
cost $1.20$. By the lemma, $c \ge 1.20$.

**(c)** The corridor is $[1.20, 1.20]$: the upper and lower bounds coincide
and the price is $1.20$, half a share financed by borrowing $0.80$. With two
states the cheapest portfolio above the corner and the dearest below it are
the same portfolio, because a line through two points matches a corner
exactly; the fence has zero width. This collapse is the theorem of
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the next lesson</Ref>, where $a = 0.5$ is
named the call's delta and $1.20$ is shown to be the only quote that admits
no arbitrage in a two-state world.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

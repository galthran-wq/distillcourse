Hold the stock exactly when it is above the strike, hold nothing below: in
ordinary calculus this strategy replicates a call at cost $K$ or zero, and
in fact its hedging performance refuses to improve no matter how frequently
it is monitored — the gap is quadratic variation, made visible.

This is the nineteenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the fifth move of its fourth module: the module's application.
The module has established that the scaled walk converges to Brownian
motion, that a Brownian path has infinite length but quadratic variation
exactly $T$, that the gain of an adapted strategy is an Itô integral, and
that a function of an Itô process obeys the chain rule only with the
second-order term $\tfrac12 f''\,dX\,dX$ added. <Ref
lesson="ito-formula">The previous lesson</Ref> closed on the one case its
theorem excludes: a call's payoff $(x - K)^+$ has no second derivative at
$K$, and a hedge built by ordinary calculus around that kink replicates the
call at no cost on paper. This lesson takes that hedge seriously: it states
the strategy and the accounting that says it is free, reproduces the
simulation in which its cost refuses to converge as monitoring is refined,
and identifies the missing term as the local time the formula cannot see.
What it hands on is the module's verdict, that hedging error is a
second-order phenomenon, which <Ref lesson="black-scholes-pde">the next
module</Ref> answers with a hedge ratio that carries the second-order term.

The route: the rule and the naive accounting, with the exact identity that
replaces it; Hull's Table 19.1 reproduced and extended, and the plateau it
shows; the crossing ledger, in which overshoots shrink like $\sqrt{\Delta t}$
while crossings multiply like $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$; the Itô reading, in which
the kink's second derivative is a spike of constant area and its integral
is local time; and the audit of what the study used and where it departs
from the theory.

## The rule, and the accounting that says it is free

The previous lesson's closing sentence asserted that a hedge built around
the payoff's kink is free on paper. This section states that hedge as a
rule, writes down the accounting that makes it free, and then replaces the
accounting with an exact identity that will carry every later section.

### The stop-loss rule

An institution has written a European call with strike $K$ on one share of
a stock and wants to hold the share whenever the option would be exercised.
The rule is the indicator of that event:

<Definition title="the stop-loss hedge">
Fix a strike $K$ and observation times $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = T$
spaced $\Delta t$ apart. The **stop-loss hedge** of a written call holds
$\Delta_j$ shares over $(t_j, t_{j+1}]$, where $\Delta_j = 1$ if $S(t_j) > K$,
$\Delta_j = 0$ if $S(t_j) < K$, and $\Delta_j = \Delta_{j-1}$ if $S(t_j) = K$
exactly; $\Delta_{-1} = 0$. At $T$ the holding is brought to
$\mathbf 1_{\{S(T) > K\}}$ at the price $S(T)$ (a share held below $K$ is
sold, a share not held above $K$ is bought), and if $S(T) > K$ the share is
delivered against the strike for $K$. The **hedge cost** is the sum of all purchases less all sales less the $K$
received on delivery, with interest ignored.
</Definition>

In words: buy one share at the first observation that finds the price above
$K$, sell it at the first later observation that finds the price below $K$,
repeat, and at expiry deliver the share if it is held. The continuous-time
version replaces the observations by the whole path and holds
$\Delta(t) = \mathbf 1_{\{S(t) > K\}}$; Shreve calls it the *stop-loss
start-gain* strategy (Exercise 4.21 of volume II), and Hull's §19.3 is the
textbook simulation of it. What is analysed here is that replication rule,
not the stop orders a broker executes; nothing below is a claim about stop
orders as a trading practice.

### The naive accounting: every trade at $K$

Suppose the path crosses $K$ finitely many times and every crossing is
observed at the moment it happens. Then each purchase is at $K$ and each
sale is at $K$: purchases and sales cancel in pairs. If $S(T) > K$ there is
one more purchase than sale, so the hedger holds one share bought for $K$
and delivers it for $K$; if $S(T) < K$ the counts match and the hedger holds
nothing. Either way the trades after time $0$ net to zero, and the only
cost is the initial position, $S_0$ if $S_0 > K$ and nothing otherwise.
Against the $K$ eventually received, the total cost of writing and hedging
is Hull's equation (19.1),
$$
Q = \max(S_0 - K,\,0),
$$
the option's intrinsic value at inception. For an out-of-the-money call
this is zero: the institution collects the premium and pays nothing.

Each step of that argument names a hypothesis, and the sections that follow
break them in turn. "Finitely many crossings" is false for a Brownian path.
"Observed at the moment it happens" is impossible with discrete monitoring,
and monitoring more often does not rescue it. "Purchases and sales cancel
in pairs" is exactly what fails when each purchase is above $K$ and each
sale below it.

### The exact accounting: cost equals payoff minus hedge gain

What replaces (19.1) is an identity that holds for every path and every
holding rule, not only the stop-loss one.

<Theorem title="the hedging shortfall identity">
For any holdings $\Delta_0, \ldots, \Delta_{n-1}$ decided at the observation
times, with $\Delta_{-1} = 0$ and the position closed at $T$ to
$\Delta_n = \mathbf 1_{\{S_n > K\}}$ (where $S_j = S(t_j)$), the cost of
writing and hedging the call, interest ignored, is
$$
\text{cost} \;=\; (S_n - K)^+ \;-\; \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \Delta_j\,(S_{j+1} - S_j).
$$
For the stop-loss rule in particular, the cost is the sum of the overshoots:
$$
\text{cost} \;=\; \sum_{\text{buys}} (S_{\text{buy}} - K)
\;+\; \sum_{\text{sells}} (K - S_{\text{sell}}),
$$
every term non-negative.

<Proof>
Purchases less sales is $\sum_{j=0}^{n} S_j(\Delta_j - \Delta_{j-1})$: at
each observation the hedger changes the holding by $\Delta_j - \Delta_{j-1}$
shares at price $S_j$, including the closing trade at $t_n$. Summation by
parts turns a sum of prices against holding changes into a sum of holdings
against price changes:
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n} S_j(\Delta_j - \Delta_{j-1})
= S_n\Delta_n - \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \Delta_j (S_{j+1} - S_j),
$$
using $\Delta_{-1} = 0$. Subtracting the $K\Delta_n$ received on delivery
and using $\Delta_n = \mathbf 1_{\{S_n > K\}}$, so that
$S_n\Delta_n - K\Delta_n = (S_n - K)^+$, gives the first display. It is
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the wealth equation</Ref> with $r = 0$,
read as a statement about the shortfall $(S_n - K)^+ - X_n$ of a hedger who
starts with nothing.

For the stop-loss rule, buys and sells alternate, so the number of buys
exceeds the number of sells by $\Delta_n$. Purchases less sales less
$K\Delta_n$ is therefore
$\sum_{\text{buys}} S_{\text{buy}} - \sum_{\text{sells}} S_{\text{sell}} - K(\#\text{buys} - \#\text{sells})$,
and distributing the $K$ over the trades gives the second display. Every buy is at a price above $K$ and
every sell at a price below $K$ by the rule, so every term is non-negative.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The naive accounting is the claim that every overshoot is zero; the cost
of the hedge is the sum of the overshoots; so the question is whether that
sum can be driven to zero by observing more often.

<Example title="the rule on Hull's Table 19.2 path">
Hull's Table 19.2 records a weekly path for the 20-week example below:
$49.00,\ 48.12,\ 47.37,\ 50.25,\ 51.75,\ 53.12,\ 53.00,\ 51.87,\ 51.38,\
53.00,\ 49.88,\ 48.50,\ 49.88,\ 50.37,\ 52.13,\ 51.88,\ 52.87,\ 54.87,\
54.62,\ 55.87,\ 57.25$, with $K = 50$.

**Trade list.** Weeks 0 to 2 are below $K$: no position. Week 3 finds
$50.25 > K$: buy at $50.25$. Weeks 4 to 9 stay above $K$: hold. Week 10 finds
$49.88 < K$: sell at $49.88$. Weeks 11 and 12 stay below (49.88 at week 12 is
below $K$): no position. Week 13 finds $50.37$: buy at $50.37$. Weeks 14 to
20 stay above: hold, and at week 20 deliver the share for $K = 50$.

**Cost by the overshoot formula.** Buys at $50.25$ and $50.37$ overshoot by
$0.25$ and $0.37$; the sale at $49.88$ undershoots by $0.12$. Cost
$= 0.25 + 0.12 + 0.37 = 0.74$ per share, against the naive prediction of
$0$.

**Cost by the shortfall identity.** The payoff is $(57.25 - 50)^+ = 7.25$.
The hedge gain is the price change while holding:
$(49.88 - 50.25) + (57.25 - 50.37) = -0.37 + 6.88 = 6.51$. Shortfall
$7.25 - 6.51 = 0.74$, the same number by the other route.
</Example>

## Hull's Table 19.1, reproduced and extended: the plateau

Hull's response to the overshoot objection is to observe more often, so
that each overshoot shrinks. This section
runs that experiment at his parameters and beyond them. From ordinary
calculus one would expect the cost's dispersion to fall to zero as the
observation interval does; the simulation shows it falling to about three
quarters of the option's price and stopping there.

### The setting and the measure

The written option is a 20-week European call on 100,000 shares; per share,
$S_0 = 49$, $K = 50$, $\sigma = 20\%$ a year, $r = 5\%$, $T = 0.3846$ years.
The stock's real-world drift is $\mu = 13\%$, and the simulation uses it:
this is a hedging experiment run under the measure the world runs under,
not a valuation, and no risk-neutral measure enters. The Black–Scholes
price, which <Ref lesson="black-scholes-formula">module 5</Ref> derives and
which is used here only as the yardstick, is $2.40$ per share.

Hull's convention for the performance measure, adopted throughout:
$$
\text{hedge performance}
= \frac{\text{standard deviation of the hedge cost across paths}}
       {\text{Black–Scholes price of the option}},
$$
with the cost computed as in the definition above, interest ignored. A
perfect hedge costs the same on every path, so its measure is $0$. The two
positions a hedge is meant to improve on set the scale: a naked position,
whose cost is the payoff itself, scores $1.88$ for this option (the payoff's
standard deviation is $4.52$ against a price of $2.40$), and a covered
position, one share held throughout, scores $1.22$. The mean of the cost is
not the test: with interest ignored and the
real drift in play, no hedge's mean cost equals the price, and what
separates a hedge from a bet is the spread.

### The protocol

Geometric Brownian motion is simulated on a grid of $0.01$ week, 2,000
steps per path, by the exact solution
$S(t) = S_0\exp\{(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma W(t)\}$ from <Ref
lesson="ito-formula">the previous lesson</Ref>, so that no discretisation
error enters the paths themselves. Coarser monitoring is the same path read
at every $k$-th grid point: $k = 500$ for 5-week observations down to
$k = 1$. Each frequency's cost is the shortfall identity applied to the
subsampled path, on 200,000 paths against Hull's one million.

<Aside>
Reading one fine path at several strides, rather than simulating each
frequency separately, couples the experiments: the differences between
columns are then differences in monitoring, not in the random draws. The
standard error of a sample standard deviation is
$s\sqrt{(\kappa - 1)/4N}$, with $\kappa$ the distribution's kurtosis; the
cost's right tail puts $\kappa$ near $4$, so at $N = 200{,}000$ the
performance measure carries an error of about $0.002$, and Hull's rounding
to two decimals contributes $\pm 0.005$ of its own. Agreement within $0.01$
is what can be expected, and is what was found.
</Aside>

The floor is a property of what is held, not of how often it is checked:

## The crossing ledger: overshoot times count is order one

The plateau is a fact about the path, and the shortfall identity says
where to look: the cost is a sum of overshoots, so it is the number of terms
times their typical size. This section measures both against $\Delta t$ and
finds that they scale in opposite directions at the same rate.

### Two measurements

The ledger records, at each monitoring interval, the average number of
trades per path (the closing trade at $T$ included) and the average
overshoot per trade. Three rows suffice:

| overshoot per trade, \$ | 0.886 | 0.435 | 0.0869 |

### Why the overshoot is $\sqrt{\Delta t}$

An overshoot is the distance past $K$ at the observation that first finds
the price on the other side. Over one interval, geometric Brownian motion
near $K$ moves by $\sigma K \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z$ with $Z$ standard normal,
up to a drift term of order $\Delta t$ that is a hundred times smaller at
weekly monitoring and negligible below it. A crossing is a step of that
size that happens to straddle $K$, and the overshoot is the part of the
step beyond $K$: a fraction of $\sigma K\sqrt{\Delta t}$. The ledger's last
row gives the scale, and its overshoot-per-trade row divided by that scale
gives the fraction: $0.886 / 1.387 = 0.64$ of $\sigma K \sqrt{\Delta t}$ at
weekly monitoring, and $0.63$ at every finer frequency tested
($0.435 / 0.693$ and $0.0869 / 0.139$). The overshoot is not a
defect of slow monitoring that fast monitoring removes; it is the size of a
Brownian step, and it shrinks only as the square root of the interval.

### Why the count is $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$

The count is where the finite-variation intuition fails. A smooth path
that crosses $K$ does so at isolated times, finitely many of them in $[0,T]$,
and a fine enough monitor sees each one exactly once: the count stops
growing. A Brownian path has no isolated crossings. This is the fenced fact
of the lesson: once a Brownian path reaches a level, it returns to that
level infinitely often in every interval after the first visit, so that the
set of times at which $W(t) = K$ has no isolated points (Karatzas and
Shreve, §2.9). Hull's footnote to §19.3 states the consequence: the
expected number of times a Wiener process equals a given value in a given
interval is infinite. This course states the theorem and does not prove it;
what it can do is show that quadratic variation forces the scaling.

The path spends some amount of time near $K$. Write $\ell_T(K)$ for its
**occupation density** at $K$: the time in $[0, T]$ spent within $\pm\varepsilon$
of $K$, divided by the width $2\varepsilon$ of the band, in the limit of a
narrow band, measured in years per dollar. Every observation that finds the
price within one Brownian step $\sigma K\sqrt{\Delta t}$ of $K$ has an
order-one chance of being on the other side from the previous observation;
the observations in that band number
$$
\frac{\text{time in band}}{\Delta t}
\approx \frac{2\,\sigma K\sqrt{\Delta t}\;\ell_T(K)}{\Delta t}
= \frac{2\,\sigma K\,\ell_T(K)}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} .
$$
A fixed fraction of them are crossings. The count grows like
$1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ because the band that matters has width
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$ while the observations in it are spaced $\Delta t$ apart,
and that mismatch between the path's step and the clock's step is
$dW\,dW = dt$ from <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion
lesson</Ref>, applied at a level rather than along the whole path.

Multiplying the two scalings, the cost is
$$
\text{count} \times \text{overshoot}
\;\sim\; \frac{\sigma K \ell_T(K)}{\sqrt{\Delta t}} \times \sigma K\sqrt{\Delta t}
\;=\; \sigma^2 K^2\, \ell_T(K) \times \text{const},
$$
a quantity with no $\Delta t$ in it. The next section replaces the
constant by its exact value, $\tfrac12$, and the heuristic by a theorem.

<KeyFact>
The overshoot is a Brownian step, $\propto \sqrt{\Delta t}$; the crossing
count is the band of one step's width divided by the clock's step,
$\propto 1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$; their product is $\sigma^2 K^2$ times the time
per dollar the path spends at the strike. On a path of finite variation the
count is eventually fixed and the cost falls like $\Delta t$; the plateau is
a property of roughness, not of the rule.
</KeyFact>

## The Itô reading: the kink's second derivative is the missing term

The ledger explained the plateau by counting. The module's calculus explains
it by differentiating, and the two agree to three figures. This section
applies the Itô–Doeblin formula to the payoff, watches it fail, smooths the
kink so that the formula applies, and reads the cost off the second-order
term.

### The formula applied to the payoff gives the naive answer

Take $f(x) = (x - K)^+$, so that $f'(x) = \mathbf 1_{\{x > K\}}$ for $x \ne K$
and $f''(x) = 0$ for every $x \ne K$, with neither defined at $K$. Applying
the Itô–Doeblin formula as if $f$ qualified, and dropping the second-order
term because $f''$ vanishes wherever it is defined:
$$
(S(T) - K)^+ \;\overset{?}{=}\; (S_0 - K)^+ + \int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S(t) > K\}}\,dS(t) .
$$
The integral is the continuous-time hedge gain, so this display is the
naive accounting, $\text{cost} = (S_0 - K)^+$, written as an equation. The
formula's hypothesis, that $f''$ be continuous, was made for exactly this
case: the previous lesson's proof needed $f''$ bounded near the path to
control the remainder sums, and a function whose second derivative is
undefined at a point the path visits provides no such control.

<Example title="the expectation audit (Shreve II, Exercise 4.21)">
Take Shreve's setting, the stock driftless and interest zero:
$dS = \sigma S\,dW$ with $\sigma = 0.2$, $S_0 = 49$, $K = 50$, $T = 0.3846$.

**Right side.** $(S_0 - K)^+ = 0$. The integrand $\mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}} S$
is adapted and bounded on $[0,T]$ by the path's maximum, so the integral
$\sigma\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}} S\,dW$ is an Itô integral and has
expectation $0$ by <Ref lesson="ito-integral">the martingale property</Ref>.
The right side has mean $0$.

**Left side.** $S(T) = 49\exp\{-\tfrac12\sigma^2 T + \sigma W(T)\}$ is
lognormal and exceeds $50$ with positive probability, so
$E[(S(T) - K)^+] > 0$. Its value is the Black–Scholes formula at $r = 0$,
$S_0\Phi(d_1) - K\Phi(d_2)$ with
$d_{1,2} = [\ln(S_0/K) \pm \tfrac12\sigma^2 T]/(\sigma\sqrt T)$: $d_1 = -0.101$,
$d_2 = -0.225$, and $49 \times 0.460 - 50 \times 0.411 = 1.98$.

The two sides differ by $1.98$ in expectation, so the display is false, and
the missing term has mean $1.98$: the driftless stop-loss hedge of a call
that starts out of the money costs, on average, the whole value of the
option.
</Example>

### Smoothing the kink: a spike of constant area

The device that repairs the computation is Shreve's Exercise 4.20: replace
the kink by a parabola of half-width $\varepsilon$, apply the formula
legitimately, and let $\varepsilon \to 0$. Define
$$
f_\varepsilon(x) =
\begin{cases}
0 & x \le K - \varepsilon,\\[2pt]
\dfrac{(x - K + \varepsilon)^2}{4\varepsilon} & |x - K| \le \varepsilon,\\[6pt]
x - K & x \ge K + \varepsilon .
\end{cases}
$$
The pieces meet: at $x = K + \varepsilon$ the parabola gives
$(2\varepsilon)^2/4\varepsilon = \varepsilon = x - K$. The derivative
$f_\varepsilon'(x) = (x - K + \varepsilon)/2\varepsilon$ on the band runs from
$0$ at $K - \varepsilon$ to $1$ at $K + \varepsilon$, so $f_\varepsilon'$ is
continuous everywhere: a ramp of width $2\varepsilon$ in place of the
indicator's step. The second derivative is
$$
f_\varepsilon''(x) = \frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\,\mathbf 1_{\{|x - K| < \varepsilon\}},
$$
a rectangle of height $1/2\varepsilon$ and width $2\varepsilon$: **area one
for every $\varepsilon$**. As the smoothing sharpens the rectangle narrows
and rises without changing its integral, which is what a point mass at $K$
looks like from a distance.

<Figure
  src="./figures/smoothed-kink.svg"
  alt="Top left: the call payoff with two parabolic smoothings of half-width 1 and one quarter drawn through the kink. Bottom left: their second derivatives as rectangles of height one over two epsilon and width two epsilon, the narrow one four times taller, both of area one. Right: the Itô correction one half the integral of f double prime times sigma squared S squared dt rewritten as a band-occupation integral and its limit one half sigma squared K squared times the occupation density, evaluated at Hull's parameters to 2.07"
  caption="Smoothing the kink makes the formula legal; the second-order term is the time the path spends in a band around K, divided by the band's width, and that ratio has a limit."
/>

For $f_\varepsilon$ the formula applies ($f_\varepsilon'$ is continuous and
$f_\varepsilon''$ is bounded and continuous except at two points, the
extended hypothesis of Shreve's Exercise 4.20, which this course cites and
does not prove). With $dS\,dS = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$:
$$
f_\varepsilon(S(T)) = f_\varepsilon(S_0) + \int_0^T f_\varepsilon'(S)\,dS
+ \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\int_0^T f_\varepsilon''(S)\,S^2\,dt
= f_\varepsilon(S_0) + \int_0^T f_\varepsilon'(S)\,dS
+ \frac{\sigma^2}{4\varepsilon}\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{|S - K| < \varepsilon\}}\,S^2\,dt .
$$
Now let $\varepsilon \to 0$. The left side and $f_\varepsilon(S_0)$ converge to
the payoff and to $(S_0 - K)^+$, since $f_\varepsilon \to f$ uniformly. The
stochastic integral converges to $\int \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}\,dS$: the ramps
converge to the indicator except at $x = K$, a level the path occupies for a
set of times of measure zero, and the Itô isometry carries the convergence
of integrands to convergence of integrals (fenced, as in <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">the extension to general integrands</Ref>). The last
term is $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}$ times $S^2$ evaluated within $\varepsilon$ of
$K$, times the time in the band divided by its width, and the band's
$S^2$ tends to $K^2$. What remains is the ratio of time-in-band to
band-width, and the claim that it has a limit is the definition of the
lesson's one new object.

<Definition title="local time (occupation density) at a level">
For a continuous process $S$ and a level $K$, the **occupation density** of
$S$ at $K$ over $[0, T]$ is
$$
\ell_T(K) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{1}{2\varepsilon}
\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{|S(t) - K| < \varepsilon\}}\,dt ,
$$
the time spent at $K$ per unit of price, in years per dollar. For an Itô
process with $dS\,dS = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$, the **local time** of $S$ at $K$
is $L_T^K = \sigma^2 K^2\,\ell_T(K)$, the same limit taken against
$d[S,S]$ instead of $dt$. Both are random: they depend on the path.
</Definition>

<Remark title="what is fenced">
That the limit in the definition exists for almost every Brownian path,
and that $\varepsilon \to 0$ commutes with the stochastic integral as claimed,
are theorems of the general theory this course does not prove: local time
is constructed in Karatzas and Shreve §3.6 and Revuz and Yor Chapter VI,
and the identity below is Tanaka's formula, the Itô–Doeblin formula for
$(x - K)^+$ with $\tfrac12 L_T^K$ in place of the second-order integral.
Shreve's Exercise 4.20 (vi) shows from the identity itself that $L_T^K$ is
finite and not almost surely zero, which is all the paradox needs.
</Remark>

The limit of the display is therefore
$$
(S(T) - K)^+ = (S_0 - K)^+ + \int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S(t) > K\}}\,dS(t) + \tfrac12\,\sigma^2 K^2\,\ell_T(K),
$$
and rearranging with the shortfall identity in its continuous form,
$$
\text{cost of the continuously monitored stop-loss hedge}
= (S(T) - K)^+ - \int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}\,dS - (S_0 - K)^+
= \tfrac12\,\sigma^2 K^2\,\ell_T(K) .
$$
The cost is the second-order term the naive computation set to zero. It is
not small: $\sigma^2 K^2 = 0.04 \times 2500 = 100$ dollars squared per year,
and $\ell_T(K)$ is a time per dollar, so the product is dollars.

### The audit: the theorem's number is the simulation's number

The discretely monitored cost is a left-endpoint Riemann sum for the
integral in the identity, so it converges to $\tfrac12 \sigma^2 K^2 \ell_T(K)$
as $\Delta t \to 0$: that is the plateau, and it is a random variable with
a spread of its own, which is why the performance measure stops at $0.75$
rather than at $0$. Its mean can be checked. Averaging the definition over
paths, $E[\ell_T(K)] = \int_0^T p_t(K)\,dt$, where $p_t$ is the lognormal
density of $S(t)$ from <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the GBM solution</Ref>;
at Hull's parameters with $\mu = 13\%$ the integral is $0.04136$ years per
dollar (the derivation exercise below), so
$$
E[\text{cost}] \to \tfrac12 \times 100 \times 0.04136 = 2.07 \text{ dollars per share},
$$
which is the mean-cost row of the table at every $\Delta t \le 0.25$ weeks
to within that row's standard error of $0.004$. The simulation's band count
gives the same figure directly: the average time within $\pm 0.5$ of $K$
on the fine grid is $0.0414$ years, and $0.0414 / (2 \times 0.5) = 0.0414$
years per dollar. In Shreve's driftless setting the same integral gives
$1.98$, the expectation audit's gap.

The plateau itself is the spread of the same random variable, and it can
be checked the same way. The measure is
$\operatorname{sd}(\tfrac12\sigma^2K^2\,\ell_T(K))/2.40 = 50\,\operatorname{sd}(\ell_T(K))/2.40$,
so a plateau at $0.75$ says $\operatorname{sd}(\ell_T(K)) = 0.036$ years per
dollar, nearly as large as the mean: the occupation density is as dispersed
as it is big, with $9\%$ of paths contributing zero and a tail of paths
that spend weeks near the strike. The second moment squares the definition
and averages the product of two band indicators, which for $t < s$ needs the
density of $S(t)$ at $K$ times the density of $S(s)$ at $K$ given
$S(t) = K$, the GBM transition density from $K$ back to $K$ over $s - t$:
$$
E[\ell_T(K)^2] = 2\int_0^T p_t(K)\int_0^{T-t} q_\tau\,d\tau\,dt,
\qquad
q_\tau = \frac{e^{-\nu^2\tau/2\sigma^2}}{K\sigma\sqrt{2\pi\tau}},
\quad \nu = \mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 .
$$
The inner integral is $\operatorname{erf}\bigl(\nu\sqrt{u/2}/\sigma\bigr)/K\nu$
at $u = T - t$ (the derivation exercise below), and the outer one at Hull's
parameters gives $E[\ell_T(K)^2] = 0.003016$, so
$\operatorname{Var}\,\ell_T(K) = 0.003016 - 0.04136^2 = 0.001305$ and
$$
\operatorname{sd}(\text{cost}) \to 50 \times \sqrt{0.001305} = 1.81
\text{ dollars per share}, \qquad \frac{1.81}{2.40} = 0.753 .
$$
The simulated standard deviation at $0.01$ weeks is $1.807$, measure
$0.753$; at $0.1$ weeks, $0.757$. The plateau is the theorem's number to
three figures.

<KeyFact>
The missing term is $\tfrac12\sigma^2 K^2\,\ell_T(K)$: the payoff's second
derivative, a point mass at the kink, integrated against the time the path
spends there. Its mean and its spread are integrals of the GBM density
alone: no monitoring interval enters anywhere in the computation, so the
theoretical plateau is exactly flat, and the constant the crossing ledger
left unnamed is the $\tfrac12$ of the Itô–Doeblin formula.
</KeyFact>

The point mass is a *gamma* concentrated at the strike, the second
derivative of the hedge's value with respect to the price. The Black–Scholes
call of the next module has a gamma that is smooth and positive at every
price, and holding its slope $c_x$ rather than the payoff's slope is what
the hedge that works consists of; the ramp $f_\varepsilon'$ is the first
approximation to that slope, and the forward exercise takes the comparison
one step further.

## The verdict: hedging error is second order

The module's application is now complete, and this section audits it in
the form every module-closing study takes: which results it used, and
where the artefact departs from the idealisation.

**Results used.** The paths are exact samples of geometric Brownian motion
by the solution of <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the Itô–Doeblin lesson</Ref>.
The cost identity is <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the wealth
equation</Ref> with $r = 0$. The crossing count's $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is
$dW\,dW = dt$ of <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion
lesson</Ref> applied at a level, together with the fenced theorem that
Brownian crossings are never isolated. The limit cost is the Itô–Doeblin
formula applied to a smoothed payoff, with <Ref lesson="ito-integral">the
martingale property and the isometry</Ref> carrying the expectation audit
and the passage to the limit.

**Departures.** The theory's statement is about the continuous limit and
local time; the simulation observes at finite intervals and never reaches
it, which is why the plateau is approached from above and why the coarse
columns have a higher mean cost than the fine ones: at 5-week monitoring
the overshoots are of order two dollars and the count is barely one, and the
ledger's product has not settled. Transaction costs are ignored, and with
them the hedge gets worse as it trades more. The drift is the real-world
$13\%$ and interest is ignored, so no mean cost here equals the option's
price; the measure compares spreads, which is what a hedge is for. And the
strategy is the replication rule $\Delta = \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}$, not a stop
order at a broker, which trades once and hedges nothing.

**The verdict.** The rule that ordinary calculus says is free costs
$\tfrac12 \sigma^2 K^2 \ell_T(K)$ per share, a random amount with mean
$2.07$ and a spread $0.75$ times the option's price, and no monitoring
frequency reduces it. The cost is the second-order term of the Itô–Doeblin
formula evaluated at a payoff whose second derivative is a point mass. A
hedge ratio that works must therefore come from a calculus that carries
the second-order term: it must hold a smooth function of the price whose
own second-order term, $\tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}$, is paid for by
something in the hedge rather than left as a shortfall.

What is established: the stop-loss hedge's cost is exactly the sum of its
overshoots, and the naive accounting is the assertion that every overshoot
is zero; Hull's Table 19.1 is reproduced to within $0.01$ and extended two
decades finer, and the performance measure stops at $0.75$; the overshoot
scales as $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and the crossing count as $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$
because a Brownian path has no isolated crossings, so their product does
not depend on $\Delta t$; applying the Itô–Doeblin formula to a smoothed
payoff and sharpening the smoothing identifies the product as
$\tfrac12 \sigma^2 K^2 \ell_T(K)$, the local time at the strike, whose mean
of $2.07$ per share and spread of $0.753$ times the price the simulation
confirms; and on a path of finite
variation the same rule's cost vanishes like $\Delta t$. What is not
established is the hedge that works. The module closes on the bench first: <Ref
lesson="quadratic-variation-lab">the lab</Ref> builds the meter and the
solver every simulation in this lesson leaned on and measures the theorems
behind them, the $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ overshoot among them, as convergence
rates. Then <Ref lesson="black-scholes-pde">Black–Scholes</Ref> holds
$c_x(t, S)$ shares of a function $c$ that is smooth in the price, applies
the same formula to $c(t, S(t))$, and finds that the $dt$ terms cancel on
every path only if $c$ solves a particular partial differential equation:
the Black–Scholes PDE, in which the stock's drift does not appear.

<Exercise>
Reproduce Hull's Table 19.1 at its six published frequencies with at least
10,000 paths. Simulate geometric Brownian motion at $S_0 = 49$, $K = 50$,

at every frequency; compute each path's cost by the shortfall identity;
report the six performance measures with standard errors, and state before

**Strategy.** One fine grid of $0.01$ week (2,000 steps) serves all

$(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z$ and

**Cost per path.** At each frequency read $S_j$ at the subsampled times,
set $\Delta_j = \mathbf 1_{\{S_j > K\}}$ (ties have probability zero on a

$(S_n - K)^+ - \sum_j \Delta_j (S_{j+1} - S_j)$. Divide the standard

standard deviation estimated from $N$ paths is $s\sqrt{(\kappa - 1)/4N}$,
and the cost's kurtosis $\kappa$ is about $4$, so with $N = 10{,}000$ the
measure carries an error near $0.007$; Hull rounds to two decimals, so
agreement means each of the six values lies within about $\pm 0.015$ of
his. With $N = 200{,}000$ the error is $0.002$ and agreement within $0.01$
is expected.

$0.98, 0.93, 0.83, 0.79, 0.77, 0.76$,
and at $0.05$ or $0.01$ weeks $0.75$. The plateau survives: the measure is

indicate a bug, most often subsampling the grid inconsistently or letting
the drift enter the increments as $\mu\Delta t$ instead of
$(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\Delta t$; the second error moves the mean cost, not
the plateau, and is caught by the $2.07$ check.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Take the same simulated paths and replace each by a smoothed version: the
centered moving average of the fine path over a half-week window, a path
with a continuous derivative and therefore finite variation. Run the
stop-loss rule on the smoothed paths at strides from $200$ down to $1$ grid
points and report the mean cost at each. Predict the scaling before
running, and say which hypothesis of the crossing ledger the smoothing
removes.

<Solution>
**Prediction.** A path with a continuous derivative crosses $K$ at finitely
many isolated times, and beyond some monitoring frequency each crossing is
seen exactly once: the count is fixed. Each overshoot is then the path's
slope times the time since the crossing, at most slope $\times \Delta t$,
so the cost falls **linearly** in $\Delta t$, not to a plateau.

**Measured.** On 50,000 smoothed paths, mean cost at strides
$200, 100, 50, 20, 10, 5, 2, 1$ (that is $\Delta t = 2, 1, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1,
0.05, 0.02, 0.01$ weeks): $1.98, 1.76, 1.38, 0.71, 0.38, 0.20, 0.081,
0.041$. Below half a week each halving of $\Delta t$ halves the cost; the
rough path on the same draws stays at $2.07$ from $0.2$ weeks down.

**Which hypothesis went.** The overshoot's $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ scaling
becomes $\Delta t$ because a smooth path's increment over $\Delta t$ is
slope $\times \Delta t$ rather than $\sigma K \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z$; the count's
$1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ becomes a constant because crossings are isolated. Both
changes are the same fact, that the smoothed path has zero quadratic
variation (<Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the smooth-function computation
of the Brownian motion lesson</Ref>), the one hypothesis both of the
ledger's scalings rest on.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "Monitoring hourly instead of weekly catches each crossing
within an hour of its happening, so the overshoot, and with it the cost,
becomes negligible." Name the quantity the claim gets right, the quantity
it omits, and the fact about Brownian paths that fixes the omitted one; then
give the cost's order of magnitude at hourly monitoring for Hull's option.

<Solution>
**What is right.** The overshoot does shrink: at hourly monitoring, an
hour of calendar time, $1/168$ of a week (the clock the whole study runs
on), the Brownian step near $K$ is
$\sigma K\sqrt{\Delta t} = 10\sqrt{1/(168 \times 52)} \approx 0.107$ dollars,
and the mean overshoot about $0.63$ of that, $0.067$ dollars, against $0.88$
at weekly monitoring.

**What is omitted.** The number of trades. The cost is the sum of the
overshoots, and the count of terms grows as $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$: from weekly
to hourly, a refinement by $168$, the count grows by $\sqrt{168} \approx 13$,
from $2.4$ to about $31$ trades per path (the table's $23.8$ at $0.01$ week,
scaled by $\sqrt{0.01 \times 168}$, gives the same $31$). The product
$31 \times 0.067 \approx 2.1$ dollars is the same mean cost as at weekly
monitoring, within the accuracy of the estimate.

**The fact.** A Brownian path's crossings of a level are never isolated:
once at $K$, the path recrosses it infinitely often in any following
interval, so a finer monitor does not see the "same" crossings sooner; it
sees more crossings, and the band of one Brownian step around $K$ contains
a number of observations proportional to $\sqrt{\Delta t}/\Delta t$. The
step that fails in the claim is "catches each crossing": there is no
finite list of crossings to catch.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The expectation audit used Shreve's driftless stock. Redo it for a stock
with drift $\mu$ and zero interest: show that the naive identity
$(S(T) - K)^+ = (S_0 - K)^+ + \int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}\,dS$ still fails
in expectation, identify what replaces the "mean zero" step, and compute
the expected shortfall at Hull's parameters ($\mu = 0.13$) from
$E[(S(T) - K)^+]$ and $E\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}\,dS$. Compare with the
simulated mean cost.

<Solution>
**Which kind of problem.** An expectation of an Itô process's integral,
with the drift breaking the martingale property; the tool is the
decomposition $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ inside the integral.

**The integral's mean.**
$\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}}\,dS = \mu\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}} S\,dt +
\sigma\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{S > K\}} S\,dW$. The second term is an Itô
integral with a bounded adapted integrand and has mean zero (<Ref
lesson="ito-integral">the martingale property</Ref>); the first has mean
$\mu\int_0^T E[S(t)\mathbf 1_{\{S(t) > K\}}]\,dt$. For lognormal $S(t)$ with
mean $S_0 e^{\mu t}$, $E[S(t)\mathbf 1_{\{S(t) > K\}}] = S_0 e^{\mu t}\Phi(d_1(t))$
with $d_1(t) = [\ln(S_0/K) + (\mu + \tfrac12\sigma^2)t]/(\sigma\sqrt t)$: the
first Black–Scholes term with $\mu$ in the place of $r$, from <Ref
lesson="ito-formula">the "who carries the mean" computation</Ref>.

**The payoff's mean.** $E[(S(T) - K)^+] = S_0 e^{\mu T}\Phi(d_1(T)) -
K\Phi(d_2(T))$ with $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt T$: at Hull's parameters
$d_1(T) = 0.302$, $d_2(T) = 0.178$, $e^{\mu T} = 1.0513$, and the mean is
$49 \times 1.0513 \times 0.619 - 50 \times 0.571 = 31.88 - 28.54 = 3.34$.

**The shortfall.** $E[\text{cost}] = 3.34 - \mu\int_0^T S_0 e^{\mu t}\Phi(d_1(t))\,dt$.
The integrand starts at $0$ (at $t \to 0$ the stock is certainly below $K$,
so $\Phi(d_1(t)) \to 0$), rises to $51.5 \times 0.619 = 31.9$ at $T$, and
averages $25.4$ over the interval; the integral over $0.3846$ years is
$9.77$, and $0.13 \times 9.77 = 1.27$. So
$E[\text{cost}] \approx 3.34 - 1.27 = 2.07$, matching the simulated plateau
mean and the local-time formula $\tfrac12\sigma^2 K^2 E[\ell_T(K)]$. The
naive identity fails by $2.07$ in expectation; with drift, the "mean zero"
step becomes "mean equal to the drift's contribution", and the failure is
the same term as before.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the mean of the occupation density and evaluate it. (a) Starting
from the definition of $\ell_T(K)$, show that
$E[\ell_T(K)] = \int_0^T p_t(K)\,dt$, where $p_t$ is the density of $S(t)$,
and state where an interchange of limit and expectation is assumed. (b)
Write $p_t(K)$ for geometric Brownian motion and evaluate the integral
numerically at Hull's parameters; confirm $\tfrac12\sigma^2 K^2 E[\ell_T(K)]
= 2.07$. (c) Explain in one paragraph why this number does not depend on
the monitoring interval, so that the theoretical plateau is exactly flat,
while the simulated mean cost at 5-week monitoring is $2.34$. (d) Show
that $E[\ell_T(K)^2] = 2\int_0^T p_t(K)\int_0^{T-t} q_\tau\,d\tau\,dt$ with
$q_\tau$ the density at $K$ of $S(t + \tau)$ given $S(t) = K$, evaluate
the inner integral in closed form, and confirm that the resulting standard
deviation of $\tfrac12\sigma^2K^2\ell_T(K)$, divided by the price, is the
plateau.

<Solution>
**(a)** For fixed $\varepsilon$, by Fubini,
$E\bigl[\tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{|S(t) - K| < \varepsilon\}}\,dt\bigr]
= \int_0^T \tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon} P(|S(t) - K| < \varepsilon)\,dt
= \int_0^T \tfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{K-\varepsilon}^{K+\varepsilon} p_t(x)\,dx\,dt$.
The inner average tends to $p_t(K)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ where $p_t$ is
continuous, and it is continuous for every $t > 0$. Exchanging
$\lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}$ with $E$ on the left is the assumed step; it holds
because the band averages are dominated by the maximum of $p_t$ over $x$,
which for $\varepsilon < |S_0 - K|$ is bounded uniformly in $t$: the band
lies away from the starting point, so the density on it vanishes as
$t \to 0$ rather than blowing up, and dominated convergence applies.

**(b)** $\log S(t) \sim N(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t,\ \sigma^2 t)$,
so
$p_t(K) = \dfrac{1}{K\sigma\sqrt{2\pi t}}
\exp\Bigl\{-\dfrac{[\ln(K/S_0) - (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t]^2}{2\sigma^2 t}\Bigr\}$.
At $S_0 = 49$, $K = 50$, $\ln(K/S_0) = 0.0202$, $\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 = 0.11$.
The density is zero at $t = 0$ (the stock has not had time to reach
$50$), peaks around $t \approx 0.01$ years, four days in, at about $0.25$
per dollar, and decays to about $0.063$ per dollar at $T$; a numerical integration on a
fine grid gives $\int_0^T p_t(K)\,dt = 0.04136$ years per dollar. Then
$\tfrac12 \times 0.04 \times 2500 \times 0.04136 = 2.068$, which is the
simulated mean at every $\Delta t \le 0.25$ weeks ($2.06$ to $2.07$, standard
error $0.004$).

**(c)** The identity $\text{cost} = \tfrac12\sigma^2 K^2\ell_T(K)$ is a
statement about the continuous strategy, and $\ell_T(K)$ is a functional
of the path alone: no monitoring interval appears in it, so the limit is
one number per path and its mean is the integral above, exactly flat as a
function of $\Delta t$ because $\Delta t$ has already gone to zero. The
discretely monitored cost is a left-endpoint Riemann sum for the Itô
integral in the identity, and a Riemann sum differs from its limit at
finite mesh. At $5$-week monitoring the "sum" has four terms, each
overshoot is a large fraction of the path's whole range, and the ledger's
count-times-size product has not reached its asymptotic value: the extra
$0.27$ in the mean is discretisation error of the hedge, not of the paths,
which are exact at every grid. It disappears by $0.25$ weeks, which is why
Hull's finest column is already on the plateau.

**(d)** Squaring the band average,
$\ell_\varepsilon^2 = \tfrac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}\int_0^T\!\int_0^T
\mathbf 1_{\{|S(t) - K| < \varepsilon\}}\mathbf 1_{\{|S(s) - K| < \varepsilon\}}\,ds\,dt$,
and the square is symmetric in $(s, t)$, so it is twice the integral over
$t < s$. Taking expectations under Fubini, the probability that both
$S(t)$ and $S(s)$ lie in the band is, by the Markov property of GBM,
$\int\!\int p_t(x)\,q_{s-t}(y \mid x)\,dx\,dy$ over the band squared, and
dividing by $4\varepsilon^2$ and letting $\varepsilon \to 0$ leaves
$p_t(K)\,q_{s-t}(K \mid K)$: the density of being at $K$ at time $t$ times
the density of returning to $K$ after $s - t$. Substituting $\tau = s - t$
gives the display. Given $S(t) = K$, $\log S(t + \tau) \sim
N(\ln K + \nu\tau, \sigma^2\tau)$ with $\nu = \mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2$, so its
density at $K$ is $q_\tau = e^{-\nu^2\tau/2\sigma^2}/(K\sigma\sqrt{2\pi\tau})$,
with the $1/\sqrt\tau$ singularity of a return to the starting point,
integrable. With $a = \nu^2/2\sigma^2$,
$\int_0^u e^{-a\tau}/\sqrt{2\pi\tau}\,d\tau = \operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{au})/\sqrt{2a}$
and $\sqrt{2a} = \nu/\sigma$, so
$\int_0^u q_\tau\,d\tau = \operatorname{erf}\bigl(\nu\sqrt{u/2}/\sigma\bigr)/K\nu$;
for small $u$ this is $\sqrt{2u/\pi}/K\sigma$, the driftless value, and at
$u = T$ with $\nu = 0.11$ it is $0.0485$ years per dollar. The outer
integral $2\int_0^T p_t(K)\,\operatorname{erf}(\nu\sqrt{(T - t)/2}/\sigma)/(K\nu)\,dt$
on the same fine grid gives $E[\ell_T(K)^2] = 0.003016$, so
$\operatorname{Var}\,\ell_T(K) = 0.003016 - 0.04136^2 = 0.001305$,
$\operatorname{sd} = 0.0361$ years per dollar, and
$50 \times 0.0361 / 2.40 = 0.753$: the value the simulated measure settles
at, from a double integral with no monitoring interval in it. The
coefficient of variation $0.0361/0.0414 = 0.87$ is the shape of the
histogram: a mass at zero and a long right tail.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The smoothed kink suggests a different rule: hold $f_\varepsilon'(S)$ shares,
the ramp from $0$ at $K - \varepsilon$ to $1$ at $K + \varepsilon$, instead of
the indicator. (a) Write the continuous-time cost of this rule using the
Itô–Doeblin formula for $f_\varepsilon$, and show that it is not zero
either: identify the residual and its size at $\varepsilon = 1.5$ dollars.
(b) Plot $f_\varepsilon'(S)$ against $S$ beside the function
$\Phi(d_1)$ with $d_1 = [\ln(S/K) + (r + \tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau]/(\sigma\sqrt\tau)$
at $\tau = 10$ weeks, $r = 0.05$, $\sigma = 0.2$, and describe what the two
share and what the second has that the ramp lacks. (c) State what a rule
would need in order for its second-order term to be paid for rather than
left as a shortfall.

<Solution>
**(a)** For the rule $\Delta(t) = f_\varepsilon'(S(t))$ the shortfall
identity gives $\text{cost} = (S(T) - K)^+ - \int_0^T f_\varepsilon'(S)\,dS$.
The Itô–Doeblin formula for $f_\varepsilon$ (legitimate, $f_\varepsilon''$
bounded) says
$$
\int_0^T f_\varepsilon'(S)\,dS = f_\varepsilon(S(T)) - f_\varepsilon(S_0)
- \frac{\sigma^2}{4\varepsilon}\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{|S - K| < \varepsilon\}} S^2\,dt ,
$$
so
$$
\text{cost} = \underbrace{(S(T) - K)^+ - f_\varepsilon(S(T))}_{\text{at most } \varepsilon/4}
+ f_\varepsilon(S_0)
+ \frac{\sigma^2}{4\varepsilon}\int_0^T \mathbf 1_{\{|S - K| < \varepsilon\}} S^2\,dt .
$$
The residual is the same band-occupation integral as before, now at a
fixed width instead of in the limit: $\tfrac{\sigma^2 K^2}{2}$ times the
time in the band divided by $2\varepsilon$. At $\varepsilon = 1.5$ the band is
$\pm 1.5$ dollars, wide enough that the path spends a good part of its life
in it: on the simulated paths the average time within $\pm 1.5$ of $K$ is
$0.12$ years, so the residual averages
$50 \times 0.12 / 3 \approx 2.0$ dollars, barely less than the indicator's
$2.07$. Smoothing the hedge ratio in the price alone does not remove the
second-order cost; it spreads the same cost over a band.

**(b)** Both functions rise from $0$ to $1$ through $K$ and are near
$\tfrac12$ at the strike. $\Phi(d_1)$ is smooth everywhere, is wider (its
rise spans about $\pm 5$ dollars at ten weeks against the ramp's
$\pm 1.5$), and, decisively, depends on $\tau$: it narrows toward the
indicator as expiry approaches and is broad far from it. The ramp is fixed
in time.

**(c)** The rule must hold the price-derivative $c_x(t, S)$ of a function
$c(t, x)$ that also depends on time, chosen so that the $dt$ term of
$c(t, S(t))$, namely $c_t + \mu S c_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}$, is
matched by the financing of the hedge rather than left over. The
second-order term $\tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}$ is then paid for by $c_t$,
the decay of the hedge's value in time. Solving for that $c$ is <Ref
lesson="black-scholes-pde">the next lesson</Ref>: the condition is the
Black–Scholes partial differential equation, and the ramp you plotted is
$\Phi(d_1)$'s shape with the time dependence removed.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

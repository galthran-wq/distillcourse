An exercise rule is admissible only if the decision to stop at time $n$ uses
the first $n$ tosses alone — and this restriction, not any modeling choice, is
what keeps a stopped martingale a martingale.

This is the tenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the first move of its third module. The second module
established that under the risk-neutral coin every discounted price is a
martingale and every claim's price is a discounted conditional expectation
(<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the pricing formula</Ref>),
and its closing exercises found the one claim that formula does not price:
on the two-period \$4 tree the \$5 put is worth $3$ at node $T$ to a holder
who may exercise there and $2$ to one who must wait, so the right to choose
the exercise date has a value, $1.36$ against $0.96$ at the root, that the
European machinery does not compute. What stands open is how to price that
right. Doing so needs two things the module did not supply: a precise class
of exercise rules the holder can actually follow, and a theorem saying what
following one does to the martingales the whole theory runs on. This lesson
supplies both. A stopping time is a random time whose decision at $n$ is
settled by the first $n$ tosses; a process stopped at one rides until the
stop and then freezes; a martingale so stopped is still a martingale; and a
rule that reads one toss ahead breaks the property by an amount the tree
computes exactly. <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">The next lesson</Ref>
defines the American price as the best value over the class defined here
and proves the optimal rule with the theorem proved here.

The route: the put holder's rule and the rule she would prefer; the
definition and its boundary cases; the stopped process; optional sampling,
proved from one identity; the same computation on the foresight rule, where
it fails and by how much; and what the American pricing problem takes from
the class.

## The put holder's rule is a random time, and the rule she would prefer reads the next toss

Every American exercise rule is a random variable: a date, one per path, at
which the holder acts. The two rules below both read the tree's prices, both
are defined on every path, and only one of them can be carried out. The
difference between them is the definition of this lesson, and the first
thing to fix is what the two lessons on information established about
"can be carried out".

### The put's rule on the two-period tree

The tree is the \$4 stock with $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$,
$\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$, two periods: prices $4$; $8$ and $2$; $16$,
$4$ and $1$. The claim is the \$5 put, whose values under early exercise
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson's last
exercise</Ref> computed node by node: at $T$ exercising pays $3$ against a
continuation worth $2$, so the holder exercises; at $H$ the put is out of the
money and holding is worth $0.40$; at the root, holding is worth
$\tfrac{1}{1.25}(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.40 + \tfrac12 \cdot 3) = 1.36$ against
$1$ for exercising, so the holder waits. At time two the surviving paths pay
$(5 - S_2)^+$: nothing at $16$, one dollar at $4$.

Written as a date per path, the rule is
$$
\tau(HH) = \infty, \qquad \tau(HT) = 2, \qquad \tau(TH) = \tau(TT) = 1,
$$
where $\tau = \infty$ means the option is left to expire unexercised. The
holder who follows $\tau$ collects, in time-zero dollars, $0$ on $HH$,
$1/1.25^2 = 0.64$ on $HT$, and $3/1.25 = 2.4$ on $TH$ and on $TT$; the
$\tilde P$-average of the four, each of weight $\tfrac14$, is
$\tfrac14(0 + 0.64 + 2.4 + 2.4) = 1.36$, the root value the backward pass
produced.

### The rule she would prefer

A holder who has taken the $H$ branch holds a put worth $0.40$ and regrets
not having taken the dollar at time zero. Had she known the first toss, she
would have exercised at the root on $HH$ and $HT$, and had she known the
second, she would have exercised at $T$ on $TH$ but waited for the $1$ price
on $TT$. That rule is
$$
\rho(HH) = \rho(HT) = 0, \qquad \rho(TH) = 1, \qquad \rho(TT) = 2:
$$
exercise at the lowest price the path will visit. Its discounted payoffs
are $1$, $1$, $2.4$ and $4/1.5625 = 2.56$, with average $1.74$, and it is in
the money on every path.

The rule $\rho$ cannot be followed. At time zero the holder sees $S_0 = 4$
and nothing else, and $\rho$ tells her to exercise on $HH$ and $HT$ but not
on $TH$ and $TT$: the paths through the root are identical at time zero and
the rule treats them differently. The same happens at $T$, where $\rho$
stops on $TH$ and continues on $TT$ with only $\omega_1 = T$ in view. The
rule $\tau$ never does this. The set of paths on which $\tau = 1$ is
$\{TH, TT\}$, the whole block $[T]$; the set on which $\tau = 2$ is
$\{HT\}$, a block of time two; the set on which $\tau = \infty$ is
$\{HH\}$, also a block of time two. Each decision is a union of blocks of
the date at which it is taken, which is what the definition requires.

## Stopping times: the event τ = n is decided by the first n tosses

The property that separates $\tau$ from $\rho$ is a statement about sets,
not about what the rule reads, and it is stated as one.

<Definition title="stopping time (Shreve I, Definition 4.3.1)">
In the $N$-period binomial model, a **stopping time** is a random variable
$\tau$ on the $N$-toss space taking values in $\{0, 1, \ldots, N\} \cup
\{\infty\}$ such that for every $n \le N$ the event $\{\tau = n\}$ depends
only on the first $n$ tosses: if $\tau(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n
\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N) = n$, then $\tau(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n
\omega'_{n+1} \cdots \omega'_N) = n$ for every continuation
$\omega'_{n+1} \cdots \omega'_N$.
</Definition>

The clause says that $\{\tau = n\}$ is a union of blocks of
$\mathcal F_n$. The value $\infty$ needs no clause of its own: on a finite
tree $\{\tau = \infty\}$ is the complement of the union of the
$\{\tau = n\}$, which depends on all $N$ tosses, as any event may.

<Remark title="notation ledger: two forms of one clause, and n ∧ τ">
$\{\tau = n\} \in \mathcal F_n$ for all $n$ and $\{\tau \le n\} \in
\mathcal F_n$ for all $n$ are the same condition on the tree. In one
direction, $\{\tau \le n\} = \bigcup_{k \le n}\{\tau = k\}$ and each
$\{\tau = k\}$ with $k \le n$ is a union of blocks of $\mathcal F_k$, hence
of the finer blocks of $\mathcal F_n$. In the other,
$\{\tau = n\} = \{\tau \le n\} \setminus \{\tau \le n - 1\}$, a difference
of two unions of $\mathcal F_n$-blocks. The $\le$ form is the one that
survives in continuous time, where the starred section restates it. The
symbol $n \wedge \tau$, read "$n$ min $\tau$", is $\min(n, \tau)$, a random
variable equal to $n$ on $\{\tau \ge n\}$ and to $\tau$ on $\{\tau < n\}$;
$\mathbb 1_A$ is the indicator of an event, $1$ on $A$ and $0$ off it.
</Remark>

Four cases fix the boundary of the definition, all on the \$4 tree.

- **The canonical case.** First passage below a level: for a number $m$,
  $\tau_m = \min\{n : S_n \le m\}$, with $\min \emptyset = \infty$. It is a
  stopping time because $\{\tau_m = n\}$ is the event $S_0 > m$,
  $S_1 > m$, …, $S_{n-1} > m$, $S_n \le m$, and every price in that list is
  known by time $n$. On the three-period tree with $m = 2$ it strikes at
  $T$ and at $HTT$, and nowhere else.
- **A case with a different surface.** The put's rule $\tau$ reads the
  put's value process rather than a price level, and is a stopping time by
  the block check of the previous section. So is any constant rule
  $\tau \equiv n$, and so is a rule that reads the path rather than the
  price, such as "the first time two consecutive tails have occurred":
  $\{\tau = n\}$ is the set of paths whose tosses $n - 1$ and $n$ are the
  first adjacent pair of tails, a union of time-$n$ blocks.
- **The degenerate cases.** $\tau \equiv 0$, exercise at once;
  $\tau \equiv N$, hold to expiry; $\tau \equiv \infty$, never exercise.
  All three are stopping times, and the closing boundary exercise reads the
  theorem at the first two.
- **The near miss.** The regret rule $\rho$. It reads nothing but observed
  prices: on $HH$ it stops at $S_0 = 4$, a price the holder sees. The clause
  it violates is that $\{\rho = 0\} = \{HH, HT\} = [H]$ is a block of time
  one, not of time zero. What is chosen by a later toss is not the price it
  acts on but *which* observation to act on, and the definition counts that
  as foresight.

</Aside>

which observation to act on is the foresight.
</KeyFact>

## The stopped process: the path rides to τ and freezes

A stopping time acts on a process by freezing it. The construction is
needed twice: to state the theorem, and to compute the value a holder
following a given rule receives.

<Definition title="stopped process">
Let $X_0, \ldots, X_N$ be an adapted process and $\tau$ a stopping time.
The **stopped process** is
$$
X_{n \wedge \tau}(\omega) = X_{\min(n, \tau(\omega))}(\omega),
\qquad n = 0, 1, \ldots, N,
$$
so that on a path with $\tau(\omega) = k \le n$ it equals $X_k(\omega)$
and on a path with $\tau(\omega) > n$, including $\tau = \infty$, it equals
$X_n(\omega)$.
</Definition>

Time is not stopped. The process continues to be indexed by $n$ past
$\tau$, and its value is held at $X_\tau$; "frozen process" would describe
it better, and "stopped process" is the term in universal use.

<Example title="the discounted stock stopped at the put's rule">
Write $M_n = S_n/1.25^n$ for the discounted stock on the two-period tree:
$M_0 = 4$; $M_1(H) = 6.4$, $M_1(T) = 1.6$; $M_2(HH) = 10.24$,
$M_2(HT) = M_2(TH) = 2.56$, $M_2(TT) = 0.64$. Stop it at $\tau$, the put's
rule.

At $n = 0$, $0 \wedge \tau = 0$ on every path, so $M_{0 \wedge \tau} = 4$.

At $n = 1$, $\tau \ge 1$ on every path, so $1 \wedge \tau = 1$ and
$M_{1 \wedge \tau} = M_1$: $6.4$ on $H$, $1.6$ on $T$.

At $n = 2$, $2 \wedge \tau$ is $2$ on $HH$ (where $\tau = \infty$) and on
$HT$ (where $\tau = 2$), and is $1$ on $TH$ and $TT$ (where $\tau = 1$).
So
$$
M_{2 \wedge \tau}(HH) = M_2(HH) = 10.24, \quad
M_{2 \wedge \tau}(HT) = M_2(HT) = 2.56, \quad
M_{2 \wedge \tau}(TH) = M_{2 \wedge \tau}(TT) = M_1(T) = 1.6 .
$$
The $\tilde P$-mean of each column: $4$; $\tfrac12(6.4 + 1.6) = 4$;
$\tfrac14(10.24 + 2.56 + 1.6 + 1.6) = 4$.
</Example>

The stopped process is built from a stopping time, and the first place the
stopping-time clause is used is in showing that the result is adapted.
Sorting the paths by the value of $\tau$,
$$
X_{n \wedge \tau} = \sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1} \mathbb 1_{\{\tau = k\}}\,X_k
+ \mathbb 1_{\{\tau \ge n\}}\,X_n ,
$$
and every factor on the right depends only on the first $n$ tosses: $X_k$
and $\mathbb 1_{\{\tau = k\}}$ for $k < n$ because $\tau$ is a stopping time
and $X$ is adapted, and $\mathbb 1_{\{\tau \ge n\}}$ because $\{\tau \ge n\}$
is the complement of $\{\tau \le n - 1\}$, decided by time $n - 1$. The
stopped process therefore has a value at each node of the tree, which is
what the figures draw.

### The engine: one identity for the increment

The stopped process changes between $n$ and $n + 1$ exactly on the paths
where the stop has not yet happened, and there it changes by the same
amount as the process itself:
$$
X_{(n+1) \wedge \tau} - X_{n \wedge \tau}
= \mathbb 1_{\{\tau > n\}}\bigl(X_{n+1} - X_n\bigr).
$$
On a path with $\tau \le n$ both sides are zero: the left because
$(n + 1) \wedge \tau = n \wedge \tau = \tau$, the right because the
indicator is zero. On a path with $\tau \ge n + 1$ the left side is
$X_{n+1} - X_n$, since $(n + 1) \wedge \tau = n + 1$ and $n \wedge \tau
= n$, and the right side is the same with indicator one. There are no
other paths.

  alt="The increment identity at the top; below it three small panels, each showing four dates around n with a dashed path and an amber stopped path: in the case tau at most n the amber line is flat and both sides are zero; in the cases tau equals n plus one and tau greater than n plus one the amber line takes the step from n to n plus one and both sides equal the increment; a box states that the indicator of tau greater than n is decided by the first n tosses and comes out of the conditional expectation"

### The theorem

<Theorem title="optional sampling, part I (Shreve I, Theorem 4.3.2)">
Let $X_0, \ldots, X_N$ be adapted on the $N$-toss space with a probability
measure giving every toss sequence positive probability, and let $\tau$ be
a stopping time. If $X$ is a martingale, then $X_{n \wedge \tau}$ is a
martingale. If $X$ is a supermartingale, so is $X_{n \wedge \tau}$; if $X$
is a submartingale, so is $X_{n \wedge \tau}$. The same holds with
$\tilde E_n$ throughout.

<Proof>
Roadmap, in four steps: the stopped process is adapted; the increment
identity; the indicator comes out of $E_n$; the hypothesis on $X$ decides
the sign of what remains.

**Step 1, adapted.** By the sorted-sum expression of the previous section,
$X_{n \wedge \tau}$ depends only on the first $n$ tosses, so the martingale
clause can be asked of it.

**Step 2, the identity.** For each $n \le N - 1$ and every path,
$X_{(n+1) \wedge \tau} - X_{n \wedge \tau} = \mathbb 1_{\{\tau > n\}}(X_{n+1} - X_n)$,
verified above in its three cases.

**Step 3, the indicator comes out.** Take $E_n$ of both sides. On the left,
$E_n[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = X_{n \wedge \tau}$ by Step 1 and taking out what
is known. On the right, $\{\tau > n\}$ is the complement of
$\{\tau \le n\}$, which is decided by the first $n$ tosses because $\tau$
is a stopping time, so the indicator is constant on each time-$n$ block
and comes out:
$$
E_n\bigl[X_{(n+1) \wedge \tau}\bigr] - X_{n \wedge \tau}
= \mathbb 1_{\{\tau > n\}}\,\bigl(E_n[X_{n+1}] - X_n\bigr).
$$
This is the sentence where the argument becomes general: nothing about
$X$ has been used, and everything about $\tau$ has.

**Step 4, the sign.** The bracket on the right is $0$ if $X$ is a
martingale, $\le 0$ if a supermartingale, $\ge 0$ if a submartingale, on
every block; the indicator is $0$ or $1$ and preserves each of those signs.
So $E_n[X_{(n+1) \wedge \tau}]$ equals, is at most, or is at least
$X_{n \wedge \tau}$ respectively, at every node and every date.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: a stopped process
either stands still or takes the same step as the original, and which of
the two it does is known before the step is taken. The steps that use the
stopping-time hypothesis: Step 1, once, and Step 3, decisively; Step 3 is
the one that fails for $\rho$, and the next section computes by how much.
The step that uses the horizon being finite: none, and the starred section
says what an unbounded horizon changes.

### The expectation at a stopping time

The one-step clause iterates. For a martingale the stopped process has
constant mean, and the mean is the starting value.

<Theorem title="optional sampling, part II (Shreve I, Theorem 4.3.3)">
Under the hypotheses of part I, for every $n = 0, \ldots, N$:
if $X$ is a martingale, $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = E[X_n] = X_0$; if $X$ is a
submartingale, $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] \le E[X_n]$; if $X$ is a
supermartingale, $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] \ge E[X_n]$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: the martingale case is part I plus constant mean; the two
inequalities need a second decomposition, of what the stop leaves out.

**Martingale.** By part I, $X_{n \wedge \tau}$ is a martingale, so by the
constant-mean theorem of <Ref
lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>,
$E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = X_{0 \wedge \tau} = X_0$, and $E[X_n] = X_0$ by the
same theorem applied to $X$.

**Sub- and supermartingale.** Part I gives $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] \ge X_0$
and $E[X_n] \ge X_0$ for a submartingale, which does not order the two.
Instead, write the gap as the steps taken after the stop:
$$
X_n - X_{n \wedge \tau} = \sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1} \mathbb 1_{\{\tau \le k\}}\,(X_{k+1} - X_k),
$$
an identity on every path, since the terms with $k < \tau$ vanish and the
rest telescope from $X_\tau$ to $X_n$ (and the whole sum is zero on
$\{\tau \ge n\}$). For each $k$, $\{\tau \le k\}$ is decided by the first
$k$ tosses, so by the tower property and taking out what is known,
$$
E\bigl[\mathbb 1_{\{\tau \le k\}}(X_{k+1} - X_k)\bigr]
= E\bigl[\mathbb 1_{\{\tau \le k\}}\,\bigl(E_k[X_{k+1}] - X_k\bigr)\bigr],
$$
and the inner bracket is $\ge 0$ on every block for a submartingale,
$\le 0$ for a supermartingale. Summing, $E[X_n] - E[X_{n \wedge \tau}]$
has that sign.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof of part II used the $\le$ form of the stopping-time clause where
part I used the $>$ form; the ledger remark is why both are available. On
the \$4 tree the martingale case reads $E[M_{2 \wedge \tau}] = 4 = M_0$,
the column mean of the example, and the put exercise's supermartingale $Y$
gives $E[Y_{2 \wedge \tau}] = \tfrac14(0 + 0.64 + 2.4 + 2.4) = 1.36$
against $E[Y_2] = \tfrac14(0 + 0.64 + 0.64 + 2.56) = 0.96$: stopping a
supermartingale early keeps more of it, which is the inequality's content.

<KeyFact>
A stopping time changes where a martingale ends up, never what it averages:
the stopped discounted stock lands on $1.6$ with probability $\tfrac12$
where the unstopped one lands on $2.56$ or $0.64$, and both average $4$.
For a submartingale, part II says stopping early can only lower the mean,
and that inequality is what will make an American call worth no more than
a European one in <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the next lesson</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

## Stopping with foresight: the same stock stopped at ρ drifts from 4 to 2.56

The regret rule reads only prices the holder has seen, and from that one
would expect the stock stopped at it to stay fair. It does not. The
arithmetic below is the same as the worked example's with $\rho$ in place
of $\tau$, and it comes out differently at exactly the nodes where $\rho$
peeks.

### The computation

At $n = 0$, $M_{0 \wedge \rho} = M_0 = 4$ on every path. At $n = 1$,
$1 \wedge \rho$ is $0$ on $HH$ and $HT$ and $1$ on $TH$ and $TT$, so
$$
M_{1 \wedge \rho}(HH) = M_{1 \wedge \rho}(HT) = M_0 = 4, \qquad
M_{1 \wedge \rho}(TH) = M_{1 \wedge \rho}(TT) = M_1(T) = 1.6,
$$
with $\tilde P$-mean $\tfrac14(4 + 4 + 1.6 + 1.6) = 2.8$. At $n = 2$,
$2 \wedge \rho$ is $0, 0, 1, 2$ on the four paths, so the values are
$4, 4, 1.6, 0.64$ with mean $\tfrac14 \times 10.24 = 2.56$. The column
means run $4, 2.8, 2.56$, and the one-step clause fails at the root, where
the average of $4$ and $1.6$ is $2.8$, and at $T$, where the average of
$1.6$ and $0.64$ is $1.12$. At $H$ it holds, because $H$ is frozen and the
two successors are the node itself.

$\tfrac12(4 + 1.6)$ and $\tfrac12(1.6 + 0.64)$. Then account for the drift

\tilde E[M_{2 \wedge \rho}] - 4
= \tilde E\bigl[\mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 0\}}(M_1 - M_0)\bigr]
+ \tilde E\bigl[\mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 1\}}(M_2 - M_1)\bigr]:

<Aside>
Answers. The averages are $2.8$ and $1.12$. First term: $\{\rho > 0\}
= \{TH, TT\}$, where $M_1 - M_0 = 1.6 - 4 = -2.4$, so the term is

so the term is $\tfrac14 \times (-0.96) = -0.24$, node $T$'s shortfall
$1.12 - 1.6 = -0.48$ weighted by $\tilde P(T) = \tfrac12$. Sum: $-1.44 =

</Aside>

### Where the proof breaks: the indicator stays inside

The increment identity holds for $\rho$ exactly as for $\tau$; it is
arithmetic on each path, and Step 2 never asked what $\rho$ is. Step 3 did.
At the root, $\{\rho > 0\} = \{TH, TT\} = [T]$ is a block of time one, so
$\mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 0\}}$ is not constant on the single block of
$\mathcal F_0$ and does not come out of $\tilde E_0$. Computed inside,
$$
\tilde E_0\bigl[\mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 0\}}(M_1 - M_0)\bigr]
= \tfrac12 \cdot 0 \cdot (6.4 - 4) + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot (1.6 - 4) = -1.2,
$$
whereas the product that Step 3 would have written,
$\mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 0\}}\,\tilde E_0[M_1 - M_0]$, is $0$ on every path
because the discounted stock is a martingale. The indicator is $1$ on
exactly the branch where the increment is negative: that correlation
between the decision and the toss it is supposed not to see is what
foresight is in the arithmetic, and $-1.2$ is its price at the root. The
same computation at $T$ gives $\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12(0.64 - 1.6)
= -0.48$, and $1.2 + \tfrac12 \times 0.48 = 1.44$ is the bias of the
figure's tally.

### Stopping is trading

The martingale lesson wrote the discounted wealth of any self-financing
strategy as a running sum,
$X_n/(1 + r)^n = X_0 + \sum_{k < n} \Delta_k\,(M_{k+1} - M_k)$. Summing the
increment identity from $0$ to $n - 1$ along a path gives
$$
M_{n \wedge \tau} = M_0 + \sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1} \mathbb 1_{\{\tau > k\}}\,(M_{k+1} - M_k):
$$
the stopped discounted stock is the discounted wealth of the strategy that
holds one share until $\tau$ and cash thereafter, with
$\Delta_k = \mathbb 1_{\{\tau > k\}}$. For a stopping time that $\Delta_k$
depends only on the first $k$ tosses, so it is a portfolio process in the
sense of <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod lesson</Ref>,
and optional sampling for the discounted stock is the discounted-wealth
theorem read for one particular rule. For $\rho$ the rule is
$\Delta_0 = \mathbb 1_{\{\rho > 0\}}$, which is $0$ on $[H]$ and $1$ on
$[T]$: a holding decided by the toss about to land, the foresight rule
that the multiperiod lesson's definition of a portfolio process was
written to exclude. The closing exercise on the mirror rule, sell at the
path's highest price, computes the arbitrage it produces in dollars.

  src="./figures/holding.svg"
  alt="Two two-period trees carrying the holding Delta_k equal to the indicator of tau greater than k in blue beside the stopped discounted stock at each node; left, the put's rule: Delta_0 = 1 at the root, Delta_1 = 1 at H and Delta_1 = 0 at the frozen node T, one number per node; right, the regret rule: the root carries Delta_0 = 0 on the edge to H and Delta_0 = 1 on the edge to T in amber, and node T likewise carries Delta_1 = 0 toward TH and Delta_1 = 1 toward TT, two numbers per node; beneath each tree a path's wealth sum is written out"
  caption="The same stopped stock as a trading strategy: for the put's rule the holding is one number at each node, a portfolio process; for the regret rule the root's holding is 0 or 1 according to the toss about to land, which no portfolio process may do."
/>

The put reads the same way. A seller who had to be hedged against $\rho$
would need $1.74$ at the root, and no hedge exists for that number: the
hedge would have to hold, at time zero, a delta that reads $\omega_1$.
Against $\tau$, and against every other stopping time, the seller needs a
hedge that is a portfolio process, and $1.36$ is what it costs.

<KeyFact>
Stopping is holding $\mathbb 1_{\{\tau > k\}}$ shares of the discounted
stock, and a stopping time is exactly a rule for which that holding is a
portfolio process. The theorem and the counterexample are the wealth
theorem and the foresight arbitrage of the second module, restated for the
one strategy an option holder controls: when to stop.
</KeyFact>

## What American pricing consumes: the admissible class and the theorem

The buyer of an American put chooses the exercise date, and the seller must
be hedged whatever choice she makes. The class of choices she can actually
make is the class defined here, because a rule she can follow decides at
each date from what she has seen, which is the stopping-time clause; and
the seller's hedge is a portfolio process, which exists against stopping
times and, by the previous section, against nothing else. The American
pricing problem of <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the next lesson</Ref>
is therefore posed over the shaded set of the figure: the value of the put
is the largest discounted expected payoff any stopping time can collect,
and the next lesson's theorem is that this largest value is attained by
the first time the price meets the payoff, and that it equals the cost of
the seller's hedge.

<Figure
  src="./figures/admissible-class.svg"
  alt="A large rounded box labelled all random times on the tree, containing an amber-shaded box labelled stopping times with five entries: first passage below 2, the put's rule, the constants N and 0, the stop-loss crossing time, and combinations of stopping times; outside the shaded box three entries: stop at the path's lowest price, sell at the path's highest price, and the last time the stock is above 4"
  caption="The admissible class: every rule the holder can follow, and only those, sits in the shaded set; the American price is a maximum over that set, and the three rules outside it are well-defined per path and unhedgeable."
/>

Two things about the class are worth recording before it is used. The
theorem proved here is what bounds the seller's exposure across the whole
class at once: for any supermartingale $Y$ that dominates the discounted
payoff at every node, part I says $Y_{n \wedge \tau}$ is a supermartingale
for every stopping time $\tau$, so its mean is at most $Y_0$, and no
stopping time can collect more than $Y_0$ from a payoff that $Y$
dominates. That is the whole of the seller's side, and the buyer's side is
finding the $\tau$ for which the bound is tight. And the class is closed
under the combinations a holder might want: the earlier of two stopping
times, the later, and "one step after", are stopping times, while "one
step before" is not; the closing exercise proves all four, and the last is
the regret rule in general form.

One rule from outside the option context belongs in the class and is worth
naming now: the hedger's stop-loss, buy the share the first time the stock
crosses the strike upward and sell it the first time it crosses back, is a
sequence of first-passage times and is therefore admissible. What it costs
to run is a question about the crossings of a continuous path, which no
tool of this module answers; <Ref lesson="stop-loss-paradox">the stop-loss
lesson</Ref> computes it and finds a hedge that never improves with
rebalancing frequency.

What is established: an exercise rule is a random time, and it is
admissible exactly when each event $\{\tau = n\}$ is a union of time-$n$
blocks, a condition the first-passage rule and the put's rule satisfy and
the regret rule fails; a process stopped at a stopping time freezes at its
value and is adapted; a martingale, supermartingale or submartingale
stopped at a stopping time keeps its type, because the increment of the
stopped process is the indicator of "not yet stopped" times the original
increment and the indicator is decided before the step; the mean of a
stopped martingale is its starting value; and a rule that reads one toss
ahead breaks the property by the correlation between its indicator and the
increment, $1.44$ on the \$4 tree by time two, which is the foresight
arbitrage of the second module in another form. What is not established is
which stopping time the holder should use and what the seller must charge.
<Ref lesson="american-derivatives">The next lesson</Ref> defines the
American price as the smallest supermartingale dominating the discounted
payoff, computes it by a backward pass with a maximum at every node, and
proves with the theorem of this lesson that the first time price equals
payoff is an optimal stopping time.

## ★ Off the tree: unbounded horizons and continuous time

Part II says $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = X_0$ for every $n \le N$, and on the
tree that is a statement about $X_\tau$ itself: on $\{\tau \le N\}$,
$X_{N \wedge \tau} = X_\tau$, and no limit is taken. Without a bound on the
horizon the step from $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = X_0$ for all $n$ to
$E[X_\tau] = X_0$ is an exchange of limit and expectation, and it can fail.

<Remark title="the doubling strategy">
Bet one dollar on a fair coin, double the stake after every loss, and stop
at the first win: $\tau$ is the first head, a stopping time with
$P(\tau < \infty) = 1$, and the wealth $X_n$ from $X_0 = 0$ is a martingale
under the fair coin. On $\{\tau \le n\}$ the stopped wealth is $+1$; on
$\{\tau > n\}$, of probability $2^{-n}$, it is $-(2^n - 1)$, the sum of the
$n$ lost stakes. So $E[X_{n \wedge \tau}] = (1 - 2^{-n}) - (2^n - 1)2^{-n}
= 0$ for every $n$, as part I requires, while $X_\tau = 1$ on every path
where the game ends, so $E[X_\tau] = 1$. The mass $2^{-n}$ sitting at
$-(2^n - 1)$ carries $-1$ of expectation at every $n$ and vanishes in the
limit. The general theorem, Doob's optional stopping theorem, restores
$E[X_\tau] = X_0$ under any of three extra hypotheses: $\tau$ bounded,
which is the tree's case; $X_{n \wedge \tau}$ bounded and $\tau$ finite
almost surely; or $E[\tau]$ finite with bounded increments. Williams,
Chapter 10, proves all three. Nothing in this course needs them: every
stopping time on a tree is bounded by $N$, and the continuous-time uses
below come with their own fence.
</Remark>

<Remark title="the continuous-time statement (Shreve II, Definition 8.2.1, Theorem 8.2.4)">
In continuous time a stopping time is a random variable $\tau$ with values
in $[0, \infty]$ such that $\{\tau \le t\}$ is in $\mathcal F(t)$ for every
$t \ge 0$; the $\le$ form is now the only usable one, since $\{\tau = t\}$
for each $t$ no longer determines $\{\tau \le t\}$ by a finite union. The
first passage of a process with continuous paths to a level,
$\tau_m = \min\{t \ge 0 : X(t) = m\}$, is a stopping time; the proof
approximates the crossing by rational times and is the one place the
argument is not a finite check. The optional sampling theorem is then the
same sentence as part I, a martingale stopped at a stopping time is a
martingale, and Shreve states it without proof, as does this course: it is
one of the named, fenced facts of the continuous half, used when
<Ref lesson="perpetual-american-put">the perpetual put</Ref> is priced by
stopping a Brownian martingale at a boundary.
</Remark>

<Exercise>
On the three-period \$4 tree ($u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$) let
$\tau = \min\{n : S_n \le 2\}$. Write its value on each of the eight paths,
verify the stopping-time clause at $n = 1$, $2$, $3$ by exhibiting each

$\tilde E[M_{3 \wedge \tau}]$ for the discounted stock $M_n = S_n/1.25^n$,

**Values.** $S$ along the paths: $HHH$: $4, 8, 16, 32$; $HHT$: $4, 8, 16,

$\tau = 3$ on $HTT$; $\tau = 1$ on $THH$, $THT$, $TTH$, $TTT$.

**The clause.** $\{\tau = 1\} = \{THH, THT, TTH, TTT\} = [T]$, one block of
time one. $\{\tau = 2\} = \emptyset$, the empty union. $\{\tau = 3\} =

$\tfrac18$ per path:
$$
\tilde E[M_{3 \wedge \tau}] = \tfrac18(16.384 + 4.096 + 4.096 + 1.024) + \tfrac48 \times 1.6 = 3.2 + 0.8 = 4 = M_0,
$$
as optional sampling part II requires of a martingale stopped at a
stopping time. The transferable move: the frozen paths contribute their
frozen value with the weight of the whole block, and the block's weight
times the frozen value is what the unstopped paths' values would have
averaged to, because the stock is fair from that node on.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Let $\lambda$ be the last time the stock price is above
$4$ on the three-period \$4 tree. Since $\lambda$ only uses observed stock
prices, it is a legitimate exercise rule." Compute $\lambda$ on all eight
paths, exhibit two paths that agree through time one on which $\lambda$

fails and the word in the claim that hides the failure.

<Solution>
**Values.** Prices above $4$ occur at $S_1 = 8$, $S_2 = 16$, and $S_3 = 8$

$HTH$: $S_3 = 8$, $\lambda = 3$. $HTT$: $S = 4, 8, 4, 2$, so $\lambda = 1$.
$THH$: $S_3 = 8$, $\lambda = 3$. $THT$, $TTH$, $TTT$: never above $4$;

**Two paths.** $HTH$ and $HTT$ agree through time one, and through time
two: both read $4, 8, 4$. On $HTT$ the rule says the holder should have
acted at time one; on $HTH$ it says she should wait until time three. At
time one, holding $S_1 = 8$ with the same past on both, she cannot tell
which instruction applies.

**The clause.** $\{\lambda = 1\} = \{HTT\}$, a single path, is not a union
of time-one blocks; the block $[H]$ contains it together with three paths
on which $\lambda = 3$. The word that hides the failure is *last*: "the
last time" quantifies over all future prices, so the rule reads observed
prices but is decided by unobserved ones. The transferable move: "first"
rules are stopping times because "first" is decided as soon as it happens;
"last" rules never are, because "last" is decided only at the end.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $\tau$ and $\sigma$ be stopping times on the $N$-period tree. Prove
that $\tau \wedge \sigma = \min(\tau, \sigma)$ and $\tau \vee \sigma =
\max(\tau, \sigma)$ are stopping times, and that $\tau + 1$ is one, with
the convention that $N + 1$ and $\infty + 1$ both mean $\infty$. Then show
by example that $\tau - 1$ need not be one, with the convention
$\infty - 1 = \infty$, and identify the rule it describes.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Use the $\le$ form of the clause from the ledger remark
for the minimum, and the $=$ form for the rest.

**Minimum.** $\{\tau \wedge \sigma \le n\} = \{\tau \le n\} \cup
\{\sigma \le n\}$, a union of two events each decided by the first $n$
tosses, hence decided by them; by the ledger remark the $\le$ form
suffices.

**Maximum.** $\{\tau \vee \sigma \le n\} = \{\tau \le n\} \cap
\{\sigma \le n\}$, an intersection of two such events.

**Successor.** For $1 \le n \le N$, $\{\tau + 1 = n\} = \{\tau = n - 1\}$,
which is a union of time-$(n - 1)$ blocks and hence of time-$n$ blocks;
$\{\tau + 1 = 0\}$ is empty. So $\tau + 1$ is a stopping time: a holder
who follows $\tau$ and then waits one more period follows an admissible
rule.

**Predecessor.** $\{\tau - 1 = n\} = \{\tau = n + 1\}$, a union of
time-$(n + 1)$ blocks, which need not be a union of time-$n$ blocks. On the
two-period tree take $\tau = \tau_2$, the first passage below $2$:
$\tau = 1$ on $[T]$ and $\infty$ on $[H]$, so $\tau - 1 = 0$ on $[T]$ and
$\infty$ on $[H]$, and $\{\tau - 1 = 0\} = [T]$ is not decided at time
zero. The rule is "act one step before the stock first reaches $2$", and
it requires the toss that will take it there. The transferable move: the
class is closed under waiting and under combining, and not under
anticipating; that is exactly why <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the
next lesson</Ref> can take the earlier of two candidate rules without
leaving the class.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the constant rules $\tau \equiv 0$ and $\tau \equiv N$. Check that
each is a stopping time, write out what optional sampling parts I and II
say for each, and say what each rule means for the holder of an American
option with payoff $G_n$: which claim's value the rule collects, and what
the two values together say about the American price on the two-period
\$5 put, whose exercise-now value is $1$ and European value $0.96$.

<Solution>
**Stopping times.** For $\tau \equiv 0$, $\{\tau = 0\} = \Omega$ and
$\{\tau = n\} = \emptyset$ for $n \ge 1$; both are unions of blocks of any
date. For $\tau \equiv N$, $\{\tau = N\} = \Omega$ and the rest are empty.
Both satisfy the clause.

**The theorem at $\tau \equiv 0$.** $n \wedge 0 = 0$, so
$X_{n \wedge \tau} = X_0$ for all $n$: a constant process, which is a
martingale under every measure. Part II reads $E[X_0] = X_0 = E[X_n]$, the
constant-mean theorem restated. The theorem is true and says nothing new.

**The theorem at $\tau \equiv N$.** $n \wedge N = n$ for $n \le N$, so
$X_{n \wedge \tau} = X_n$: the stopped process is the process, and both
parts are the hypothesis repeated. Nothing about the theorem excludes
these cases; they are the endpoints of the class, and the theorem is
uniform over it.

**For the holder.** $\tau \equiv 0$ collects $G_0$, the exercise-now
value; $\tau \equiv N$ collects the European claim, worth
$\tilde E[G_N/(1 + r)^N]$. Both rules are available to an American holder,
so the American price is at least the larger of the two: on the two-period
\$5 put, at least $\max(1, 0.96) = 1$. It is $1.36$, so the best stopping
time is neither endpoint; it is the put's rule, which stops at $T$ and
holds at $H$. The transferable move: the constant rules give the two
cheapest lower bounds on an American price, immediate exercise and the
European value, and the early-exercise premium is what the interior of the
class adds.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $\sigma$ be the rule "sell at the path's highest discounted price":
on the two-period \$4 tree, $\sigma(HH) = 2$, $\sigma(HT) = 1$,
$\sigma(TH) = \sigma(TT) = 0$. Compute $\tilde E[M_{2 \wedge \sigma}]$ for
the discounted stock and the excess over $M_0 = 4$. Then run the strategy
that borrows $4$ at time zero, buys one share, sells it at $\sigma$ and
holds the proceeds in the money market: compute its wealth at time two on
all four paths, decide whether it is <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">an
arbitrage</Ref>, and name the step of the no-arbitrage theorem's proof in
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>
that this strategy escapes.

<Solution>
**The mean.** $M_{2 \wedge \sigma}$ is $M_2(HH) = 10.24$, $M_1(H) = 6.4$,
$M_0 = 4$, $M_0 = 4$ on the four paths, so
$\tilde E[M_{2 \wedge \sigma}] = \tfrac14(10.24 + 6.4 + 4 + 4) = 6.16$, an
excess of $2.16$ over $4$ in time-zero dollars. The rule is the mirror of
$\rho$, and it biases the stopped stock upward by the same mechanism:
$\mathbb 1_{\{\sigma > 0\}}$ is $1$ on $[H]$, the branch where the
increment is $+2.4$, and $0$ on $[T]$.

**The ledger.** The loan grows to $4 \times 1.5625 = 6.25$ by time two.
$HH$: the share is held to time two and worth $16$; wealth $16 - 6.25 =
9.75$. $HT$: sold at time one for $8$, grown to $10$; wealth $3.75$. $TH$
and $TT$: sold at time zero for $4$, repaying the loan; wealth $0$. These
time-two wealths are $1.5625 \times (M_{2 \wedge \sigma} - 4)$; in
time-zero dollars they are $M_{2 \wedge \sigma} - 4$, that is
$9.75/1.5625 = 6.24$, $3.75/1.5625 = 2.4$, $0$, $0$, with mean $2.16$.

**Arbitrage.** Zero initial cost, never negative, positive on $HH$ and
$HT$, each of positive probability: an arbitrage.

**The escaped step.** The strategy holds $\Delta_0 = \mathbb 1_{\{\sigma > 0\}}$
shares, $1$ on $[H]$ and $0$ on $[T]$, decided by $\omega_1$. The
discounted-wealth theorem's proof takes $\Delta_n$ out of $\tilde E_n$
because it depends only on the first $n$ tosses; here it does not, and the
step $\tilde E_0[\Delta_0(M_1 - M_0)] = \Delta_0\,\tilde E_0[M_1 - M_0] = 0$
is false: the left side is $\tfrac12 \times 2.4 = 1.2$. Without the
martingale property of discounted wealth the constant-mean corollary
fails, and the no-arbitrage theorem, which is that corollary plus
equivalence, has nothing to say. The transferable move: every foresight
rule is an arbitrage, and every arbitrage from foresight is caught at the
same step of the same proof, the one that needs $\Delta_n$ adapted.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the two-period \$4 tree let $G_n = (5 - S_n)^+$ be the put's intrinsic
value: $G_0 = 1$; $G_1 = 0, 3$; $G_2 = 0, 1, 1, 4$. List every stopping
time with values in $\{0, 1, 2\}$ by the set of nodes at which it stops,
compute $\tilde E[G_\tau/1.25^\tau]$ for each, and report the largest
value and the rule attaining it. Then say why allowing the value $\infty$
adds no better rule.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** A stopping time with values in $\{0, 1, 2\}$ is a
decision at the root, then, if not stopped, a decision at each of $H$ and
$T$, then forced stopping at time two. Count the choices.

**The list.** Stop at the root: one rule. Otherwise choose stop or
continue at $H$ and at $T$ independently: four rules. Five in all.

1. $\tau \equiv 0$: collects $G_0 = 1$. Value $1$.
2. Stop at $H$ and at $T$ ($\tau \equiv 1$): $\tfrac12 \cdot 0/1.25 +
   \tfrac12 \cdot 3/1.25 = 1.2$.
3. Stop at $H$, continue at $T$: $\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot
   1/1.5625 + \tfrac14 \cdot 4/1.5625 = 0.16 + 0.64 = 0.8$.
4. Continue at $H$, stop at $T$: $\tfrac14 \cdot 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot
   1/1.5625 + \tfrac12 \cdot 3/1.25 = 0.16 + 1.2 = 1.36$.
5. Continue at both ($\tau \equiv 2$): $\tfrac14(0 + 0.64 + 0.64 + 2.56)
   = 0.96$, the European put.

**The maximum.** $1.36$, attained by rule 4, which is the put's rule
$\tau$ up to the value it assigns on $HH$: stopping at time two there
collects $0$, as does never stopping.

**The value $\infty$.** A rule that assigns $\infty$ on a path where a
finite time was available replaces a payoff $G_n \ge 0$ by $0$, and can
only lower the discounted expectation; on this tree it lowers it strictly
except on $HH$, where $G_2 = 0$ already. Counting all rules with values in
$\{0, 1, 2, \infty\}$ gives $26$, and none exceeds $1.36$.

The number $1.36$ is the American put's price from the backward pass, and
the maximizing rule is "stop the first time the price $V_n$ equals the
payoff $G_n$": at $T$, $V_1 = 3 = G_1$; at $H$, $V_1 = 0.40 > 0 = G_1$; at
the root, $1.36 > 1$. <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">The next
lesson</Ref> proves both facts for every tree and every payoff: the
American price is the maximum over all stopping times, and the first time
price meets payoff attains it, with the theorem of this lesson as the
proof's engine.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

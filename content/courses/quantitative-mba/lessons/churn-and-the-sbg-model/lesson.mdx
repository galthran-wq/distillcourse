Cohort churn rates fall with tenure in nearly every dataset, and the
intuitive explanation — customers grow loyal — is usually wrong.

The module so far has one object and one identity:
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the cohort matrix</Ref>, whose rows are
survival data, and $S(t) = r_1 r_2 \cdots r_t$ from
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the survival lesson</Ref>, which also
left two questions standing: why the observed rates $r_t$ rise with tenure,
and how to continue $S(t)$ past the observed window. This lesson answers both
with a single two-parameter model, the shifted-beta-geometric, in which no
individual customer's behavior changes at all. The route: the projection task
on a real published dataset, the failure of curve fitting, the sBG model
built from constant-churn individuals plus heterogeneity, the sorting effect
that makes its aggregate retention rise, the maximum-likelihood fit, and the
projection that lands within 2–4% five years past the data.

## Churn, and the projection problem

Both standing questions concern the rate at which customers leave; the
business word for that rate is *churn*. The churn rate of period $t$ is the
fraction of customers active at the end of period $t-1$ who are gone by the
end of $t$, the complement $1 - r_t$ of the
retention rate of <Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the previous
lesson</Ref>. Throughout this lesson churn counts **customers**; when account
sizes differ, a revenue-weighted churn behaves differently, and
<Ref lesson="mrr-nrr-and-negative-churn">the revenue-retention lesson</Ref>
takes that version up.

## Curve fitting fails off the page

A first instinct is to fit a flexible function of time to the observed
points and read the extrapolation off the fitted equation. Berry and Linoff
ran this exercise on the High End segment; Fader and Hardie replicate it.
Fitting years 0–7 gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{linear:} \quad & y = 0.925 - 0.071\,t, & R^2 = .922\\
\text{quadratic:} \quad & y = 0.99671 - 0.14223\,t + 0.010185\,t^2, & R^2 = .998\\
\text{exponential:} \quad & \ln y = -0.062 - 0.102\,t, & R^2 = .963
\end{aligned}
$$

with $y$ the fraction surviving. The quadratic's coefficients are printed to
five decimals because extrapolation amplifies rounding: the three-decimal
version the paper prints, $y = 0.997 - 0.142\,t + 0.010\,t^2$, gives $0.733$
at year 12 ($+86\%$ against the actual) where the unrounded fit gives
$0.757$, the $+92\%$ quoted below. All three fit the window well, the
quadratic almost perfectly. Extrapolated to year 12 and compared with the
held-out actuals, the linear model comes in **81% too low**, the exponential
30% too low, and the quadratic **92% too high**.

<Figure
  src="./figures/quadratic-seduction.svg"
  alt="High End survival data for years 0 to 12 with the quadratic fit: nearly perfect through year 7, then curving upward while the actual points keep falling, a +92% gap at year 12"
  caption="The in-sample champion is the out-of-sample disaster: R² = .998 on years 0–7, +92% at year 12."
/>

The failures are structural, not unlucky. The linear model's line crosses
zero just past year 13 and keeps going: negative survival. The quadratic's
parabola bottoms out at $t \approx 7$, the window's edge, and **rises**
afterward, and a survivor function cannot rise. On the Regular segment the same three fits
give the same story (the exponential, the best of the three there, is 54%
too low at year 12). Polynomials and exponentials in $t$ are shapes, not
explanations; nothing in them knows that the curve is a product of
period-by-period renewal decisions, so nothing constrains their behavior
where there is no data to hold them down.

<KeyFact>
In-sample $R^2$ measures interpolation and only interpolation. Extrapolation
is governed by the assumed functional form, and a form chosen for fit
carries no information about behavior outside the window: $R^2 = .998$ and a
92% projection error are the same model.
</KeyFact>

## A population of coins: the sBG model

What survives off the page is a model of the **process** that generates
renewals, because a process assumption constrains every future period the
same way it constrains the observed ones. Fader and Hardie build the model
from a deliberately minimal story:

- at the end of each period, a customer flips a coin: heads, cancel; tails,
  renew;
- for a given customer, P(heads) never changes;
- P(heads) varies across customers.

Nobody renews by coin flip; the authors call the story a *paramorphic*
representation: a mechanism that reproduces the behavior without claiming
to be the behavior. The discipline is to start with the simplest story and
add richness only when the data rejects it.

### One customer: the shifted geometric

<Definition title="shifted-geometric lifetime">
For a customer with churn probability $\theta \in (0,1)$, the lifetime $T$
(the period in which cancellation occurs) satisfies

$$
P(T = t \mid \theta) = \theta (1-\theta)^{t-1}, \qquad
S(t \mid \theta) = (1-\theta)^{t}, \qquad t = 1, 2, \ldots
$$
</Definition>

A $\theta = 0.3$ customer renews each year with probability $0.7$:
$S(1) = 0.7$, $S(2) = 0.49$, and the probability of cancelling in year 3 is
$0.7^2 \times 0.3 = 0.147$. Every retention rate of this customer is the
same $0.7$: the geometric distribution is memoryless, so a homogeneous
population of such customers has a **flat** $r_t$ series. The data's series
is not flat: the Regular segment runs from $r_1 = 0.631$ up to
$r_7 = 0.920$, and the closing exercise of
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the previous lesson</Ref> showed that
no constant rate fits even four years of it. Refitting here confirms it: the
best single coin for the Regular segment ($\hat\theta = 0.226$, by the
likelihood method below) predicts a flat $0.774$ against an observed climb
from $0.631$ to $0.920$. The first two story elements cannot carry the data.
The third does.

### Three customers: heterogeneity already produces the rise

<Example title="a three-type cohort">
Let a cohort be one-third each of $\theta = 0.1$, $\theta = 0.3$, and
$\theta = 0.5$ customers. The aggregate survivor function is the average of
three geometric curves:

$$
S(t) = \tfrac{1}{3}\,(0.9)^t + \tfrac{1}{3}\,(0.7)^t + \tfrac{1}{3}\,(0.5)^t,
$$

so $S(1) = 0.700$, $S(2) = 0.517$, $S(3) = 0.399$, $S(4) = 0.320$. The
aggregate retention rates $r_t = S(t)/S(t-1)$ are

$$
r_1 = 0.700, \quad r_2 = 0.738, \quad r_3 = 0.772, \quad r_4 = 0.801:
$$

rising, while every individual's rate stays fixed at $0.9$, $0.7$, or
$0.5$. The reason is visible in the cohort's composition. At acquisition the
three types are 33/33/33. Among year-4 survivors the weights are
proportional to $0.9^4 : 0.7^4 : 0.5^4$, which normalizes to
**68% / 25% / 7%**: the $\theta = 0.5$ customers have mostly eliminated
themselves, so the survivors are increasingly the $\theta = 0.1$ type, and
the average of the survivors' rates drifts up toward $0.9$.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A magazine's subscribers are one quarter loyal ($\theta = 0.2$) and three
quarters casual ($\theta = 0.8$). Compute $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$ for the
cohort, and the composition of the survivors at the end of year 2.

<Solution>
Survival: $S(1) = 0.25(0.8) + 0.75(0.2) = 0.350$;
$S(2) = 0.25(0.64) + 0.75(0.04) = 0.190$;
$S(3) = 0.25(0.512) + 0.75(0.008) = 0.134$.

Rates: $r_1 = 0.350$, $r_2 = 0.190/0.350 = 0.543$,
$r_3 = 0.134/0.190 = 0.705$.

Composition at the end of year 2: loyal survivors $0.25 \times 0.64 = 0.16$
of the original cohort, casual survivors $0.75 \times 0.04 = 0.03$, so the
surviving population is $0.16/0.19 = 84\%$ loyal against 25% at acquisition.
Two constant coins reproduce the shape of the published magazine-renewal
climb (about 30%, then 50%, then 75%;
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">lesson 1</Ref>'s numbers): a renewal rate
that more than doubles while no individual changes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### From a histogram to a density

Three types are arbitrary; so are ten. The tutorial version of this
derivation (Fader and Hardie's
[ART Forum handout](http://www.brucehardie.com/talks/intro_tut_art_16_HO.pdf),
pp. 26–42) refines the grid: an equally spaced 3-point grid at
$\theta \in \{1/6, 1/2, 5/6\}$ can put its mean only between $0.167$ and
$0.833$; a 10-point grid is more flexible and
less parsimonious; letting the number of grid points go to infinity replaces
the histogram with a continuous density $g(\theta)$ on $(0,1)$, and the
mixture sum becomes an integral. For $g$, the standard choice is the beta
distribution: a two-parameter family on exactly $(0,1)$, flexible enough to
be U-shaped, J-shaped, reverse-J, or single-peaked,

$$
g(\theta \mid \alpha, \beta)
= \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)},
\qquad \alpha, \beta > 0,
\qquad E(\theta) = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
$$

where $B(\alpha,\beta) = \int_0^1 \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}
\,d\theta$ is the beta function.

  caption="From one coin to a population: the mixture of constant-rate individuals, refined until the histogram becomes a Beta density."
/>

<Task>

curves in frame 2 are each geometric (constant proportional decay); the

flat late. Convexity of the aggregate is the visual signature of
heterogeneity.
</Task>

<Definition title="shifted-beta-geometric (sBG) model">
A cohort follows the **sBG model** with parameters $\alpha, \beta > 0$ when
each customer's lifetime is shifted-geometric with churn probability
$\theta$, and $\theta$ varies across customers as
$\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$. Averaging the individual-level formulas over
$g(\theta \mid \alpha, \beta)$ gives, for $t = 1, 2, \ldots$,

$$
P(T = t) = \frac{B(\alpha+1,\, \beta+t-1)}{B(\alpha, \beta)},
\qquad
S(t) = \frac{B(\alpha,\, \beta+t)}{B(\alpha, \beta)}.
$$
</Definition>

The integrals collapse because the geometric terms $\theta(1-\theta)^{t-1}$
merge with the beta kernel into another beta integral; the step-by-step
derivation is Appendix A of the 2007 paper. No beta function ever needs to
be evaluated in practice: dividing consecutive terms and using
$\Gamma(x+1) = x\,\Gamma(x)$ gives a **forward recursion**,

$$
P(T=1) = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
\qquad
P(T=t) = \frac{\beta+t-2}{\alpha+\beta+t-1}\;P(T=t-1),
$$

and $S(t) = 1 - \sum_{i \le t} P(T=i)$. Everything the model produces is
seven multiplications away from the two parameters.

## Sorting, not loyalty

With the model built, the module's standing question, the rise of observed
retention rates, has a closed-form answer.

<Theorem title="sBG retention rate (Fader & Hardie 2007, eq. 8)">
Under the sBG model, the aggregate retention rate is

$$
r_t = \frac{\beta+t-1}{\alpha+\beta+t-1}
    = 1 - \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta+t-1},
$$

strictly increasing in $t$ and approaching 1, while every individual's
retention probability $1-\theta$ is constant.

<Proof>
By <Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the product identity</Ref>,
$r_t = S(t)/S(t-1)$. Substituting the sBG survivor function,

$$
r_t = \frac{B(\alpha, \beta+t)}{B(\alpha, \beta+t-1)}
    = \frac{\Gamma(\beta+t)\,\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+t-1)}
           {\Gamma(\beta+t-1)\,\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+t)},
$$

after writing each beta function as
$B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$ and cancelling the common
$\Gamma(\alpha)$ factors. The recursion $\Gamma(x+1) = x\,\Gamma(x)$ reduces
the two ratios to $(\beta+t-1)/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$. The second form follows
by algebra; it is increasing in $t$ because the subtracted fraction shrinks,
and it tends to 1 because the fraction tends to 0.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The second form of the formula says what is actually happening. Among
survivors of $t-1$ periods, the churn probabilities are no longer
distributed as the acquisition-day $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$: surviving
$t-1$ flips multiplies the density by $(1-\theta)^{t-1}$, and renormalizing
gives $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\, \beta+t-1)$, a distribution pushed toward low
$\theta$. Its mean is $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$: exactly the fraction
subtracted in the theorem. So

$$
r_t = 1 - E[\theta \mid \text{survived } t-1 \text{ periods}]:
$$

each period's aggregate retention is one minus the **mean churn probability
of whoever is left**, and whoever is left is an ever-more-selected group.
For the fitted High End segment (parameters below), mean churn is 14.9% on
acquisition day and 7.9% among four-year survivors: the cohort's churn rate
halves while not one customer changes.

  caption="Sorting made visible: survivorship shifts mean churn from 14.9% at acquisition to 7.9% among four-year survivors (left), and the aggregate retention rate rises on composition alone (right)."

The practitioner reading of rising renewal rates is that **customers become
more loyal as the relationship deepens**; the magazine renewal figures of
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">lesson 1</Ref> (roughly 30% at first
renewal, 50% at second, 75% for longtime readers) circulate in print as
evidence for exactly that. The inference is invalid. The sBG reproduces any
such climb with individuals who never change, so an aggregate climb, by
itself, is **zero evidence** of individual change; the two-type magazine
exercise above manufactures the published pattern from two constant coins.
Vaupel and Yashin, demographers who catalogued the same trap in mortality
data in 1985, call this family of artifacts the *ruse of heterogeneity*:
dynamics at the population level that are pure composition, with no dynamics
at the individual level.

The retention curve itself cannot separate the stories. Two designs can:

- **Same age, different vintages.** Loyalty-building (say, an onboarding
  investment) should raise the early-age rates of the cohorts acquired
  after it; sorting predicts identical $r_1$ for every vintage. This is
  the cohort matrix's
  <Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels" id="the-column-read-same-age-successive-vintages">column read</Ref>,
  and it needs no new machinery.
- **A model that allows individual dynamics.** Replacing the geometric with
  a discrete Weibull lets each customer's churn probability rise or fall
  with tenure and lets the data vote: the beta-discrete-Weibull (BdW)
  model, which Fader, Hardie, Liu, Davin, and Steenburgh (2018) fit to the
  two segments of this lesson's dataset. The vote runs against the loyalty
  story twice over. Where the constant-$\theta$ assumption fails,
  individual churn probabilities most likely **rise** with tenure — even
  while the aggregate retention rate climbs — and accounting for
  cross-sectional heterogeneity matters more than accounting for any
  individual-level dynamics. The continuous-time analogue, a Weibull-gamma
  model, reaches the same verdict on a telecommunications provider's churn
  data (Schweidel, Fader, and Bradlow 2008): individual risk rising with
  tenure. (The BdW also reproduces an initial *dip* in a retention series,
  $r_2$ falling below $r_1$ before the climb, as the High End segment's
  rates do — a shape no mixture of constant coins can produce.)

<KeyFact>
The sBG is the **null model to beat**, not a law of customer behavior.
</KeyFact>

## Maximum likelihood in five columns

Fitting the model to the observed window is the audience's home ground:
write the likelihood of the data as a function of $(\alpha, \beta)$ and
maximize it. For a cohort of $N$ customers observed for 7 periods, the data
reduce to eight counts: $n_t$ customers lost in period $t$ for
$t = 1, \ldots, 7$, and $N - \sum_t n_t$ still active at the end of period
7. For 1,000 High End customers the losses are 131, 126, 90, 60, 42, 34, 26,
with 491 survivors.

Each customer lost in period $t$ contributes a factor $P(T = t)$; each
survivor contributes $S(7)$: a lifetime known only to exceed 7, the
censored observations of
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the previous lesson</Ref> entering the
likelihood as survival probabilities rather than being discarded. The
log-likelihood is

$$
LL(\alpha, \beta)
= \sum_{t=1}^{7} n_t \ln P(T = t)
+ \Bigl(N - \sum_{t=1}^{7} n_t\Bigr) \ln S(7),
$$

with $P(T=t)$ and $S(7)$ supplied by the forward recursion.

  caption="The whole estimation, as printed: three parameter cells and seven rows. The abstract's promise is literal — the model “can be implemented in a simple Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.”"

The paper's Appendix B implements this in a worksheet of five columns ($t$,
$P(T=t)$, $S(t)$, number active, number lost) plus three cells for
$\alpha$, $\beta$, and $LL$. The recursion lives in the $P(T=t)$ column
(each cell multiplies the one above by $(\beta+t-2)/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$),
and the whole apparatus is an argument in itself: the model asks for nothing
a 2005 spreadsheet could not deliver.

The optimization run: starting values $\alpha = \beta = 1$ give
$LL = -2115.5$; Solver, told to maximize the $LL$ cell by changing the two
parameter cells subject to both being $\ge 0.0001$, converges to
$LL = -1611.2$ at $\hat\alpha = 0.668$, $\hat\beta = 3.806$. Restarting from
$\alpha = \beta = 0.01$ (where $LL = -2741.7$) reaches the same optimum:
the standard check that the optimizer found a maximum of the surface, not an
artifact of the starting point.

<Remark title="a numerical rule">
Never evaluate the beta-function ratios directly: $\Gamma(\beta+t)$
overflows floating point long before $t$ is interesting. The forward
recursion avoids the issue entirely; where a direct evaluation is wanted
(one-off checks, continuous $t$), compute in logs via `gammaln` and
exponentiate the difference.
</Remark>

<Example title="the same fit in numpy">
The worksheet translates line for line:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

lost  = np.array([131, 126, 90, 60, 42, 34, 26])   # n_t, years 1-7
alive = 491                                         # active at year 7

def negLL(params):
    a, b = params
    p = [a / (a + b)]
    for t in range(2, 8):
        p.append(p[-1] * (b + t - 2) / (a + b + t - 1))
    p = np.array(p)
    return -(lost @ np.log(p) + alive * np.log(1 - p.sum()))

fit = minimize(negLL, x0=[1.0, 1.0], bounds=[(1e-4, None)] * 2)
print(fit.x, -fit.fun)        # [0.668  3.806]  -1611.2
```

Run once with `x0=[1.0, 1.0]` and once with `x0=[0.01, 0.01]`; both print
the same $(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta)$. The same code with the Regular segment's
counts is one of the closing exercises.
</Example>

## Projection to year twelve: within 2–4%

The fitted model projects by running the recursion past the window: the
same formula that fit years 1–7 produces years 8–12, because the model's
future is the same process as its past, not a curve's continuation. For High
End, $S(12 \mid 0.668, 3.806) = 37.8\%$ against the held-out actual
$39.4\%$: **4% low**. For Regular ($\hat\alpha = 0.704$,
$\hat\beta = 1.182$, fit the same way), $17.0\%$ against $17.3\%$: **2%
low**. The regressions on the identical window missed by −81%, +92%, and
−30%.

### What the fitted parameters say

The parameters are not just projection machinery; they describe the
population. Both segments fit with $\alpha < 1 < \beta$, the
**reverse-J** shape: a large mass of low-churn customers plus a tail of
high-churn ones who exit early. The mix is why the aggregate curve is
convex and the single-coin fit failed. The segments differ in the mean:
$E(\theta) = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ is **15%** for High End and **37%** for
Regular. The four qualitative Beta shapes are worth knowing as vocabulary,
because a fitted shape is a one-glance diagnosis of the customer base:

<KeyFact>
The projection inherits the model's assumptions, not just its fit: the sBG
treats every cohort as a draw from one fixed $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$, so
a vintage effect — the same failure that breaks
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival" id="independent-censoring-is-an-assumption">independent
censoring</Ref> — invalidates the extrapolation even where the in-window fit
is excellent. And both fitted $\hat\alpha < 1$ make $\sum_t S(t)$ diverge —
the model says expected tenure is infinite — which is why
<Ref lesson="customer-lifetime-value">the next lesson</Ref> can only sum
$S(t)$ under a discount factor.
</KeyFact>

## The model's cell: discrete-time, contractual

The sBG's writ has boundaries, and they are the taxonomy the module's later
lessons fill in. Two distinctions classify any customer base: whether
departure is **observed** (contractual: cancellations, non-renewals) or
latent (noncontractual: a retail customer who simply stops), and whether
transactions can happen at **any instant** (continuous) or only at fixed
renewal points (discrete). The sBG occupies exactly one of the four cells: discrete-time,
contractual. Its continuous-time contractual analog is the
exponential-gamma model, with the same sorting arithmetic; the
noncontractual cells, where even "still a customer?" must be inferred (the
Pareto/NBD and BG/NBD models in continuous time, the BG/BB at discrete
opportunities), are the subject of
<Ref lesson="repeat-purchase-models">the repeat-purchase lesson</Ref>.
Applying the sBG outside its cell (to usage-based "churn" definitions, or
where death is unobserved) is a category error, not an approximation.

  src="./figures/model-taxonomy.svg"
  alt="Two-by-two grid classifying customer bases by contractual versus noncontractual relationship and discrete versus continuous time: the sBG in the contractual discrete-time cell, the exponential-gamma in the contractual continuous-time cell, and BG/BB, Pareto/NBD and BG/NBD in the noncontractual row"
  caption="The four cells of customer-base analysis (Fader & Hardie 2009, after Schmittlein et al. 1987). The sBG covers one; the noncontractual row belongs to the repeat-purchase lesson."

What is established: churn words resolve to a probability model; a
two-parameter mixture of constant-churn individuals fits a retention series
by maximum likelihood in a spreadsheet, projects five years past the window
within 2–4%, and produces rising aggregate retention by sorting alone,
$r_t = (\beta+t-1)/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$. The fitted $S(t \mid \alpha,\beta)$
now extends to any horizon, which is precisely what a value calculation
needs: <Ref lesson="customer-lifetime-value">the next lesson</Ref> weights
it by margin and discount and calls the sum customer lifetime value.

<Exercise>
With the High End parameters $\alpha = 0.668$, $\beta = 3.806$: compute
$r_1$, $r_2$, and $r_{10}$ from the closed form, and $S(3)$ via the forward
recursion (three $P(T=t)$ values, then the complement).

<Solution>
Closed form $r_t = (\beta+t-1)/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$:

$$
r_1 = \frac{3.806}{4.474} = 0.851, \quad
r_2 = \frac{4.806}{5.474} = 0.878, \quad
r_{10} = \frac{12.806}{13.474} = 0.950.
$$

Recursion: $P(T{=}1) = 0.668/4.474 = 0.1493$;
$P(T{=}2) = 0.1493 \times 3.806/5.474 = 0.1038$;
$P(T{=}3) = 0.1038 \times 4.806/6.474 = 0.0771$. Then
$S(3) = 1 - (0.1493 + 0.1038 + 0.0771) = 0.670$.

Cross-check: $S(3) = r_1 r_2 r_3 = 0.851 \times 0.878 \times 0.897 = 0.670$
with $r_3 = 5.806/6.474 = 0.897$: the two encodings agree, as the product
identity requires.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The High End likelihood used the counts (131, 126, 90, 60, 42, 34, 26; 491
still active). State which term of $LL(\alpha,\beta)$ is the censored one,
which cells of the cohort matrix each count comes from, and what would go
wrong if the 491 were dropped from the likelihood (fitted on the seven loss
counts alone, renormalized).

Then, for each situation, name the estimator the module supplies — the
product-limit (Kaplan–Meier) estimator of
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the previous lesson</Ref>, the
single-coin geometric, or the sBG — and the cue that decides: (a) a board
asks for year-12 survival of a cohort observed for seven years; (b) an
analyst pools staggered cohorts to describe survival over the observed
window, no projection asked; (c) a product's cohort shows retention rates
flat at 0.78 across seven years.

<Solution>
The censored term is $491 \times \ln S(7)$: the survivors' lifetimes are
known only to exceed 7, so they enter through the survivor function: the
same rule as <Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">the risk-set
arithmetic</Ref>, where a censored customer contributes exposure but never a
death.

Cohort-matrix locations: each loss count $n_t$ is the difference of two
adjacent cells in the cohort's row, $n_{c,t-1} - n_{c,t}$ (1,000 − 869 =
131, 869 − 743 = 126, and so on); the 491 is the row's last observed cell,
the one sitting on the staircase boundary where
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the matrix</Ref> meets the unobserved
triangle.

Dropping the survivors conditions the model on $T \le 7$: it fits the
distribution of lifetimes *given* that the customer churned in the window.
The sample is then selected for high $\theta$ (only churners are kept), the
fitted heterogeneity shifts toward high churn, and projected survival is
biased low: the same discard-the-censored error as the previous lesson's
$15/850$ estimate, now committed inside a parametric model.

Estimator choice. (a) The sBG: projecting past the window requires a
process model — the product-limit estimator stops at the staircase
boundary, and the second section showed what extrapolating fitted shapes
does. (b) Kaplan–Meier: description inside the window needs no functional
form, and the product-limit estimator already combines rows of different
lengths under censoring; a parametric model would add assumptions where
none are needed. (c) The single coin: a flat $r_t$ series is the
memoryless signature, so heterogeneity has nothing to explain (the
$\beta \to \infty$ limit of a later exercise is already the best fit) and
one parameter beats two. The deciding cues: describe or project, and
whether the $r_t$ series rises.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take $\beta \to \infty$ with the mean churn probability
$\alpha/(\alpha+\beta) = c$ held fixed. Show what happens to the variance of
$g(\theta)$, to $r_t$, and to the model; state which observed feature of the
data the limiting model can no longer produce.

<Solution>
The Beta variance is
$\mathrm{var}(\theta) = \dfrac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}
= \dfrac{c(1-c)}{\alpha+\beta+1} \to 0$: the density collapses to a point
mass at $c$, so every customer carries the same coin.

For the retention rate, write $r_t = 1 - \alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t-1)$. With
$\alpha = c(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\alpha + \beta \to \infty$, the subtracted
term is $c\,(\alpha+\beta)/(\alpha+\beta+t-1) \to c$ for every fixed $t$,
so $r_t \to 1 - c$: **constant in $t$**. The sBG degenerates to the
homogeneous shifted-geometric model.

The lost feature is the rise. A flat $r_t$ series cannot match the observed
climb ($0.631 \to 0.920$ for Regular), which is why the single-coin fit
failed in the lesson. The heterogeneity assumption is not decoration: the
variance of $g$ is exactly what buys a rising aggregate retention rate, and
in its absence the model reproduces none of the module's central
observation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Our renewal rate climbed from 60% to 85% over four
years, so our onboarding investments made customers more loyal." Produce the
same climb from constant individuals, then state what additional data would
separate the two stories.

<Solution>
Fit an sBG to the two endpoints. Require $r_1 = 1 - \alpha/(\alpha+\beta) =
0.60$ and $r_5 = 1 - \alpha/(\alpha+\beta+4) = 0.85$. The first gives
$\alpha = 0.4\,s$ with $s = \alpha+\beta$; substituting into the second:
$0.4s/(s+4) = 0.15$, so $0.25 s = 0.6$, $s = 2.4$, giving
$\alpha = 0.96$, $\beta = 1.44$. Check the path:
$r_2 = 1 - 0.96/3.4 = 0.72$, $r_3 = 0.78$, $r_4 = 0.82$, $r_5 = 0.85$: a
smooth 60→85 climb from customers whose individual probabilities never
move. The claimed inference fails at the step "aggregate rose, therefore
individuals changed": sorting produces the rise for free.

Separating data: compare cohorts **across vintages at the same age**. If
onboarding (which new customers experience once, at the start) built
loyalty, cohorts acquired after the investment should show higher $r_1$ and
$r_2$ than earlier cohorts did at the same ages; under pure sorting, every
vintage repeats the same age profile. Individual-level histories would also
do it (testing whether a given customer's hazard falls with tenure), but
the vintage comparison uses only the cohort matrix the company already has.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the distribution of $\theta$ among customers who have survived $t$
periods, show
$E[\theta \mid T > t] = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t)$, and verify it equals
$1 - r_{t+1}$. Interpret the result as Bayesian updating.

<Solution>
By Bayes' rule, the density of $\theta$ conditional on survival is

$$
g(\theta \mid T > t)
= \frac{S(t \mid \theta)\, g(\theta)}{S(t)}
\propto (1-\theta)^t \cdot \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}
= \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+t-1},
$$

which is the $\text{Beta}(\alpha,\ \beta+t)$ kernel, so
$g(\theta \mid T>t) = \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta+t)$ and its mean is
$\alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t)$.

From the theorem, $r_{t+1} = 1 - \alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t)$, so
$1 - r_{t+1} = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta+t) = E[\theta \mid T > t]$: next
period's aggregate churn rate is the posterior mean churn probability of
the current survivors.

Interpretation: each observed renewal is one Bernoulli "success" carrying
evidence that the customer's $\theta$ is low, and the Beta prior updates by
adding the survival count to its second parameter: conjugacy, exactly as
in a Beta-Bernoulli posterior. Sorting **is** Bayesian updating read at the
population level: the market performs the update physically, by deleting
the high-$\theta$ customers, and the analyst performs it algebraically.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For the High End segment ($\alpha = 0.668$, $\beta = 3.806$) and an annual
discount rate $d = 10\%$, compute the expected discounted number of future
renewals, $\sum_{t=1}^{\infty} S(t)/(1+d)^t$ (truncate the sum when the
terms stop mattering). Compare it with the "expected lifetime" a naive
calculation would use, $1/E(\theta)$, and state what each number would mean
attached to a per-period margin.

<Solution>
The recursion gives $S(t)$; the discounted sum converges geometrically
(each term is at most $S(t)/1.1^t$), and truncating at $t = 200$ is more
than enough at $d = 10\%$:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{S(t)}{1.1^t} \approx 4.92.
$$

The naive lifetime is $1/E(\theta) = 1/0.149 = 6.70$ years. It is wrong
twice over. First, it ignores discounting: a renewal in year 15 is not
worth a renewal in year 1. Second, under heterogeneity the sBG's actual
expected tenure is not $1/E(\theta)$ at all: with $\alpha = 0.668 < 1$,
$S(t)$ decays like $t^{-0.668}$ and $\sum_t S(t)$ **diverges**: the fitted
expected tenure is infinite, because the reverse-J puts non-negligible mass
on customers with $\theta$ near zero who effectively never leave. The
discounted sum is finite anyway: discounting caps what the far tail can
contribute.

Attached to a per-period margin $m$ (paid at each renewal), $m \times 4.92$
is the expected present value of the customer's future renewals; $m \times
6.70$ is a number with no consistent interpretation: an undiscounted
lifetime the model itself contradicts. The first object is, up to the
margin convention, the customer lifetime value that
<Ref lesson="customer-lifetime-value">the next lesson</Ref> defines as
$E(\mathrm{CLV}) = \sum_t m\,S(t)/(1+d)^t$, and the gap between the two
numbers is that lesson's subject.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Every quantity in this course is computed from one object: the log of who did
what, when — rolled up into a funnel and a cohort matrix.

This is the opening move of the course's argument. The course claims that the
vocabulary of a subscription business (retention, churn, lifetime value,
acquisition cost) names statistical models fitted to data the company already
has, and before any model can be fitted the data object has to be fixed. This
lesson fixes it: the event log, the funnel computed from it, the cohort × age
matrix that every later lesson fits models to, and the notation the whole
course reuses. <Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">The next lesson</Ref>
reads the matrix's rows as survival data.

The route: the event log and the running case, funnels and the discipline
their conversion rates require, cohorts and their two time axes, the cohort
matrix and its three reads, and the reason blended aggregates mislead.

## The event log is the primary object

First on the route is the object every later stop is computed from. A
subscription software company (*SaaS*: software sold as a recurring
subscription rather than a one-time license) records its history as events.
Each event is one row with four fields: a customer identifier, an event type,
a timestamp, and an amount where money moved. Visits, trial signups,
activations, payments, cancellations: everything the business knows about
its customers is some set of these rows.

<Figure
  src="./figures/event-log.svg"
  alt="A six-row event log on the left; arrows fan out to three aggregations: a funnel, a cohort matrix, and an MRR ledger"
  caption="One log, three roll-ups: order events within a customer (funnel), group customers by signup month (cohort matrix), sum amounts by calendar month (MRR)."
/>

The course works one dataset throughout. **The running case:** Relay, a
fictional invoicing tool priced at \$50 per month, selling since January
2025 and observed through June 30, 2025; every worked number in this module
is a roll-up of its log. Three roll-ups matter:

- **Funnel**: order the event types within a customer's history and count
  how many customers reach each one. The conversion view.
- **Cohort matrix**: group customers by signup month and count who is still
  active each month after. The retention view.
- **MRR ledger**: sum payment amounts by calendar month. *MRR*, monthly
  recurring revenue, is the revenue view.

<KeyFact>
Every metric in this course (retention curves, churn, CLV, LTV:CAC, NRR) is
a grouping and a count or sum over these four fields. A metric whose
definition cannot be written as a query against the event log is not
measurable, and every definitional dispute later in the course (who counts as
active, over which window, with which denominator) is a dispute about the
query.
</KeyFact>

## Funnels: conversion rates multiply along the path

The first roll-up orders events inside one customer's history. Relay's path
to revenue has four states, and April 2025 put concrete numbers on each:
40,000 site visits produced 2,000 trial signups; 1,400 of those signups
activated (created their first invoice) within 14 days; 350 paid within 30
days of signing up. Each hand-off has a rate: 5.0% of visits become signups
in the same month, 70% of signups activate within 14 days, 25% of activated
trials pay within 30 days.

<Figure
  src="./figures/funnel.svg"
  alt="Four funnel stages with counts, per-hand-off conversion rates with their denominators and windows on the arrows, and the end-to-end product below"
  caption="Relay's April funnel. Every rate carries its denominator and window; the rates multiply to the end-to-end conversion."
/>

<Definition title="funnel, conversion rate">
A **funnel** is an ordered sequence of customer states. The **conversion
rate** of a hand-off is the fraction of units entering a stage that reach the
next one, measured over a stated window and against a stated denominator.
</Definition>

The rates multiply, and the reason is visible in the fractions themselves:

$$
\frac{350}{40{,}000}
= \frac{2{,}000}{40{,}000} \times \frac{1{,}400}{2{,}000} \times \frac{350}{1{,}400}
= 0.050 \times 0.70 \times 0.25 = 0.00875.
$$

Each denominator cancels the previous numerator, so the product telescopes to
the end-to-end rate: **0.875%** of April's visitors became paying customers.
The cancellation is also the condition: it works only because each rate's
denominator is exactly the previous stage's converts, and each window sits
inside the end-to-end window.

A rate stated without its denominator and window ("conversion is 3%") is
not a number that can enter this product. Relay's 5.0% (of visits, same
month) and its 25% (of activated trials, within 30 days of signup) have
different denominators, different windows, and different levers behind them;
a report that drops the qualifiers invites multiplying rates that do not
compose.

<KeyFact>
Rates read off calendar-month aggregates violate both conditions at once:
numerator and denominator draw from different entering cohorts, and the
windows do not nest. The resulting bias is not small; the calendar-window
exercise at the end of the lesson constructs a case where the measured rate
understates the true rate by a quarter.
</KeyFact>

### Running the funnel backwards

The same product, read right to left, turns a target into a requirement.

<Example title="forward planning from a customer target">
Relay wants 600 new paying customers in September. Walking the funnel
backwards at April's rates:

$$
\frac{600}{0.25} = 2{,}400 \text{ activations}, \quad
\frac{2{,}400}{0.70} \approx 3{,}429 \text{ trial signups}, \quad
\frac{3{,}429}{0.050} \approx 68{,}600 \text{ visits}.
$$

Equivalently in one step: $600 / 0.00875 \approx 68{,}600$. September needs
roughly 69,000 visits, against April's 40,000. The number is a planning
input: it prices the gap in traffic that marketing has to close.
</Example>

The full machinery of decomposing a target into owned driver metrics is
<Ref lesson="north-star-and-metric-trees">a later lesson</Ref>; here the
one-line version suffices.

<Exercise>
Relay's onboarding team believes it can raise activation from 70% to 80% by
September. At the improved rate, how many visits does the 600-customer target
require, and by what fraction does the improvement cut the requirement?
Then state which single-stage change would cut the required visits in half.

<Solution>
The end-to-end rate becomes $0.050 \times 0.80 \times 0.25 = 0.010$, so the
requirement is $600 / 0.010 = 60{,}000$ visits, about 8,600 fewer. The cut
is exactly the ratio of the old rate to the new: $1 - 0.70/0.80 = 12.5\%$.

Halving the required visits means doubling the end-to-end rate, and the
product is linear in each factor, so doubling any single stage rate does it:
5.0% → 10% on signup, or 25% → 50% on payment (70% → 140% is not available).
The stages are interchangeable in the arithmetic; they differ only in what a
doubling costs, which is a question the arithmetic cannot answer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Cohorts: age is not calendar time

The funnel ends at "paid," but the customer's history continues: they pay
monthly until they stop. The second roll-up groups customers by when they
were acquired. A *cohort* is the set of customers who signed up in the same
period: Relay's January cohort, February cohort, and so on. A cohort is
tracked by *age*: the number of periods since its acquisition month, written
$t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

The two time axes do not coincide. The February cohort at age 1 is March on
the calendar; the May cohort at age 1 is June. Comparing cells at the same
age compares different calendar months, and comparing cells in the same
calendar month compares different ages. Keeping the axes separate is most of
what cohort analysis is.

### Building the matrix from the log

<Definition title="cohort matrix">
The **cohort matrix** is the table with one row per cohort $c$ and one column
per age $t$, whose cell $n_{c,t}$ counts the cohort's customers still active
$t$ periods after acquisition. The cohort's size is $n_{c,0}$, and the
fraction still active at age $t$ is $n_{c,t}/n_{c,0}$. The cell $(c,t)$ falls
in calendar period $c + t$.
</Definition>

For Relay, "active in a month" means at least one payment event in that
month. The matrix is built by two passes over the log: assign each customer
to a cohort by their signup month, then for each cohort count active
customers at each age.

### The unobserved triangle is the future

The empty lower-right triangle is the matrix's defining feature. January has
been observed for six months and has six cells; June has one. The triangle is
not missing data to be imputed: those months have not happened. Every live
customer base has this shape, and any method that consumes cohort data has to
work on a staircase, not a rectangle.
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">The next lesson</Ref> gives the
condition its proper name, censoring, and shows what estimating a curve from
a staircase requires.

<Aside>
Three choices sit inside the matrix's construction, and each changes cell
values without changing the object's shape. Bucket width: monthly cohorts
suit a \$50/month product; weekly buckets suit a consumer app where the first
days decide everything. Activity definition: "made a payment" differs from
"logged in," and for Relay the payment definition is the one wired to
revenue. Age convention: calendar-month ages (used here) put a customer who
signed up on January 31 at age 1 the next day; strict 30-day ages avoid that
at the cost of splitting cohorts across calendar months. Reports that
disagree usually disagree in these choices, not in the log.
</Aside>

## One matrix, three reads

Each direction through the matrix holds one variable fixed, and each answers
a different question.

<Figure
  src="./figures/matrix-reads.svg"
  alt="The same staircase matrix drawn three times: a row highlighted for one cohort aging, a column highlighted for one age across vintages, and the diagonal highlighted for one calendar month"
  caption="Row: one cohort ages (retention). Column: same age, successive vintages (product changes). Diagonal: one calendar month (MRR)."
/>

### The row read: one cohort ages

A row is one cohort's whole recorded life. The canonical example, used
across this module, is an annual-contract business that acquired 1,000
customers at the start of Year 1 and observed renewals for four years:

<Example title="reading one cohort's row">
The row is 1,000 → 631 → 468 → 382 → 326. As fractions of the cohort:
1.000, 0.631, 0.468, 0.382, 0.326. The period-to-period retention rates,
each year's survivors as a fraction of the previous year's, are

$$
\frac{631}{1{,}000} = 63.1\%,\quad
\frac{468}{631} = 74.2\%,\quad
\frac{382}{468} = 81.6\%,\quad
\frac{326}{382} = 85.3\%.
$$

The fraction surviving falls, and the retention rate **rises** every year.
Both facts come from the same five numbers; which one a report shows decides
what the reader concludes.
</Example>

The rise is not a property of this dataset. Cohort-level retention rates
rise with age almost everywhere they are measured: regional-magazine renewal
rates run near 30% at the first renewal, 50% at the second, and 75% for
longtime readers. This cohort returns in
<Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">the churn lesson</Ref>, which fits a
model to the row and settles what the rise does and does not mean.

<Exercise>
Read Relay's February row (240, 154, 122, 106, 96) the same way: compute the
fraction still active at each age and the four period-to-period retention
rates. State whether the February cohort shows the same signature as the
annual-contract cohort.

<Solution>
Fractions of the cohort: $240/240 = 1.000$, $154/240 = 0.642$,
$122/240 = 0.508$, $106/240 = 0.442$, $96/240 = 0.400$.

Retention rates: $154/240 = 64.2\%$, $122/154 = 79.2\%$, $106/122 = 86.9\%$,
$96/106 = 90.6\%$.

Same signature: the surviving fraction falls toward a flattening tail while
the retention rate rises every month (64.2% → 90.6% over four hand-offs).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### The column read: same age, successive vintages

A column compares cohorts at the same age. Because age is held fixed, any
movement down a column is a difference between *vintages*: the state of the
product, onboarding, pricing, and customer mix at each cohort's acquisition
date. The column read is how product changes are audited: one SaaS company's
first-month churn fell from
[15% for its January cohort to **4%** for its July cohort](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/)
after fixes to onboarding and early product experience. The improvement is real and
attributable precisely because every compared cell sits at the same age; the
same 11-point difference smeared into an aggregate would be unreadable.

### The diagonal read: one calendar month

Cells with equal $c + t$ share a calendar month. Summing the June diagonal of
Relay's matrix (each cohort's newest cell) counts everyone active in June:

$$
70 + 96 + 138 + 193 + 280 + 460 = 1{,}237 \text{ active customers},
$$

which at \$50 per month is June MRR of \$61,850. The diagonal is where the
cohort view and the accounting view meet: a company's headline "active
customers" and MRR numbers are always diagonal sums, one calendar period cut
across every cohort's age.

<Task>
Compute the May diagonal the same way: the May-dated cell of each cohort
acquired by May. The sum should land at 979 active customers and \$48,950 of
MRR.
</Task>

Replacing the count $n_{c,t}$ by the cohort's summed payment amounts gives
the *revenue cohort matrix*, the second of the two standard cohort views.
The two matrices move differently once per-customer amounts vary: a cohort's
revenue row can rise while its count row falls, when expansion by the
survivors outweighs the leavers.
<Ref lesson="mrr-nrr-and-negative-churn">The MRR lesson</Ref> computes that
case.

<KeyFact>
Rising within-cohort retention rates are the near-universal signature of
cohort data: 63.1% → 85.3% in the annual-contract cohort, 64.2% → 90.6% in
Relay's February row. Two rival explanations fit it: customers grow more
loyal with tenure, or the churn-prone leave first and the average survivor
was always stickier.
<Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">The sBG lesson</Ref> shows the second
explanation suffices with no individual changing at all, and choosing
between them is what the module's models are for.
</KeyFact>

## Blended aggregates mix age with vintage

Each of the three reads holds one axis fixed. A *blended* metric holds
neither: it pools every cohort into one number. Relay's blended monthly
retention (customers active this month who were also active last month,
divided by last month's actives) is

$$
\text{Apr} \to \text{May}: \frac{579}{754} = 76.8\%,
\qquad
\text{May} \to \text{Jun}: \frac{777}{979} = 79.4\%.
$$

### The blend moves when the mix moves

The blend rose 2.6 points. What it measures is a weighted average of
age-specific retention rates, weighted by each cohort's share of last
month's base: the May→June figure mixes January's age-5 rate (92.1%) with
May's age-1 rate (70.0%), and the weights shift every month as new cohorts
enter and old ones shrink.

The tempting conclusion, that the blend rose because the product got
stickier, is wrong. The blend moves when the **mix** moves. A company whose
every cohort behaves identically forever will still see its blended
retention climb whenever acquisition slows (the base tenures up) and sink
whenever acquisition surges (young cohorts at their lowest-retention ages
flood the denominator). The extreme case: a two-cohort company with no new
acquisition at all, one cohort retaining a constant 85% of its actives each
month and the other a constant 95%, sees its blend rise from 88.0% to 90.9%
within a year, because the worse-retained cohort shrinks faster and the
weights drift toward the better one. The mix-shift exercise at the end of
the lesson works the arithmetic.

  caption="No cohort changes; the blend rises anyway. Differential shrinkage shifts the weights toward the better-retained cohort."

### The pooled curve matches no cohort

<Figure
  src="./figures/pooled-vs-cohort.svg"
  alt="Cohort-specific retention rate curves each rising steeply with age, plotted against calendar year, while the pooled aggregate retention rate stays nearly flat around 0.71"
  caption="Every cohort's retention rate rises with age; the pooled aggregate barely moves. Continuous acquisition keeps the base young, and the blend reports the mix. (After Fader & Hardie, ART Forum tutorial, 2016.)"
/>

The construction: a business acquires a new cohort every year for ten
years, and each cohort's retention rate rises from 63% toward 90% as it
ages. The aggregate rate computed on the pooled base starts at 63%, climbs
only while the first cohorts age in, and settles near 71% by year five;
from then on it barely moves. The aggregate is flatter than **every**
cohort's own curve: it is not a summary of them but a different quantity,
pinned down by the perpetual inflow of young cohorts.

A membership society's newsletter, the *ION Newsletter* (Winter 2011–2012),
supplies the specimen: "I am
happy to report that 41% of new members who joined in 2011 renewed their
membership in 2012, and that ION has an overall retention of 78%." Both
numbers are true. The 78% is propped up by long-tenured members at their
high age-specific rates and says nothing about the new-member experience;
the 41% is the number that describes it, and it is a row read: the 2011
cohort at age 1.

### What is established

The event log of (customer, event, timestamp, amount) is the primary object.
A funnel is its conversion roll-up, with rates that multiply only under
stated denominators and nested windows. The cohort matrix $n_{c,t}$ is its
retention roll-up, with age and calendar as separate axes and an unobserved
triangle where the future has not happened. Rows, columns, and diagonals
answer different questions; blended aggregates answer none of them cleanly.

One caution carries forward: the matrix records what happened, not what
customers are disposed to do. In Fader and Hardie's phrase, the analyst sees
the underlying propensities only through a
["foggy window"](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Fader_hardie_jim_09.pdf),
and a customer who made two purchases last year is not thereby a
"two-per-year buyer."
<Ref lesson="retention-and-survival">The next lesson</Ref> reads the
matrix's rows as survival data: the fraction-still-active sequence becomes a
survivor function, the unobserved triangle becomes censoring, and the
period-to-period rates computed here get their standard names.

<Exercise>
Relay's June cohort (460 customers) has exactly one filled cell. State its
retention curve as observed so far. Then evaluate the claim "our retention
improved this month — look at the June cohort," and state what the earliest
date is at which June's month-1 retention can be compared against May's.

<Solution>
The observed curve is the single point $n_{c,0}/n_{c,0} = 1.00$ at age 0.
Every cohort in history passes through this point by construction, so the
curve as observed carries no information about retention at all.

The claim is empty: no June-cohort cell at age ≥ 1 exists, so no retention
rate for the June cohort has been observed. The first comparable number is
June's age-1 count, which falls in calendar July and is observable only
after July closes; at that point $n_{\text{Jun},1}/460$ can be set against
May's $280/400 = 70\%$. Until then, any "this month" retention claim is
either a diagonal read (a calendar statement mixing every cohort's age) or a
statement about older cohorts, not about June.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim with a construction: "Our blended monthly retention rose
from 88% to 91%, so the product got stickier." Build a two-cohort company
that acquires no new customers, in which each cohort's monthly retention
rate is constant forever, yet the blend rises from 88.0% to 90.9%. Name the
mechanism.

<Solution>
Let cohort A retain 85% of its actives each month and cohort B retain 95%,
both constant at every age, with no new customers entering. Blended
retention in any month is the active-weighted average of the two rates.

Start with 700 A-actives and 300 B-actives:
$$
\frac{0.85 \times 700 + 0.95 \times 300}{1{,}000}
= \frac{595 + 285}{1{,}000} = 88.0\%.
$$

Each month A shrinks by the factor 0.85 and B by 0.95, so the weights drift
toward B. After 11 months, $700 \times 0.85^{11} \approx 117$ A-actives and
$300 \times 0.95^{11} \approx 171$ B-actives remain:
$$
\frac{0.85 \times 117 + 0.95 \times 171}{288}
= \frac{99.45 + 162.45}{288} = 90.9\%.
$$

No rate changed; only the weights did. A's share of the base fell from 70%
to 41% because the worse-retained cohort shrinks faster, so a closed base
tenures toward its better-retained members. The mechanism is **mix shift**:
a blend is a weighted average of age- and segment-specific rates, and
differential shrinkage alone moves the weights toward the better-retained
group. Any inference from a blend to product change must first hold the mix
fixed, which is exactly what a per-cohort read does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The telescoping product in the funnel section required each rate's
denominator to be the previous stage's converts. Break it: suppose 5% of any
month's visitors sign up, half in the same calendar month and half in the
next, and visits are growing: March 10,000, April 20,000, May 40,000. An
analyst computes April's "signup rate" as April signups over April visits.
Compute that rate, compare it with the true 5%, state what happens to the
measured rate if traffic is flat instead, and give the general condition
under which stage rates multiply to end-to-end conversion.

<Solution>
April's signups are half of April's eventual converts plus the delayed half
of March's: $0.025 \times 20{,}000 + 0.025 \times 10{,}000 = 500 + 250 =
750$. The measured rate is $750 / 20{,}000 = 3.75\%$, understating the true
5% by a quarter. May gives the same figure: $(1{,}000 + 500)/40{,}000 =
3.75\%$. Under steady doubling the bias is stable, so it does not even
announce itself as a trend break.

Flat traffic at 20,000: $500 + 500 = 1{,}000$ signups, measured rate
$1{,}000/20{,}000 = 5.0\%$, which is correct. The calendar ratio equals the true
rate only when inflow is constant; under growth the denominator has already
grown while the delayed numerator still reflects the smaller past.

The general condition: fix one entering cohort and follow it. End-to-end
conversion is $(\text{paid within } W)/(\text{entered})$, and it factors as
a product of stage rates exactly when each rate is measured on the previous
stage's converts from that same cohort (denominators nest, so the product
telescopes) and each stage window is contained in $W$ (so no convert is
counted outside the end-to-end window, and none twice). Calendar-aggregate
rates violate the nesting: their numerators and denominators draw from
different cohorts.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The table gives monthly churn (the percentage of a cohort's remaining
customers lost in a given month of life) in the format of the cohort table
in Skok's SaaS Metrics 2.0. The Month-1 endpoints, 15% and 4%, are Skok's

| Cohort | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 | Month 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Jan | 15% | 9% | 7% | 6% |
| Mar | 10% | 9% | 7% | — |
| May | 6% | 7% | — | — |
| Jul | 4% | — | — | — |

Decide whether the January-to-July improvement is a vintage effect or an age

<Solution>
It is a vintage effect. The 15% → 10% → 6% → 4% series runs down the
Month-1 **column**: all four cells sit at the same age (first month of life),
so age is held fixed and the only thing that varies is the acquisition
vintage, the state of onboarding and product at each cohort's start.

The age effect is a different read: along a **row**, January's churn falls
15% → 9% → 7% → 6% as the cohort ages, which is the ordinary rising-retention
signature and would occur with no product change at all.

A fuller audit compares the later columns across vintages: a fix aimed at
onboarding should concentrate in Month 1 and leave Month 2 onward roughly
flat, while a uniform downward shift of entire rows would mean the change
acts at every age, not at the onboarding hand-off.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For Relay's January cohort (200, 124, 98, 84, 76, 70), compute two series:
the fraction of the original cohort surviving at each age, and the fraction
of the previous month's survivors lost in each month. State which of the two
series looks closer to constant, and what a forecast of month 6 would have
to assume.

<Solution>
Surviving fractions: $124/200 = 0.620$, $98/200 = 0.490$, $84/200 = 0.420$,
$76/200 = 0.380$, $70/200 = 0.350$.

Fraction of survivors lost each month: $(200-124)/200 = 38.0\%$,
$(124-98)/124 = 21.0\%$, $(98-84)/98 = 14.3\%$, $(84-76)/84 = 9.5\%$,
$(76-70)/76 = 7.9\%$.

Neither series is constant, and the loss series is the more structured of
the two: it falls steeply, then its decline slows (38 → 21 → 14 → 9.5 →
7.9), while the survivor series just shrinks by ever-smaller steps. A
month-6 forecast must assume something about the next loss fraction:
extrapolating the last value (7.9%) and extrapolating the trend give
different answers, and nothing computed in this lesson says which is right.

The two series are the module's central objects under their working names:
the survivor fractions are the survivor function $S(t)$, and the per-month

A retention curve is a survival curve: the fraction of a cohort still active
at each age, estimated from data in which most lifetimes are unfinished.

<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">The previous lesson</Ref> fixed the course's
data object: the cohort matrix, with age and calendar time as separate axes
and an unobserved triangle where the future has not happened. Its rows were
already read as fractions still active, and the period-to-period rates
computed there rose with age. This lesson supplies the mathematics of the row
read: the survivor function, the retention rate, the product identity
connecting them, and the estimator that combines rows of different lengths
without corrupting the answer. It establishes that the rates rise but does
not explain the rise; that explanation, and the projection of a curve beyond
its observed window, is what <Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">the churn
lesson</Ref> contributes next.

The route: the survivor function and the retention rate and the product that
ties them, censoring and the estimate it wrecks, the product-limit estimator
(starred), the independence assumption inside every pooled risk set, and the
diagnostics a curve's shape supports.

## One row, two encodings

The row to be formalized is the one the previous lesson called the canonical
cohort: an annual-contract business acquires 1,000 customers at the start of
Year 1, and the successive year-end counts are 1,000, 631, 468, 382, 326
(<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels" id="the-row-read-one-cohort-ages">the row
read</Ref>).

<Task>
Before reading on, recompute two numbers from the previous lesson, closed
book: the fraction of this cohort still active after three renewals, and the
third-year retention rate. They should land at $382/1{,}000 = 0.382$ and
$382/468 = 81.6\%$.
</Task>

Throughout this module the setting is *contractual*: the customer holds a
subscription, cancellation is an observed event, and "retained at age $t$"
means **has not yet cancelled**, not "was active in period $t$". Under this
definition a customer's *lifetime* $T$ is the number of periods completed
before cancellation, and what a cohort row records is the distribution of
$T$.

<Definition title="survivor function, retention rate">
For a lifetime $T$ measured in periods, the **survivor function** is
$$
S(t) = \Pr(T > t), \qquad t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$
with $S(0) = 1$. The **retention rate** of period $t$ is the conditional
probability of surviving period $t$ given survival so far:
$$
r_t = \Pr(T > t \mid T > t-1) = \frac{S(t)}{S(t-1)}.
$$
</Definition>

The two encodings determine each other, exactly as the funnel's conversion
rates multiplied out to the end-to-end rate in
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the previous lesson</Ref>: a cohort's life
is a funnel whose stages are ages.

<Theorem title="product identity">
For any lifetime distribution with $S(t-1) > 0$ (so that every rate through
$r_t$ is defined),
$$
S(t) = r_1 r_2 \cdots r_t.
$$
<Proof>
The events $\{T > 0\} \supset \{T > 1\} \supset \cdots \supset \{T > t\}$
are nested, so each factor $r_i = S(i)/S(i-1)$ exists ($S$ is
nonincreasing, hence $S(i-1) \ge S(t-1) > 0$), and the product telescopes:
$$
r_1 r_2 \cdots r_t
= \frac{S(1)}{S(0)} \cdot \frac{S(2)}{S(1)} \cdots \frac{S(t)}{S(t-1)}
= S(t).
$$
Nothing else is assumed: the identity is bookkeeping, valid for every
lifetime distribution.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Example title="the canonical cohort, both encodings">
As fractions of the 1,000 acquired, the survivor function is

$$
S(0) = 1, \quad S(1) = 0.631, \quad S(2) = 0.468, \quad S(3) = 0.382,
\quad S(4) = 0.326.
$$

The retention rates are the ratios of successive values:

$$
r_1 = \frac{0.631}{1} = 0.631, \quad
r_2 = \frac{0.468}{0.631} = 0.742, \quad
r_3 = \frac{0.382}{0.468} = 0.816, \quad
r_4 = \frac{0.326}{0.382} = 0.853.
$$

The product check runs the identity forward:
$0.631 \times 0.742 \times 0.816 \times 0.853 = 0.326 = S(4)$.
</Example>

  caption="The bridge: one row of the cohort matrix, replotted against age, is the empirical survivor function."

The two encodings carry identical information, but they do not survive messy
data equally well. $S(t)$ needs the whole row from a single cohort; each
$r_t$ needs only one **transition**: who was there at age $t-1$, who was
still there at age $t$. Transitions can be counted wherever they are
observed, in any cohort old enough to show them. That difference is why the
rest of this lesson works with rates.

<KeyFact>
The product identity is the module's load-bearing equation: every method
that estimates or models retention (the pooled estimator below, the sBG
model of <Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">the next lesson</Ref>, the
CLV formula after that) computes conditional survival period by period and
multiplies.
</KeyFact>

<Remark title="what is not a survivor function">
Consumer products often report "week-4 retention": the fraction of a cohort
active *in* week 4, under some activity definition. That series can rise,
since a dormant user can return, so it violates the one structural property the
definition imposes, that $T > t$ events are nested and $S$ never increases.
It is a legitimate metric but not a survivor function, and none of this
lesson's machinery applies to it. The noncontractual setting, where
cancellation is never observed at all, gets its own machinery in
<Ref lesson="repeat-purchase-models">the repeat-purchase lesson</Ref>.
</Remark>

Relay, <Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the running case</Ref> (the
fictional \$50-per-month invoicing tool observed January through June 2025),
has a March cohort with counts 300, 198, 159, 138 at ages 0–3.
Compute its survivor function and retention rates, and verify the product
identity at $t = 3$.

<Solution>
Survivor function: $S(0) = 1$, $S(1) = 198/300 = 0.660$,
$S(2) = 159/300 = 0.530$, $S(3) = 138/300 = 0.460$.

Retention rates: $r_1 = 0.660$, $r_2 = 159/198 = 0.803$,
$r_3 = 138/159 = 0.868$.

Check: $0.660 \times 0.803 \times 0.868 = 0.460 = S(3)$. The rates rise with
age, as in every Relay row so far.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Censoring: an unfinished lifetime is information

The definitions above assumed a finished row. The matrix's staircase says
most rows are not: Relay's May cohort has one observed transition, June's has
none, and every live business is in this position permanently. A customer
still active when the observation window closes has a lifetime known only
partially: it exceeds the observed value.

<Definition title="censoring">
An observation is **censored** at age $L$ when the event of interest
(cancellation) has not occurred by $L$ and the observation ends there. The
recorded value says $T > L$: a lower bound, not a missing value and not an
event.
</Definition>

The obvious computation, survivors divided by everyone whose status at the
target age is known (the censored set aside as unusable), does
not degrade gracefully on censored data: it produces results Kaplan and
Meier call absurd. The founding paper of the field,
[Kaplan and Meier (1958)](https://web.stanford.edu/~lutian/coursepdf/KMpaper.pdf),
opens with a two-sample example built to make the failure undeniable.

<Example title="two samples, one estimate (Kaplan & Meier 1958, §1.3)">
A random sample of 100 items goes on test at the start of 1955; during the
year 70 die and 30 survive. At the start of 1956 a second sample of 1,000
items is added. During 1956, 15 items of the first sample and 750 of the
second die, leaving 15 two-year survivors and 250 one-year survivors. The
analysis date is the end of 1956; wanted is $S(2)$, the probability of
surviving two years.

| | Sample I (from 1955) | Sample II (from 1956) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial number | 100 | 1,000 |
| Died in first year of age | 70 | 750 |
| Survived one year | 30 | 250 |
| Died in second year of age | 15 | — window closed |
| Survived two years | 15 | — window closed |

Sample II's 250 survivors are censored at age one: alive, observed for one
year, lifetimes known only to exceed it.

**The pooled estimate.** Only 15 items out of everything on test survived
two years. Setting aside the 250 censored items as unusable leaves
$1{,}100 - 250 = 850$, and

$$
\hat{S}(2) = \frac{15}{850} \approx 0.018,
$$

an absurd result: sample I alone, a complete two-year sample, gives
$15/100 = 0.15$, eight times larger. The 850-item version discarded exactly
the items that had survived their first year, so its denominator is loaded
with early deaths.

**The product estimate.** Both samples observed the first year of age, so
the first-year rate pools:

$$
\hat{r}_1 = \frac{30 + 250}{100 + 1{,}000} = 0.255.
$$

Only sample I observed a second year, so the second-year rate comes from its
30 one-year survivors alone: $\hat{r}_2 = 15/30 = 0.50$. The product
identity assembles the answer:

$$
\hat{S}(2) = \hat{r}_1 \times \hat{r}_2 = 0.255 \times 0.50 = 0.127.
$$
</Example>

  alt="Four frames: two cohorts drawn as bars over an age axis with one window ending early; the discarded-censored estimate 15/850 marked wrong; the pooled first-year rate 0.255; the second-year conditional rate 0.50 and the product 0.127"
  caption="Kaplan and Meier's two-sample example: the censored-discarding estimate first, then the product of conditional rates."
/>

<Task>
Step through the four frames and track where each number's denominator comes
from. Frame 2's denominator (850) mixes items observed for one year with
items observed for two; frame 3's (1,100) counts only first-year exposure;
frame 4's (30) counts only items that both survived a year and stayed
observable. The repair is entirely a matter of matching numerators to the
population actually at risk.
</Task>

The set of customers eligible to churn at a given age (still active, still
under observation) is called the *risk set* at that age, and the repair
above has one rule: each rate uses its **own** risk set. A censored lifetime
enters every risk set up to its bound and no risk set after. It is neither
discarded nor counted as churned; it simply stops contributing when the data
about it stops.

How far a rate can be trusted is a sample-size question with a standard
answer: a rate $\hat{r}$ computed on a risk set of $n$ customers carries a
binomial standard error of $\sqrt{\hat{r}(1-\hat{r})/n}$, about 1.7
percentage points for a rate near 0.7 pooled over 750 customers (the Relay
computation below) and over 20 points on a risk set of four. The working
rule this module adopts: distrust every value of $\hat{S}$ from the first
rate computed on **fewer than about five at-risk customers**, where one
cancellation moves the rate by twenty points or more and the standard error
swamps the estimate.

<KeyFact>
For a growing business the censored share is not a nuisance term but most of
the dataset: every cohort younger than the horizon of interest is censored at
its current age. An analysis that drops or "churns" censored customers is
biased downward by construction, and the faster the company grows, the worse
the bias: the young, mostly-censored cohorts dominate the base.
</KeyFact>

product estimate, which sharpens it with May's 400 additional first-year

</Solution>

## ★ The product-limit estimator

The two-sample trick (pool each conditional rate over its own risk set,
then multiply) generalizes to any number of cohorts, any pattern of
censoring, and timestamps of any precision. The general form is the
*product-limit estimator*, now universally called the Kaplan–Meier estimator
after the 1958 paper.

Order the observed cancellation ages $t_1 < t_2 < \cdots$. Let $n_j$ be the
risk set just before $t_j$, still active and still observed, and $d_j$ the
number cancelling at $t_j$. Censored customers leave the risk set at their
own ages and appear in no $d_j$. Then

$$
\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{j:\; t_j \le t} \frac{n_j - d_j}{n_j}.
$$

Each factor is one period's retention rate computed on exactly the customers
who could have churned then; between cancellation ages the estimate is
constant, so $\hat{S}$ is a step function that drops only where a
cancellation was observed.

<Example title="eight customers by hand (Kaplan & Meier 1958, Table 464)">
Eight customers, timestamps in months. Cancellations at 0.8, 3.1, 5.4, 9.2;
censored (still active when their windows closed) at 1.0, 2.7, 7.0, 12.1.

| Cancellation age $t_j$ | At risk $n_j$ | Factor $(n_j - d_j)/n_j$ | $\hat{S}(t_j)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.8 | 8 | 7/8 | 7/8 = 0.875 |
| 3.1 | 5 | 4/5 | 7/10 = 0.700 |
| 5.4 | 4 | 3/4 | 21/40 = 0.525 |
| 9.2 | 2 | 1/2 | 21/80 = 0.2625 |

The risk-set column is the whole computation. All 8 are at risk at 0.8. The
censorings at 1.0 and 2.7 remove two, so 5 are at risk at 3.1. The
cancellation itself leaves 4 at risk at 5.4; the censoring at 7.0 removes
one more, leaving 2 at risk at 9.2. After 9.2 one customer remains, censored
at 12.1, so beyond 12.1 the estimate is undefined: the last lifetime is
unfinished, and $\hat{S}(t)$ for $t > 12.1$ is known only to lie between 0
and 0.2625.
</Example>

### What the paper proves

Three facts complete the picture, stated here and proved in the paper.

- **It is the nonparametric MLE.** Among all distributions, the product-limit
  estimate maximizes the likelihood of the observed deaths and censorings
  (Kaplan & Meier 1958, §5): not one heuristic among several but the
  censored-data analogue of the empirical distribution.
- **The five-at-risk rule has a bound behind it.** The paper bounds the
  probability of an undefined tail by $e^{-\bar{N}(t)}$, already under 1%
  while the expected number still at risk is five (§2.2, eq. 2d): the
  censoring section's working rule marks where the estimator starts running
  out of data entirely, not merely where its variance grows.
- **Discrete data needs no new machinery.** Monthly cohort data has
  cancellations in month buckets, and for a single cohort with censoring
  only at the window's edge every factor cancels: $\hat{S}(t) = n_t/n_0$,
  the simple division the first section already did. The estimator earns its
  place exactly when observation windows differ (the standing condition of
  the staircase matrix) or when timestamps are fine enough that "the risk
  set at this instant" matters.

## Independent censoring is an assumption

Every pooled risk set in this lesson leaned on an unstated premise: that the
customers censored early would have gone on to survive like the customers
observed longer. Pooling a risk set assumes, in Kaplan and Meier's
formulation (§3.2), that for a customer censored at age $L$ the conditional
survival beyond $L$ equals that of customers whose windows extend past
$L$: lifetime and censoring age are independent.

In cohort data, censoring age is cohort age: who gets censored at $L$ is
exactly the cohorts acquired $L$ periods before the analysis date. So the
assumption fails whenever survival depends on vintage. Kaplan and Meier's
example is a change in surgical technique five years before the data are
analyzed, which alters survival only for patients with observation limits
under five years; the business translation is a pricing change three months
before Relay's June 30 analysis date, which alters the early ages of recent
cohorts only. The pooled
$\hat{r}_1$ then mixes a pre-change rate (from old cohorts) with a
post-change rate (from young ones), and no reweighting of the pool can
separate them: age and vintage effects **entangle** inside every factor.

### The diagnostic: estimate per cohort, compare

The check is the one the paper itself prescribes and the previous
lesson already built: group by observation limit, that is by cohort;
estimate separately; compare. A vintage effect shows up as rows that
disagree at the same age, which is the
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels" id="the-column-read-same-age-successive-vintages">column
read</Ref>. "Disagree" means beyond sampling noise: two cohorts' rates at
the same age differ by luck alone by about the root sum of their squared
standard errors. For Relay's first-year rates, April's $238/350 = 0.680$
against May's $280/400 = 0.700$, that yardstick is about 3.4 percentage
points, so the observed 2-point gap licenses pooling; a 10-point gap at the
same cohort sizes would not. Pooling is a modelling decision that the
per-cohort comparison must license, not a default.

## Shape: what a finished curve can and cannot say

With estimation settled, the curve itself becomes legible, and practitioners
read it for one signature before any model is fit. The question is what the
curve does at the right edge.

<Figure
  src="./figures/curve-shapes.svg"
  alt="Three stylized survivor curves: one declining to zero, one flattening at a plateau near 0.4, one dropping steeply in the first periods before flattening low"
  caption="Three shapes, three verdicts. Only the plateau, wherever it sits, indicates a retained core."
/>

A curve that runs to zero says every acquired cohort eventually drains away:
growth means perpetually refilling what
[Skok's SaaS Metrics 2.0](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/)
calls the leaky bucket, and acquisition spend buys temporary revenue. A
curve that flattens says a fraction of every cohort stays indefinitely at
the observed horizon: a retained core, the standard cohort-level signature
that some segment gets durable value from the product (the folklore reading:
*product–market fit*, a product the segment needs enough to keep paying
for). Where the plateau sits, and how much
is lost before it, separates a retention problem from an onboarding problem:
a steep early drop to a stable plateau loses customers the product never
activated, while a slow drift to the same plateau loses them along the way.

Two cautions bound the read. A rate computed on fewer than about five
at-risk customers is noise, so a "flat tail" carried by three customers is
not a plateau; and a flat stretch within the window never proves flatness
beyond it. The shape read is a diagnostic, not a projection.

### The fact that stands open

The rate encoding of the same data shows the module's central observation.
The canonical cohort's retention rates, computed in the first section, are:

$$
r_1 = 0.631, \qquad r_2 = 0.742, \qquad r_3 = 0.816, \qquad r_4 = 0.853.
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/rising-r.svg"
  alt="Four points plotting the retention rate against period: 0.631, 0.742, 0.816, 0.853, rising and concave"
  caption="Retention rates rise with tenure. Explaining this is the next lesson."
/>

The rise is the rule, not the exception: every Relay row shows it, and
the previous lesson's magazine-renewal figures (about 30% at the first
renewal, 50% at the second, 75% for longtime subscribers) show it in
published form. Nothing in this lesson explains it, and the two candidate
explanations from
<Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the previous lesson</Ref> (customers
grow loyal, or the loyal are what remains) are both still unrefuted.

The rise also blocks the module's practical need. Expected customer tenure
and lifetime value are sums over $S(t)$ for **all** $t$, but the data
delivers $\hat{S}$ only up to the observed horizon.
Extending the curve requires a rule for how the
$r_t$ series continues, and a series still rising at the window's edge
offers no obvious one; the forward exercise below demonstrates that the
simplest rule, a constant rate, cannot even fit the observed part.

<KeyFact>
What is established: retention data is survival data; $S(t) = r_1 r_2
\cdots r_t$; censored lifetimes are lower bounds that belong in risk sets
and are never discarded or counted as churned; and observed retention rates
rise with tenure.
What is open: why they rise, and how to project $S(t)$ past the window.
<Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">The next lesson</Ref> answers both
with one two-parameter model in which no individual customer's behavior
changes at all.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
From $S(1) = 0.60$, $S(2) = 0.45$, $S(3) = 0.38$, compute $r_1, r_2, r_3$.
Then invert: from $r_1 = 0.55$, $r_2 = 0.80$, $r_3 = 0.90$, recover
$S(1), S(2), S(3)$.

<Solution>
Rates from survival: $r_1 = S(1)/S(0) = 0.60$; $r_2 = 0.45/0.60 = 0.75$;
$r_3 = 0.38/0.45 = 0.844$.

Survival from rates, by the product identity: $S(1) = 0.55$;
$S(2) = 0.55 \times 0.80 = 0.44$; $S(3) = 0.44 \times 0.90 = 0.396$.

The two computations are inverses; either series determines the other
exactly.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two boundary cohorts, both with $S(1) = 0.60$. In cohort A nobody cancels
in period 2 ($r_2 = 1$); in cohort B everybody does ($r_2 = 0$). For each,
state $S(2)$ and what the later rates $r_3, r_4, \ldots$ look like, and
identify which quantities stop being estimable. Then contrast B with a
cohort whose risk set empties by censoring instead.

<Solution>
**Cohort A:** $S(2) = 0.60 \times 1 = 0.60$: the curve is flat for one
period, a local piece of the plateau signature. Later rates are unaffected:
the risk set at age 2 still holds the full 60% of the cohort, and $r_3$ is
estimable as usual.

**Cohort B:** $S(2) = 0$. Every later $S(t)$ is also 0 (cancellation is
absorbing, so the curve can never recover), but the later *rates* are
$r_3 = S(3)/S(2) = 0/0$: undefined, because the risk set is empty. The
survivor values are known (zero) while the conditional rates past the
extinction have no data and no meaning. If B were the only cohort observed
to those ages, no pooled $\hat{r}_t$ for $t > 2$ could be computed from any
cell of the matrix.

**The contrast:** when censoring empties the risk set (the last customer's
window closes while they are still active), $\hat{S}$ beyond that age is
undefined but
bounded between 0 and its last value. Death-emptied: $S$ known, rates
undefined. Censoring-emptied: both unknown. The estimator's domain ends at
whichever comes first.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "We can estimate 24-month retention by averaging the
24-month survival status of all customers who have been with us at least 1
month." Using Kaplan and Meier's two-sample numbers (with years in place of
months), compute what this procedure returns, name the error, and state the
number the correct procedure gives.

<Solution>
Every one of the 1,100 items was observed for at least a year, so the
procedure averages a 0/1 "survived two years" indicator over all of them,
or over the 850 left after setting aside the 250 whose status at two years
is unknown. Either way the censored items are mishandled: counted as
failures, $15/1{,}100 = 0.014$; excluded, $15/850 = 0.018$. Both sit an
order of magnitude below the complete-sample benchmark $15/100 = 0.15$.

The error: a censored lifetime is the event $\{T > 1\}$, and the procedure
treats it as either $\{T \le 2\}$ (churned) or as ignorable. It is neither:
it is information that belongs in the first-year risk set and in no
second-year quantity. Averaging survival *statuses* replaces conditioning
with deletion.

The correct procedure multiplies conditional rates over their risk sets:
$\hat{S}(2) = 0.255 \times 0.50 = 0.127$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The product identity is bookkeeping; the pooled estimator built on it is
not. When $\hat{r}_t$ pools the age-$t$ transitions of several cohorts,
write down the population quantity the pooled fraction actually estimates,
and state the exact condition under which it equals $r_t$.

<Solution>
A cohort with observation limit $L$ contributes to the age-$t$ risk
set only if $L \ge t$. The pooled fraction of survivors among those at risk
therefore estimates

$$
\Pr(T > t \mid T > t-1,\; L \ge t),
$$

survival conditioned on being *observable* at age $t$ as well as alive.
This equals $r_t = \Pr(T > t \mid T > t-1)$ exactly when $T$ and $L$ are
independent given survival to $t-1$, which is the independent-censoring
assumption. Since $L$ is set by acquisition date, the condition says
survival does not depend on vintage; a vintage effect (a pricing change, a
product overhaul) breaks the equality, and the pooled estimate silently
blends the regimes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Solution>
The censoring at 0.9 removes one customer before the first cancellation.

| $t_j$ | At risk $n_j$ | Factor | $\hat{S}(t_j)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.2 | 9 | 8/9 | 0.889 |
| 2.5 | 8 | 7/8 | 0.778 |
| 4.1 | 6 | 5/6 | 0.648 |
| 6.3 | 4 | 3/4 | 0.486 |
| 8.8 | 2 | 1/2 | 0.243 |

Between 2.5 and 4.1 the censoring at 3.0 shrinks the risk set to 6; the
censorings at 5.5 and 7.2 produce the risk sets of 4 and 2. Exactly:
$\hat{S}(8.8) = \frac{8}{9}\cdot\frac{7}{8}\cdot\frac{5}{6}\cdot\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{35}{144} = 0.243$.

The last customer is censored at 9.5, so $\hat{S}(t)$ is undefined for
$t > 9.5$, bounded between 0 and 0.243. The five-at-risk rule flags the
6.3 factor, computed on $n_j = 4$: the last two values rest on four, then
two, lifetimes and are indicative only.

Discarding the four customers censored before 8.8 leaves six, of whom one
(the 9.5 censoring) outlived 8.8: $1/6 = 0.167$ against the product
estimate's 0.243: biased low, as always, by deleting survivors.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For the canonical cohort ($r_t = 0.631, 0.742, 0.816, 0.853$), find the
constant retention rate $\bar{r}$ that best reproduces the observed
survivor values, by trying a few values. Then report what your $\bar{r}$
predicts for $S(8)$ and for $r_5$, and state the structural reason no
constant can fit.

<Solution>
A constant rate means a geometric curve $S(t) = \bar{r}^{\,t}$, so fitting
is anchoring: matching $S(1) = 0.631$ forces $\bar{r} = 0.631$, which
predicts $S(4) = 0.631^4 = 0.159$ against the observed 0.326; half the
cohort's actual survivors are missing from the model. Matching $S(4) = 0.326$
forces $\bar{r} = 0.326^{1/4} = 0.756$, which predicts $S(1) = 0.756$
against the observed 0.631. Any compromise (say $\bar{r} = 0.70$:
predicted $S$ = 0.700, 0.490, 0.343, 0.240) misses both ends in opposite
directions.

Taking the end-anchored $\bar{r} = 0.756$: $S(8) = 0.756^8 = 0.106$, and
$r_5 = 0.756$: a fall from the observed $r_4 = 0.853$, where the trend
of the series points at 0.87 or higher.

The structural reason: a geometric model has one rate for everybody at
every age, so its $r_t$ series is flat, and no flat series matches a rising
one. The observed curve is more concave than any geometric curve: early
retention too low for the tail, tail too high for the start. The repair is
not a fancier curve through the points but a population of customers with
*different* constant rates, whose mixture produces exactly this rising
series: the sBG model of
<Ref lesson="churn-and-the-sbg-model">the next lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The pooled estimator computes $\hat{r}_t$ for each age from the cohort
matrix of <Ref lesson="cohorts-and-funnels">the first lesson</Ref>. In the
vocabulary of that lesson's three reads (row, column, diagonal), which
read does one pooled $\hat{r}_t$ perform, which read is a single cohort's
$\hat{S}(t)$, and which feature of the matrix decides each risk set's
membership?

<Solution>
One pooled $\hat{r}_t$ is a **column read**: two adjacent columns, ages
$t-1$ and $t$, summed down the cohorts: numerator $\sum_c n_{c,t}$,
denominator $\sum_c n_{c,t-1}$, over cohorts observed to age $t$. Age is
held fixed and vintages are mixed, which is precisely why the
independent-censoring assumption is a no-vintage-effect assumption.

A single cohort's $\hat{S}(t) = n_{c,t}/n_{c,0}$ is a **row read**: one
cohort followed through its own life.

Risk-set membership is decided by the **staircase boundary**, the diagonal
of last-observed cells. A cohort belongs to the age-$t$ risk set exactly
when its row extends to column $t$, i.e., when the cell $(c, t)$ lies on or
above the staircase; the diagonal is where censoring lives in the matrix.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

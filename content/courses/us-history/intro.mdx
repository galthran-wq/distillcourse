In 1750 the thirteen Atlantic colonies held about 1.2 million people on the
edge of a continent claimed by three larger empires. Two centuries later
their successor state produced more than a third of the world's output,
held its oceans, and had written the institutions of the postwar order.
This course asks the one question that span poses — **how did that
happen?** — and argues its answer from documents and numbers, lesson by
lesson.

The argued answer has four parts, in causal order:

1. **A selected population.** Migration filtered for the literate, the
   dissenting, and the commercially minded — measurable in New England's
   near-universal male literacy, the highest ordinary wages in the world by
   the eighteenth century, and Franklin's compounding arithmetic: a
   population doubling every 20–25 years by natural increase alone.
2. **Institutions that compounded.** Self-government arrived in the founding
   documents themselves — compact, covenant, town meeting, colonial
   assembly — and was machined at Philadelphia, from classical Roman and
   English materials, into the constitution at right: the most imitated
   political design of the modern era.
3. **A revolution that should have failed.** Independence was won against
   the strongest empire on earth at measurable odds — about 2,400 men
   crossing the Delaware six days before the army's enlistments expired.
4. **Enabling conditions.** Land made cheap by the post-contact demographic
   collapse; technology from the cotton gin to mass production; immigration
   at continental scale; war mobilization that twice reset the world order.

<KeyFact>
Achievements are stated as measurements, not declarations — and the record
is read whole: slavery, conquest, and Reconstruction stay in the causal
story, told in the register Lincoln and Douglass used. The founding as
promise, the Civil War as the terrible price of self-correction, civil
rights as its enforcement.
</KeyFact>

Every lesson is one move in that argument and says so; the chain runs from
an emptied continent to the closing audit, where the course's own thesis is
put on the bench beside every other lens of American historiography. The
evidence is primary wherever it survives — parchments, tribute registers,
ships' manifests, statutes, ballots, census series — and every image in the
course is a real artifact, credited to its source. Starred (*extra*)
chapters are optional depth; the core path never depends on them.

The Seven Years' War left Britain with a debt 13.5 times its annual revenue,
and the taxes meant to pay it down cost Britain thirteen colonies.

The course's argument stands at its collision point. Module 1 built a
selected, literate population that compounded self-government from shipboard
compact to colonial assembly; <Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">the previous
lesson</Ref> bound those colonies into one public through revival, print, and
war, and closed on Britain's postwar ledger and the Proclamation Line. What
stands open is what happened when the empire tried to collect. This lesson
establishes, in order: that imperial finance, not tyranny, started the crisis;
that the colonial objection was institutional, an argument about consent made
by assemblies with a century of practice behind them; that each British
enforcement attempt widened colonial unity until an intercolonial government
existed in fact; and that the Declaration of Independence is a structured
legal argument, edited by committee, whose editing is itself evidence. The
war the argument became belongs to <Ref lesson="war-of-independence">the next
lesson</Ref>.

## Parliament's arithmetic: the debt chose the policy

The ledger of 1763 is already established: a national debt of about £133
million against roughly £10 million in annual revenue, interest consuming
about half of each year's income, and a new 10,000-man garrison in America
costing over £200,000 a year
(<Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">the 1763 ledger</Ref>). British
landowners were already taxed near the limit of political tolerance, and the
colonies were visibly rich: free colonists were among the best-paid ordinary
people in the world
(<Ref lesson="colonial-societies">the wage evidence</Ref>). To Parliament the
conclusion was obvious. America should pay part of the cost of defending
America.

<Figure
  src="./figures/imperial-arithmetic.svg"
  alt="Bar chart of Britain's 1763 ledger: a national debt of about 133 million pounds against annual tax revenue of about 10 million, with a dashed bracket marking the debt as 13.5 times revenue"
  caption="The recap, measured: Britain's debt stood at 13.5 times its annual revenue, interest alone consuming about half of each year's income."
/>

The measures followed in sequence. The Sugar Act of 1764 halved the duty on
molasses but built enforcement teeth: smugglers would face vice-admiralty
courts, without juries. The Currency Act the same year barred colonial paper
money in the middle of a postwar recession. The Stamp Act of March 1765
crossed a line the earlier acts had not: it taxed paper itself, from
newspapers and legal documents to playing cards, the first direct tax
Parliament had ever laid on the colonies, touching printers, lawyers,
merchants, and anyone who read or sued or played cards.

### The amounts were trivial; the argument was not

From a fiscal emergency one would expect a fight about amounts. The record
shows the opposite. Around 1765 the average Briton paid on the order of 26
shillings a year in taxes; the average colonist paid about one shilling. The
new duties would not have closed even a tenth of the gap. "No taxation
without representation" was therefore not a complaint about rates. It was a
constitutional claim about who may levy a tax at all.

  src="./figures/tax-burden.svg"

/>

<KeyFact>
Colonists paid roughly one shilling of tax per person per year against a
British average of about 26; the imperial crisis was an argument over
consent, conducted by people whose tax burden was about a twenty-sixth of
Britain's.
</KeyFact>

## The colonial objection: consent, practiced for a century

The objection had institutions behind it. Every colony had a representative
assembly, and those assemblies had taxed their own populations for a century:
Virginia's House of Burgesses and county courts
(<Ref lesson="colonial-societies">the two polities</Ref>), New England's town
meetings voting their own rates
(<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenanted town</Ref>). The
assemblies also paid the royal governors' salaries, which made the power of
the purse a working check, not a theory. When Parliament proposed to tax
colonists directly, it was proposing to do the one thing colonial
institutions had always done for themselves.

The argument was made in a specific vocabulary, and the vocabulary had a
bookshelf. <Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">The previous lesson</Ref>
established that the colonists' favorite political authors were the London
opposition writers Trenchard and Gordon, whose *Cato's Letters* taught that
power always encroaches on liberty. The pamphlets of the imperial crisis
wrote in that idiom: power has "an encroaching nature"; it is "grasping"; it
is like "jaws … always opened to devour."

This was working equipment, not decoration. When the Boston lawyer Josiah
Quincy Jr. wrote his will in 1774, he left his son Sidney's works, Locke's
works, Bacon's works, Gordon's *Tacitus*, and *Cato's Letters*, adding:
"May the spirit of liberty rest upon him!" A population reading power
through that lens would read each new revenue act as one more advance of a
predator.

### Internal against external, actual against virtual

The first colonial position drew a line inside taxation itself. Daniel
Dulany, a Maryland lawyer whose 1765 pamphlet became the resistance's
standard text, granted Parliament the power to regulate trade but denied the
rest: "A right to impose an internal tax on the colonies, without their
consent for the single purpose of revenue, is denied." Duties at the ports
were regulation; taxes inside the colonies required consent, and consent
required representation.

Britain's answer was the doctrine of *virtual representation*: every member
of Parliament represented every British subject everywhere, including the
large English towns that elected no members at all. James Otis, the Boston
lawyer who had opened the argument in 1764, took that example and reversed
it: "To what purpose is it to ring everlasting changes to the colonists on
the cases of Manchester, Birmingham, and Sheffield, who return no members? If
those now so considerable places are not represented, they ought to be."
Consent, on the colonial theory, could travel only through an actual
election.

<Figure
  src="./figures/representation-two-theories.svg"
  alt="Two-panel diagram. Right panel, the colonial theory of actual representation: a solid arrow runs from colonial electors to their assembly, labeled 'elect', and a dashed arrow from Parliament to the colonists is crossed out, labeled 'no election, no consent'."
  caption="The two theories of representation, side by side: on Britain's, election is optional; on the colonists', consent can travel only through it."
/>

Parliament closed the debate by refusing every distinction. The
[Declaratory Act](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/declaratory_act_1766.asp)
of March 1766 asserted "full power and authority to make laws … to bind the
colonies and people of America … in all cases whatsoever." Internal or
external, revenue or regulation: the distinctions colonists were prepared to
live with were exactly the ones Parliament declared void.

<KeyFact>
Both sides argued constitutional theory sincerely: parliamentary sovereignty
against consent through actual representation. The Declaratory Act of 1766
made the difference explicit and unresolvable, in the words "in all cases
whatsoever."
</KeyFact>

## The Stamp Act cycle: enforce, resist, retreat

With the theories fixed, the decade 1765–1775 ran as a repeating mechanism:
Parliament enforced, the colonies resisted, Parliament partly retreated while
restating its claim, then enforced again. Each cycle left the colonies more
organized than the last. The Stamp Act ran the mechanism first, and ran it
end to end.

<Figure
  src="./figures/escalation-cycle.svg"
  alt="Cycle diagram of three boxes joined by arrows: Parliament enforces (Stamp Act, Townshend duties, Tea Act) leads to the colonies resist (resolves, boycott, crowds, congresses), which leads to Parliament retreats while restating the claim (repeal plus Declaratory Act, Townshend repeal minus tea), which loops back to the next act of enforcement."
  caption="The mechanism of 1765–1775. Each pass around the loop left the colonies more organized than the pass before."
/>

The Stamp Act drew resistance at every level of colonial society at once.
Assemblies passed resolves — Virginia's, of May 1765, printed across the
continent in radicalized versions — and merchants in the port cities signed
*non-importation* agreements that by January 1766 had London merchants
begging Parliament for repeal. Crowds did the rest: they burned the
Massachusetts stamp distributor in effigy and wrecked the lieutenant
governor's house, and by November every distributor in the colonies had
resigned, leaving no one to sell the stamps the law required.

### The cycle's first product: a congress

The resistance also built something new. Nine colonies sent delegates to a
Stamp Act Congress in New York in October 1765 — the first intercolonial
body since the Albany Congress of 1754, whose
<Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">plan of union zero of thirteen assemblies
had ratified</Ref> eleven years earlier. In March 1766 Parliament completed
the turn: it repealed the unenforceable act in the same session that
produced the Declaratory Act's "in all cases whatsoever." Enforcement had
failed, the resistance had acquired a congress, and the claim stood intact
for the next attempt.

## From Townshend to the Coercive Acts: every enforcement widened the union

The Townshend duties of 1767 renewed the cycle with an institutional edge:
the new customs revenue would pay governors and judges directly, cutting the
assemblies' power of the purse. Resistance widened accordingly. Merchants
reinstated non-importation, and enforcement moved into households: women
circulated and signed subscription lists, organized spinning meetings, and
made homespun cloth a public badge of politics, while committees of
inspection published violators' names in the newspapers. Boycott turned
consumption itself into a vote, and it was a vote women could cast.

Britain answered the Boston resistance with soldiers: two regiments of
regulars landed on October 1, 1768, and two more followed within weeks — a
peacetime garrison of four regiments quartered in a town of about fifteen
thousand people. A standing army in peacetime was the exact institution
forty years of Whig reading had taught colonists to fear. Two of the four
regiments were withdrawn in 1769; the 14th and the 29th stayed. On March 5,
1770, a crowd pressed a sentry party outside the Custom House; soldiers of
the 29th fired, and five Bostonians died, among them Crispus Attucks, a
formerly enslaved man working the docks. The soldiers were tried in Boston
and acquitted, defended by John Adams. What traveled was not the trial
record but a picture.

Paul Revere, a Boston silversmith, engraved the scene within three weeks:
soldiers firing in a dressed line on command into an unarmed crowd, under a
sign he invented, "Butcher's Hall." Witnesses at the trial described chaos,
provocation, and no order to fire. The engraving is the period's most
effective propaganda print precisely because it is unreliable as a record of
the event: what it documents is what the resistance wanted every colonist to
see, and thousands did.

### From cheap tea to a continental government

Parliament repealed the Townshend duties in 1770, keeping the tea duty to
mark the claim. The Tea Act of 1773 then made East India Company tea cheaper
than ever, with the duty attached: accepting the bargain would concede the
precedent. On December 16, 1773, after weeks of town meetings, Boston men
emptied 342 chests of tea into the harbor in under four hours, damaging
nothing else. Parliament answered in the spring of 1774 with four statutes —
the Coercive Acts to Parliament, the Intolerable Acts to the colonists, one
set of laws under two names: Boston's port closed, the Massachusetts charter
government dismantled, trials of royal officials moved overseas, troops
quartered in colonists' buildings.

The Coercive Acts were designed to isolate Massachusetts and had the
opposite effect. Food convoys moved toward Boston from other colonies;
committees of correspondence, built during the Townshend resistance, carried
the news; and in September 1774 every colony but Georgia sent delegates to a
First Continental Congress in Philadelphia. Its Continental Association
banned trade with Britain and directed "that a committee be chosen in every
county, city, and town" to enforce the ban and publish violators. An
intercolonial body now legislated, and thousands of local committees
executed. On April 19, 1775, British regulars marched to seize militia
stores at Lexington and Concord, two towns northwest of Boston; the militia
fought them back into the city and besieged it. This lesson's narrative of
events stops there: the fighting is <Ref lesson="war-of-independence">the
next lesson's</Ref> subject.

<Aside>
The colonists read the separate statutes of 1763–1775 as one plot, and
Bailyn's "Note on Conspiracy" argues the inference was rational by period
standards, not paranoid. The colonists' Whig theory held that power advances by increments
and by stealth; the evidence of 1763–1775 (new taxes, new courts without
juries, a standing army in peacetime, salaries detached from assemblies)
matched that theory's predictions in detail; and each British denial was, on
the theory, exactly what plotters would say. Bailyn's summary: colonists
became convinced they faced "a deliberate assault launched surreptitiously by
plotters against liberty both in England and in America." The same
pattern-argument, moved from pamphlets into law, became the Declaration's
"long train of abuses … evinces a design." John Adams looked back in 1818
and located the revolution in exactly this change of interpretation: "The
Revolution was effected before the war commenced. The Revolution was in the
minds and hearts of the people."
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
In 1754 zero of thirteen assemblies would ratify a union; by late 1774
twelve colonies ran a joint trade policy enforced by elected committees in
nearly every county. British enforcement built, in nine years, the
intercolonial machine the colonies had refused to build for themselves.
</KeyFact>

## Fifteen months between war and independence

Fighting began in April 1775; independence followed only in July 1776, and
the gap was not hesitation about the argument but about the remedy. The
Second Continental Congress, meeting in May 1775, ran two tracks at once:
it adopted the New England militia besieging Boston as a Continental Army
and named George Washington to command it, and in July it sent the king the
Olive Branch Petition, a final profession of loyalty.

The Crown, not the Congress, closed the reconciliation track. George III
refused to receive the petition, declared the colonies in rebellion in
August, and told Parliament in October that the resistance aimed at
"establishing an independent empire."

The lower track, the Congress's: June 1775, the Continental Army adopted with Washington commanding; July 1775, the Olive Branch Petition sent; January 1776, Common Sense published; then May 10, new governments ordered; June 7, Lee's resolution moved; July 2, the resolution passes twelve votes to none."

### Monarchy itself becomes the question

Two publications then moved the argument from Parliament's powers to
monarchy itself. In November 1775 Lord Dunmore, Virginia's royal governor,
declared martial law and offered freedom to the enslaved men of rebel
masters who would join him. Perhaps five hundred to a thousand did, and the
proclamation — the first mass emancipation in American history — convinced
many planters that slavery was safer outside the empire than in it.

In January 1776 Thomas Paine, an English immigrant in Philadelphia,
published [*Common Sense*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/147), a pamphlet
that attacked kingship as an institution in plain scriptural English:
"There is something absurd, in supposing a continent to be perpetually
governed by an island." Printers reissued it in some two dozen American
editions before the year was out, and contemporaries put the copies in
circulation above one hundred thousand — on the order of one for every
twenty-five people in the colonies. Independence became a popular question
rather than a delegates' question.

### The legal act, in stages

On May 10, 1776, Congress told the colonies to
form new governments; on June 7 Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moved
"That these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, Free and
Independent States"; and on July 2 the resolution passed twelve votes to
none, New York abstaining. That vote was the declaration of independence in
law.

<Remark title="July 2, July 4, August 2">
A standing misconception holds that independence was declared, and the
famous document signed, on July 4, 1776. It was not. The legal act was
Congress's adoption of Lee's resolution on July 2; John Adams wrote home the
next day that "The Second Day of July 1776, will be the most memorable
Epocha, in the History of America." July 4 is the date Congress approved the
explanatory text, the date printed on it. The engrossed parchment was signed
by most of its 56 signers on August 2, and by some later still. The
confusion comes from the document itself, which carries the July 4 date, and
from a century of paintings that compressed drafting, adoption, and signing
into one scene. The discriminating test: New York's convention did not even
accede to independence until July 9, yet its delegates' signatures stand on
the parchment, which no one-day account can explain.
</Remark>

## The Declaration: a legal argument in three parts

The document itself was drafted as a justification of an act Congress was
about to take. On June 11, 1776, Congress named a Committee of Five: Thomas
Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert
Livingston. Jefferson wrote the draft; Adams and Franklin made small
changes; Congress, sitting over the text from July 2 to July 4, cut roughly
a quarter of it. What survived has the structure of a legal brief, and it
reads best as one.

<Figure
  src="./figures/declaration-anatomy.svg"
  alt="Schematic of the Declaration as three labeled zones: the natural-rights premise, the 27 grievances offered as proof of a design of despotism with the struck slave-trade charge marked as a deleted block, and the operative clause enacting Lee's resolution verbatim"
  caption="The Declaration as a brief: premise, proof, operative clause — with the one deletion Congress made in the proof section marked."
/>

**The premise** is a theory of government stated as self-evident:
"all men are created equal," endowed with "certain unalienable Rights," with
governments "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed"
and forfeiting legitimacy when they destroy the rights they exist to secure.
The vocabulary is John Locke's: rights held prior to government, government
as a trust, revolution as the remedy for breach. Note the premise plainly
now; when <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the founding module</Ref> presents
the republican reading of 1776, the two vocabularies must be held together,
because the founders used both.

**The proof** is a list of 27 grievances against the king, marshaled to
establish not misgovernment but intent: "a long train of abuses and
usurpations, pursuing invariably the same Object evinces a design to reduce
them under absolute Despotism." That is the conspiracy inference of the
pamphlet decade, entered into the national charter as a finding of fact.

**The operative clause** does the legal work, and Congress wrote it by
inserting Lee's July 2 resolution nearly verbatim: "these United Colonies
are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States." The
[full text](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript)
runs about 1,300 words; the independence it declares occupies one sentence,
already voted.

### Reading an edit: the struck slave-trade charge

<Example title="what a deletion establishes">
Jefferson's draft closed its grievance list with its longest charge, an
attack on the Atlantic slave trade. The king, it read, "has waged cruel war
against human nature itself, violating it's most sacred rights of life and
liberty in the persons of a distant people who never offended him,
captivating & carrying them into slavery in another hemisphere, or to incur
miserable death in their transportation thither," and was "determined to
keep open a market where MEN should be bought & sold." Congress struck the
whole passage.

Jefferson's own record of why survives in his notes: the clause "was struck
out in complaisance to South Carolina and Georgia, who had never attempted
to restrain the importation of slaves, and who on the contrary still wished
to continue it," while northern delegates "felt a little tender under those
censures; for tho' their people have very few slaves themselves yet they had
been pretty considerable carriers of them to others."

Read the edit as evidence, in three steps. First, the draft establishes that
in June 1776 the drafting committee thought an antislavery charge belonged
in the founding document, and expected it to be arguable. Second, the
deletion establishes where the veto sat: two Deep South delegations plus
northern carrying interests were sufficient to remove it, which measures the
coalition independence required. Third, what remained establishes the
compromise's shape: the adopted text's only surviving reference to slavery
is the charge that the king "has excited domestic insurrections amongst us,"
an oblique complaint about Dunmore's proclamation. The document blamed the
king for enslaved people's flight to freedom after deleting the charge that
enslaving them was a crime. No narrative source states the founding
compromise more precisely than this pair of edits. Exercise 3 applies the
same method to a second edit Congress made.
</Example>

## The object itself: parchment against engraving

The physical document carries its own evidence, and it is not the evidence
most readers expect. After adoption, Congress ordered the Declaration
"fairly engrossed on parchment"; the clerk Timothy Matlack probably penned
it, and the signing began on August 2. That parchment is the object on
display at the National Archives, and it is largely illegible: the banner
line survives, but the body text and most of the 56 signatures have faded to
shadows.

The legible Declaration in circulation is a copy with a history. In 1820
Secretary of State John Quincy Adams commissioned the printer William J.
Stone to engrave an exact facsimile, finished in 1823; the wet-ink transfer
process Stone likely used lifted ink from the original, and from 1841 to
1876 the parchment hung opposite a window in the Patent Office, 35 years of
sunlight doing the rest. Every crisp reproduction descends from Stone's
copperplate.

## The paradox on the record

The Revolution's central contradiction is best stated as adjacent facts,
and this course states it that way deliberately. The Declaration asserted
that all men are created equal in a society where about one person in five
was enslaved.

The assertion's reach was known at the time. James Otis had written in 1764
that by "the law of nature" all men are "free born," and that "all men"
meant "white or black." Congress then struck the one grievance that applied
the principle to the slave trade.

The enslaved acted on the principle themselves. During the war that
followed, historians estimate that between 30,000 and 100,000 enslaved
people freed themselves by flight to British lines, the largest
self-emancipation in American history before 1861; the mechanics belong to
<Ref lesson="war-of-independence">the war lesson</Ref>. The same rhetoric
also began doing antislavery work almost immediately, in northern
gradual-emancipation laws and in freedom petitions by Black New Englanders
that turned the natural-rights language against slavery.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paradox-tally.svg"
  alt="Two panels side by side: the vocabulary of 1776 (all men created equal; Otis's white or black; consent of the governed) and the record (one in five enslaved; 30,000 to 100,000 flights to British lines; the struck slavery charge; a stronger central state by 1787)"
  caption="The paradox as a tally: what the Revolution said, beside what the record shows. Both columns are period fact."
/>

The Revolution was also a civil war, which the next lesson takes up in
full. Perhaps a fifth of white colonists remained loyal to the Crown, and
about 60,000 Loyalists ultimately left the country — roughly one person in
forty of a population of about 2.5 million. The two numbers measure
different things: loyalty was a sentiment, exile the measured act of the
most exposed; most who had sided with the Crown stayed and submitted. Most
Native nations that entered the war sided with Britain, the power that had
drawn the Proclamation Line against settler expansion.

The second half of the paradox is institutional: a revolt against a distant
central power produced, within eleven years, a central government stronger
than the one overthrown, a construction project the whole next module
examines. When the course reaches
<Ref lesson="sectional-crisis">the sectional crisis</Ref>, Frederick
Douglass will read these same founding documents as an enforceable promise;
the reading starts from the facts on this page.

What is now established: the imperial crisis began in fiscal arithmetic and
became a constitutional argument over consent; escalation built an
intercolonial government; independence was enacted by resolution on July 2,
1776, and justified by a three-part legal argument whose one antislavery
charge was edited out. <Ref lesson="war-of-independence">The next
lesson</Ref> follows the war that argument required: an outmatched rebellion
won at measurable odds, from Boston through Trenton and Saratoga to
Yorktown.

dead civilians, and (in the fallen figure of Crispus Attucks) who died.
Argues: that the killing was a deliberate, commanded act of military
tyranny, complete with an invented "Butcher's Hall" sign. Omits: the
crowd's size, its weapons (clubs, ice, stones by trial testimony), the
provocation, and the absence of any proven order to fire, all of which
emerged at the trial that acquitted the soldiers.

(d) It cannot establish what happened in King Street, because it
contradicts the sworn record on the points above. It firmly establishes
what the resistance wanted colonists to believe and how effectively such
beliefs could be distributed: the print, reprinted across the colonies,
is primary evidence for the propaganda system, not for the massacre.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Congress made a second load-bearing edit to Jefferson's draft: it replaced
his operative sentence (which "rejected and renounced all allegiance" to
the British crown in Jefferson's own words) with the text of Lee's
resolution of July 2, nearly verbatim: "these United Colonies are, and of
Right ought to be Free and Independent States…" Apply the reading-an-edit
method from the worked example: state (a) what the substitution establishes
about the relationship between the Declaration and the resolution, and
(b) what it establishes about whose act independence was.

<Solution>
(a) The substitution establishes that the Declaration is subordinate to
the resolution: Congress treated the words it had already voted on July 2
as the legally operative formula and fitted the explanatory document
around them. The Declaration announces and justifies an act; the
resolution is the act. This is why the lesson dates independence to
July 2, not July 4.

(b) It establishes that independence was Congress's corporate act, not the
drafter's. Jefferson's committee could propose language, but where the text
did legal work, Congress substituted its own adopted words, exactly as it
struck the slave-trade charge where the text exceeded what the coalition
would vote for. Both edits show the same thing from opposite directions:
every politically binding sentence in the document is the assembly's, and
the draft's eloquence survived only where it did not commit anyone to
anything the coalition could not carry.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating conditions for
American independence as declared in July 1776, defending each placement in
a sentence: (1) Britain's postwar debt of 13.5 times revenue; (2) the
colonial assemblies' century of self-taxation; (3) the Coercive Acts;
(4) the Commonwealth-Whig reading tradition. State one placement a
reasonable reader could dispute, and why.

<Solution>
(1) The debt is **necessary**: without the fiscal emergency there is no
sustained program of colonial taxation, and every crisis of 1764–1775
descends from that program (this course's opening claim).

(2) The assemblies' practice is **necessary**: the objection "no taxation
without our consent" required institutions that had actually exercised the
taxing power; a population without its own assemblies would have had a
grievance but not a constitutional counter-theory or the machinery to
enforce one.

(3) The Coercive Acts are **precipitating**: they converted resistance
into intercolonial government within months (Congress, Association,
committees), and by dismantling a charter they demonstrated that any
colony's government existed at Parliament's pleasure.

(4) The Whig tradition is **enabling**: it supplied the interpretive frame
that strung separate statutes into a "design," which accelerated and
unified resistance, but it had circulated harmlessly for forty years
without producing a crisis until the taxes gave it material.

Disputable placement: (2) can be argued as enabling rather than necessary,
on the ground that other under-taxed, assembly-less populations also
rebelled in this era; the counter is that this revolution's specific form,
a lawyers' argument about consent conducted by functioning legislatures,
is unexplainable without the institutional inheritance, and the form is
what the lesson is ranking causes for.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Three documents bear on the paradox section: the Somerset ruling (1772),
which undercut slavery's legality in England itself; Dunmore's proclamation
(November 1775), offering freedom to enslaved men of rebel masters who
joined the British; and Virginia's wartime regulations threatening
freedom-seekers with sale to the West Indies or execution. State what each
document, on its own, can establish about the relationship between the
Revolution and slavery, and then state the claim that only the three
together support.

<Solution>
Somerset establishes that by 1772 the empire's own courts were becoming
legally unreliable for slaveholders: the metropolis could rule against
slavery without consulting the colonies. Dunmore's proclamation establishes
that the British would weaponize emancipation instrumentally (it applied
only to the enslaved of rebels, and Dunmore was no abolitionist), and that
enslaved people acted on it at the risk of their lives. The Virginia
regulations establish that revolutionary governments answered flight with
escalated coercion, in statute, while fighting a war in liberty's name.

Together, and only together, they support the claim that for many southern
planters independence was in part a proslavery choice: the empire looked
increasingly dangerous to slavery (Somerset, then Dunmore), the new nation
promised surer protection (the Virginia statutes delivering it), and the
enslaved read the same situation in reverse, treating the British line as
the freedom side. Any one document alone is consistent with softer
readings; the triangulation is what fixes the interpretation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A survey sentence claims: "The American Revolution was a tax revolt." Name
two facts established in this lesson that the claim fails to account for,
and state what would have to be true, but is not, for the claim to survive.

<Solution>
Two facts it cannot absorb. First, the burden: colonists paid roughly one
shilling per person per year against a British average of about 26, and
resistance actually intensified when taxes were lowered (the Tea Act made
tea cheaper; the Tea Party destroyed it anyway, because the duty carried a
precedent). Second, the settlement terms: the colonists' stated objection
survived every repeal, because the Declaratory Act's "in all cases
whatsoever" kept the constitutional claim alive even with almost no taxes
being collected; and the Declaration's grievance list is dominated by
charges about courts, charters, standing armies, and consent, not rates.

For "tax revolt" to survive, resistance would have to track the size of
the burden: rising with higher taxes, subsiding with lower ones, and
ending when the money demands ended. The record shows the opposite
correlation; the revolt tracked the claim of authority, not the amount.
The defensible version is the one this lesson argues: a constitutional
revolt about consent, started by a tax program.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Continental Association of 1774 succeeded where the
<Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">Albany Plan of 1754</Ref> failed 0-for-13,
though both proposed intercolonial machinery with real power. Using
established facts from the previous lesson and this one, name three things
that changed between 1754 and 1774 that explain the difference, and state
what the comparison establishes about how the union was actually built.

<Solution>
Three changes. First, shared experience and infrastructure: by 1774 the
colonies had a functioning intercolonial public (the Awakening's audience,
the newspaper network, committees of correspondence), where 1754's
colonies were thirteen separate publics; the Association could be enforced
in print, by publishing violators' names. Second, a common adversary
replaced a common project: Albany asked assemblies to surrender their tax
power to a joint council for defense, while the Association asked them to
coordinate resistance in defense of that very tax power; the 1774 design
ran with the grain of assembly self-interest instead of against it.
Third, demonstrated threat: in 1754 the empire was the colonies'
protector; by 1774 the Coercive Acts had shown that Parliament could
dissolve a colonial government outright, making every assembly's
self-preservation an argument for union.

The comparison establishes that the union was built by escalation, not by
design: colonial leaders did not persuade the colonies into machinery in
the abstract (that had failed unanimously); British enforcement created
the shared threat and the resistance created the institutions, cycle by
cycle. The disunion of 1754 and the Association of 1774 are the endpoints
this lesson's escalation ladder connects.
</Solution>

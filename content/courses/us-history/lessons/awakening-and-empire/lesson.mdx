Between 1739 and 1763 two continental events gave thirteen separate colonies
their first shared experiences: an itinerant preacher whom a large fraction
of the population heard in person, and a world war that began with a Virginia
colonel's ambush in a Pennsylvania ravine.

The course's argument so far has built three separate societies: a covenanted
New England (<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>), a
Chesapeake governed by assembly and county court
(<Ref lesson="colonial-societies">the two-societies lesson</Ref>), and the
pluralist commercial middle region of
<Ref lesson="middle-colonies">the previous lesson</Ref>. All three were rich
by the standards of the age, unusually literate, and self-governing; they had
also never acted as one public, and their empire had never tried to govern
them directly. This lesson supplies the binding and the collision's cause, in
order: the revival and the print machinery that carried it, what the revival
changed in how colonists treated authority, the war that began with two
skirmishes near the Forks of the Ohio, the Native nations that fought that
war as independent parties, and the ledger of 1763, which closes on the
number the next module opens with.

## One voice, thirteen colonies

A shared experience needs a shared medium, and by 1739 the colonies had
built one without intending to. New England's school statutes had
manufactured near-universal male literacy
(<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the 1642 and 1647 laws</Ref>), and the
commercial, pluralist port cities had built presses to sell to those readers
(<Ref lesson="middle-colonies">Franklin's Philadelphia</Ref>): in 1740 five
colonial towns printed their own newspapers, from Boston south to
Charleston. An intercolonial audience existed before anyone had addressed
it.

### One preacher, fourteen months, every print town

The man who addressed it was
[George Whitefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Whitefield), a
twenty-four-year-old Church of England clergyman already famous in Britain
for preaching outdoors to crowds no church could hold. He landed at Lewes,
Delaware, a pilot town at the mouth of Delaware Bay, on October 30, 1739,
and spent the next fourteen months working the seaboard: Philadelphia and
New York in November, overland through the Chesapeake to Savannah for the
winter, then New England in the fall of 1740. Revivals were nothing new;
Jonathan Edwards's Northampton, on the Connecticut River in western
Massachusetts, had seen a famous local one in 1734–35. What Whitefield did
was make revival a single, continental event, running on advance newspaper
publicity and arriving on schedule.

### Boston's audience was larger than Boston

The crowds outran every building. Refused many pulpits and too large for
the rest, Whitefield preached from courthouse steps, wagons, and
hillsides, and the newspapers counted for him: Philadelphia papers reported audiences of
twenty-five thousand, roughly the city and its suburbs entire. In
mid-October 1740 his farewell sermon on Boston Common drew a crowd
contemporaries put at twenty to thirty thousand, in a town of about
sixteen and a half thousand people; the audience had come in from the
countryside, and it was larger than the town itself. Franklin, watching
Philadelphia, recorded the saturation in one sentence: "one could not walk
thro' the town in an evening without hearing psalms sung in different
families of every street."

<KeyFact>
Whitefield's Boston farewell crowd was larger than Boston: the First Great
Awakening was the first experience colonists in every region consumed
simultaneously, and it ran on the literacy and press infrastructure the
previous lessons measured.
</KeyFact>

This wave is what historians call the *First Great Awakening*: the
evangelical revival of the 1730s–40s, "First" to distinguish it from the
larger, theologically different revival of the early republic that
<Ref lesson="religion-and-reform">a later lesson</Ref> takes up.

The revival's best-documented distribution deal was struck by a skeptic.
Benjamin Franklin never accepted Whitefield's theology ("Ours was a mere
civil friendship"), but he printed Whitefield's *Sermons* and *Journals*,
sold them by advance subscription through the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, and
covered the tour as news; papers from Boston to Charleston did the same.
Whitefield titles led the colonial book market in 1740, and the
[Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20203) records both sides
of the relationship: the printer's ledger and the hearer's experience. The
revival was not carried by church structures, which mostly resisted it; it
was carried by the commercial press.

<Aside>
Franklin measured Whitefield's pull on himself and reported the result
deadpan. He had refused on policy to fund Whitefield's Georgia orphanage,
then attended a sermon with a pocketful of coins: "As he proceeded I began
to soften, and concluded to give the coppers. Another stroke of his oratory
made me asham'd of that, and determin'd me to give the silver; and he
finish'd so admirably, that I empty'd my pocket wholly into the collector's
dish, gold and all." A friend who had emptied his pockets in advance, as a
precaution, tried to borrow money for the collection from the one neighbor
who refused him: "At any other time, Friend Hopkinson, I would lend to thee
freely; but not now, for thee seems to be out of thy right senses."
</Aside>

Franklin also did what no one else thought to do: he treated the crowd
reports as a measurement problem.

Newspapers claimed twenty-five thousand hearers at a time when no colonial
building held two thousand. Before reading the worked example, write down

arithmetic; then compare your design with Franklin's.

<Example title="Franklin's thirty thousand">
Standing at the rear of a Whitefield crowd on Market Street, Franklin
"had the curiosity to learn how far he could be heard, by retiring
backwards down the street towards the river," and found the voice
distinct "till I came near Front-street." That is the whole measurement
as he published it: a start point, an end point, no figure in feet. The
radius is this course's reconstruction from the ground, not Franklin's
number: Whitefield preached from the courthouse steps at Second and
Market, and on Philadelphia's street grid the walk from there to Front
Street is roughly **500 feet**, anywhere from about 400 to 550, since
"near Front-street" fixes neither where Whitefield stood within the
crowd's edge nor where exactly Franklin stopped.

He then modeled the audience: "Imagining then a semicircle, of which my
distance should be the radius, and that it were fill'd with auditors, to
each of whom I allow'd two square feet, I computed that he might well be
heard by more than thirty thousand."

Run the model across the plausible radii. At $r = 500$ feet the
semicircle's area is $\tfrac{1}{2}\pi r^2 \approx 390{,}000$ square feet,
about **195,000 places** at 2 square feet per hearer; at $r = 400$ feet,
about 125,000 places. The model does not drop to Franklin's published
"more than thirty thousand" until the radius shrinks below 200 feet,
under half the shortest plausible walk. The right conclusion is not that
Franklin was cautious by some exact factor (any such factor depends on a
radius he never stated) but that the bound clears the newspapers' largest
crowds at *every* radius consistent with a walk of one to two blocks.

What the computation establishes is deliberately limited. It bounds how
many people *could* hear one unamplified voice; it counts places, not
people, so it cannot confirm any particular crowd figure. What it did for
Franklin was calibrate plausibility: it "reconcil'd me to the newspaper
accounts of his having preach'd to 25,000 people in the fields, and to the
ancient histories of generals haranguing whole armies, of which I had
sometimes doubted." A period estimate, tested by a period skeptic, with the
method on the record. Exercise 2 reruns it under hostile assumptions.
</Example>

## What the revival changed: authority became something chosen

The crowds are established; the harder question is what the revival left
behind in how colonists dealt with institutions. The answer starts with
what was actually preached. The theology of the revivalists demanded a *new
birth*: an immediate, personal conversion that no minister, sacrament, or
church membership could stand in for. Jonathan Edwards put the stakes in
the sermon that became the revival's most famous text,
["Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"](https://ccel.org/ccel/edwards/sermons.sinners.html),
preached at Enfield, Connecticut, on July 8, 1741: "The God that holds you
over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider, or some loathsome insect
over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked … you hang by a
slender thread, with the flames of divine wrath flashing about it." If
salvation was a transaction between the individual and God, the standing
of every human intermediary became an open question.

### The structural residue: splits, itinerants, lay judgment

Hearers drew the conclusion and acted on it, and the actions left records.
Gilbert Tennent, a New Jersey Presbyterian, preached and printed *The
Danger of an Unconverted Ministry* (1740), telling congregations to judge
whether their own minister was saved and to leave if he was not.
Itinerants preached in other ministers' parishes without invitation, which
had scarcely happened in a century of colonial religion. Connecticut's
assembly answered in 1742 with a statute stripping the salary of any
minister who preached outside his own parish uninvited: an established
church legislating against the location of sermons is a measure of what it
thought was under attack.

The denominations split along the same line. Presbyterians divided into Old
Side and New Side in 1741; New England Congregationalists divided into "Old
Lights" and "New Lights"; Separate congregations formed, and many became
Baptist. Where <Ref lesson="middle-colonies">the middle colonies</Ref> had
made religious choice a fact of pluralist geography, the Awakening
generalized it: by the 1750s, in most colonies, which church to attend, and
whether its minister deserved his pulpit, was a decision ordinary laypeople
expected to make.

<KeyFact>
The Awakening's durable product was not piety statistics but a habit:
denominations multiplied, itinerants ignored parish lines, and laypeople
judged and replaced their ministers. Established religious authority became
something a colonist could evaluate and decline.
</KeyFact>

### The political payoff is an interpretation, and contested

That habit's political payoff is an interpretation, and it should be stated
as one. Historians from Alan Heimert in the 1960s to Patricia Bonomi have
read the Awakening as a rehearsal for the imperial crisis: a population
practiced in challenging established religious authority was readier, a
generation later, to challenge established political authority. The
evidence is structural, the splits and the itinerancy and the lay judgment
just described, not a documented chain of intent; and the reading has a
standing objection on the record, Jon Butler's argument that "the Great
Awakening" is an "interpretative fiction" stitched after the fact from
scattered regional revivals. The claim this course carries forward is the
narrow, well-evidenced one: the revival normalized choosing among
authorities, and it did so simultaneously, in public, in every colony.

### The bookshelf beside the Bible

The same presses carried a political literature, and it had a definite
shape. The colonial reading public's favorite political authors were the
London opposition writers John Trenchard and Thomas Gordon, whose *Cato's
Letters* (1720–23) taught that power always encroaches, that liberty is
fragile, that standing armies are the classic instrument of tyranny, and
that corruption is the occupational disease of courts. The circulation is
documented: a sixteen-year-old Franklin copied passages of *Cato* into his
first published essays in 1722; Trenchard and Gordon's *Independent Whig*
was reprinted in Philadelphia in 1724 and again in 1740; and the Zenger
newspaper whose 1735 trial closed
<Ref lesson="middle-colonies">the previous lesson</Ref> reprinted their
essays so steadily that its run reads as an anthology of them. In 1740
this Commonwealth-Whig tradition was a reading taste. After 1763 it became
the lens through which colonists read every act of Parliament, and
<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the founding module</Ref> traces what they
built with it.

<KeyFact>
Decades before Parliament wrote a single postwar revenue act, the colonies'
favorite political reading — *Cato's Letters* and the Commonwealth-Whig
canon — had supplied the frame those acts would be read through: power
encroaches, and standing armies and new taxes are its signature.
</KeyFact>

## An ambush in a ravine becomes a world war

Print bound the colonies through their eyes; the war bound them through
men and money, and it began in land the colonies' own growth was pressing
into. The *Ohio Country*, the valley lands west of the Appalachian crest
drained by the Ohio River, was claimed on paper by Virginia and by New
France, and in the early 1750s both acted: a Virginia land company held a
royal grant to the lands around the Forks of the Ohio, where
the Allegheny and Monongahela meet at the site of modern Pittsburgh, and
France answered by building a chain of forts down to the Forks, finishing
with Fort Duquesne there in the spring of 1754. New France ran on the
fur-and-alliance model
(<Ref lesson="european-empires">the three-empires lesson</Ref>); a British
settlement frontier in the Ohio Valley threatened the entire alliance
system behind it.

### 1754: fifteen minutes at Jumonville Glen

  width="s"

Virginia sent a twenty-two-year-old militia lieutenant colonel named George
Washington to contest the Forks. At dawn on May 28, 1754, near a rocky
ravine now called Jumonville Glen, Washington's forty-odd Virginians and a
dozen allied warriors under Tanaghrisson, an Iroquois leader in the Ohio
Country whom the English called the Half King, opened fire on a
thirty-five-man French detachment. The fight lasted about fifteen minutes,
and all but one of the French party were killed or taken. The detachment's
commander, Ensign Joseph Coulon de Villiers de Jumonville, wounded in the
volley, was killed by Tanaghrisson himself; the party, it turned out, had
been carrying a diplomatic summons.

The reprisal came in five weeks. Washington fell back to a hastily built
stockade he named Fort Necessity, in a meadow too wide for its walls, and
on July 3 a larger French and Native force pinned his roughly four hundred
men in flooding trenches and shot about a hundred of them. Near midnight
Washington signed articles of capitulation: a rain-soaked French text,
rendered aloud by a Dutch translator, in which he twice acknowledged
*l'assassinat* of Jumonville. Washington maintained ever after that the
word had been translated to him as "death" or "loss"; France printed the
document across Europe as a signed confession that Britain's agents had
murdered an ambassador. The future commander in chief's first campaign was
an ambush, a capitulation, and an admission he could not read; he learned
his trade from it, and the course meets him again at
<Ref lesson="war-of-independence">Trenton</Ref>.

### Albany, June–July 1754: the union nobody wanted

The same summer produced the first formal attempt at colonial union, and
its failure is the era's best measurement of disunion. The Board of Trade
had ordered seven colonies to meet at Albany, the old Dutch fur town up
the Hudson, to repair the alliance with the Six Nations of the Iroquois,
whose neutrality both empires courted. On
the way there Franklin drafted, and the congress adopted unanimously, the
[Albany Plan of Union](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/albany.asp):
one president-general appointed by the Crown, one elected intercolonial
Grand Council, with power to raise troops and levy taxes for defense. To
promote it Franklin had already published, in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* of
May 9, 1754, the first American political cartoon: a snake cut into pieces
labeled with the colonies' initials, over the caption "Join, or Die."

Every colonial assembly refused the Plan or let it die, and London shelved
it. Franklin's postmortem in the *Autobiography* is the period's own
diagnosis: "the assemblies did not adopt it, as they all thought there was
too much *prerogative* in it, and in England it was judg'd to have too much
of the *democratic*." Assemblies that would not surrender their tax power
to their own joint council in 1754 would have it demanded by Parliament
within eleven years.

<KeyFact>
In 1754 the distance to union was total: the Albany Plan, adopted
unanimously by the delegates who wrote it, was ratified by zero of the
thirteen colonial assemblies. "Join, or Die" records what its author was
arguing against.
</KeyFact>

### Escalation: from the Forks to four continents

Britain answered Fort Necessity with regulars. In July 1755 General Edward
Braddock's column of about 1,300 marched on Fort Duquesne and collided,
seven miles short of it on the Monongahela, with a smaller French and
Native force, most of it Native fighters. In three hours roughly two thirds
of the column was killed or wounded and Braddock was mortally hit;
Washington, serving as an unpaid aide, wrote to his mother that "I luckily
escap'd with't a wound, tho' I had four Bullets through my Coat, and two
Horses shot under me." The frontier war Britain had meant to end in a
season now ran out of control, and in 1756 the empires declared war
formally. Horace Walpole, an English MP whose thousands of letters are the
period's running commentary on London politics, fixed the scale inversion
in one sentence: "The
volley fired by a young Virginian in the backwoods of America set the world
on fire."

The world war ran through Europe, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and,
by 1762, the Philippines. William Pitt, directing British strategy from
1757, subsidized Prussia to fight France in Europe, poured regulars and
naval power into North America, and treated the colonial assemblies as
partners, reimbursing their war costs rather than commanding them. The
method worked: Quebec fell in September 1759 in a battle that killed both
commanding generals, and at Montreal's surrender in 1760 the 11,000 British
regulars stood beside 6,500 colonial militia drawn from every colony north
of Pennsylvania. Tens of thousands of colonists soldiered at some point in
the war: after the revival, a second continental experience, this time with
muskets and pay disputes.

## Native nations were parties, not terrain

The war's American name invites a specific error, and the record refutes
it. Britain's name for the conflict is the Seven Years' War; Americans
remembered it as the French and Indian War, a phrase that reads as if
"French and Indians" were one enemy and Native people a feature of the
hostile wilderness. They were neither. Native nations entered, left, and
switched alliances as independent powers pursuing their own objectives:
French field power in America *was* largely allied Native power (the
Monongahela victory was won mostly by Native fighters), the Six Nations
spent the war leveraging their neutrality against both empires, and
Tanaghrisson at Jumonville Glen acted for his own collapsing position, not
for Virginia. The alliance logic runs the same way it did in
<Ref lesson="natives-and-english">the New England wars</Ref>: alliance and
war were both instruments, chosen rationally as the balance shifted.

### Easton, 1758: the turning point was a treaty

The clearest proof is that the war's turning point in the West was a
treaty, not a battle. At Easton, Pennsylvania, a town on the Delaware
River upstream of Philadelphia, delegates of thirteen nations met in
October 1758 and agreed to abandon the French alliance in exchange for a
British promise that the Ohio Country would be closed to settlement after
the war. Stripped of allied fighters, the French position collapsed; Fort
Duquesne fell within weeks.

### Victory broke the alliance system that produced it

With France expelled, the balance Native diplomacy
had played on was gone, and the British
commander, Jeffrey Amherst, cut off the diplomatic gift-giving that
alliance protocol required. In May 1763 nations across the Great Lakes and
Ohio Country rose against the posts Britain had just inherited, in the war
remembered as Pontiac's War, after the Ottawa leader among its
organizers. Within weeks every British fort west of the Appalachians but
three had fallen, the demonstration that fixed the postwar garrison in
place: the interior would be held by troops and diplomacy, or not at all.

<Aside>
The rising's extent, for the record: eight interior forts taken in the
opening weeks; of the three that held, Detroit stood a six-month siege and
Fort Pitt a summer one; the fighting subsided into negotiated peace by
1766. Casualty estimates remain rough (some hundreds of soldiers, settler
deaths several times that), but the strategic fact they established in
London was not contested.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The Seven Years' War was fought through Native alliance systems, and its
western turning point was the Treaty of Easton, not a battle. Pontiac's War
in 1763 then proved that the interior Britain had just won could be held
only by paid troops and restored diplomacy: the postwar garrison was its
direct product.
</KeyFact>

## 1763: the ledger that forces the crisis

Pontiac's War set the last charge on the empire's postwar account: an
interior that could be held only by troops somebody had to pay. The
account's other entries had been written eight months earlier, at the peace
table. The Peace of Paris, signed in February 1763, ended France as a
continental American power: Canada and everything east of the Mississippi went to
Britain, Louisiana west of the river went to Spain, and Spanish Florida
went to Britain. The colonies' entire strategic environment, the
French-and-allied threat that had justified British protection for four
generations, disappeared in one treaty.

### A debt of 13.5 times revenue

What Britain gained in territory it carried in debt. The war roughly
doubled the national debt to about £133 million, against yearly government
revenue of roughly £10 million: **13.5 times** what the state collected in
a year, with interest payments alone consuming about half of each year's
revenue. On top of the interest came a new permanent charge, the decision
to station ten thousand regulars in postwar North America at over £200,000
a year, to hold the interior that Pontiac's War had just shown could not
be held for free.

Britain ended the Seven Years' War owing about £133 million against roughly
£10 million in annual revenue: 13.5 times its income, plus a new 10,000-man
American garrison to pay for. Every imperial measure of the next decade
descends from this arithmetic.
</KeyFact>

### October: the Proclamation Line

With Pontiac's War still running, in October 1763 the Crown issued the
[Royal Proclamation](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/proc1763.asp)
drawing a boundary along the Appalachian crest: colonial settlement was
barred west of it. The Proclamation Line made the Easton promise imperial
policy, and it stabilized the interior at the price of enraging colonists
who had fought the war precisely for that land.

### Parliament's answer: Sugar and Stamp

Parliament's answer to the arithmetic was to make the colonies, visibly rich and lightly
taxed by British standards, carry part of the American charge: the Sugar
Act of 1764 taxed the molasses trade, and the Stamp Act of 1765 taxed
paper, from newspapers to legal documents. The colonists those acts reached
were no longer thirteen unacquainted publics. They shared a revival that
had taught them to judge established authority, a press that reported all
thirteen colonies to each other, a war they had fought side by side, and a
political bookshelf that explained standing armies and new taxes as the
signature of encroaching power.

<Aside>
How historians argue this last step: Fred Anderson's *A People's Army*
(1984) reconstructed the Seven Years' War service of Massachusetts
provincial soldiers from muster rolls, orderly books, and diaries, and
found a consistent contractual understanding of soldiering. A provincial
enlisted for one campaign, under a named officer, for stated pay and
rations; if the terms were altered (terms extended, rations cut, men
placed under British regulars and their lash), soldiers held the contract
void and walked home with a clear conscience, to British officers'
disgust. Anderson's inference is that thousands of colonists carried out
of the war a worked example of authority as a bargain that could be
breached, a decade before Parliament's taxes posed the same question in
civilian form. It is an argument from behavior, not from statements of
belief, which is both its strength and its limit.
</Aside>

What is now established: by 1763 the colonies had shared experiences, a
functioning intercolonial public, and a demonstrated inability to unite on
their own initiative; Britain had a debt of 13.5 times its revenue, an
army in America to fund, and a Parliament resolved to tax the colonies
directly. The next lesson, <Ref lesson="american-revolution">the imperial
crisis</Ref>, opens with that arithmetic and follows the constitutional
argument it started to the Declaration of Independence.

<Exercise>
Rerun Franklin's computation under hostile assumptions. (a) Recompute the
capacity bound with a radius of 400 feet and 4 square feet per hearer.
(b) At Franklin's own 2 square feet, what radius would make the bound
exactly his published figure of 30,000? (c) Using (a) and (b), state
precisely what Franklin's experiment can and cannot establish about the
newspaper reports of 25,000 hearers.

<Solution>
(a) $\tfrac{1}{2}\pi(400)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi \cdot 160{,}000 \approx
251{,}000$ square feet; at 4 square feet per hearer, about **63,000
places**. Even with the radius cut and the packing halved, the bound still
exceeds every reported crowd.

(b) Solve $\tfrac{1}{2}\pi r^2 / 2 = 30{,}000$, so $r^2 = 120{,}000/\pi
\approx 38{,}200$ and $r \approx 195$ feet. The voice would have to fail
at under two hundred feet, less than half a Philadelphia block, before
Franklin's claim collapsed; the course's reconstruction puts the actual
walk, courthouse to Front Street, at two to two and a half times that
far.

(c) The computation establishes *feasibility*: audiences of 25,000 within
the hearing radius of one voice were physically possible with room to
spare, under any reasonable packing assumption. It cannot establish that
any particular reported crowd was actually that large: it counts places,
not people, and the reports themselves remain period estimates by
interested observers. Note also what (a) and (b) together show about the
radius: since Franklin published no distance (the ~500 feet is
reconstructed from the street grid), the argument rests on the bound
holding across the whole range of plausible radii, not on any single
value. Feasibility plus independent reports from multiple
towns and papers is the corroborated claim; an exact head count is beyond
the surviving evidence.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Run the full source pass on "Join, or Die", using the
<Ref lesson="indigenous-america">records / argues / omits</Ref> method.
(a) Metadata: who made it, where and when it appeared, for what audience,
and what its maker was simultaneously drafting. (b) Prediction from the

(a) Benjamin Franklin, printer of the *Pennsylvania Gazette* and a

(b) An advocate for union publishing on the eve of his own union proposal

would do the defending.

shows union was *argued for* in the most public medium available, and the
0-for-13 ratification shows it was *refused* by every assembly asked. One

constituency. The distance between 1754 and 1776 is the distance between
those two facts.

<Exercise>
The Fort Necessity capitulation of July 3, 1754, survives as a signed
document in which Washington twice acknowledged the *assassinat* of
Jumonville. France circulated it as a confession; Washington claimed the
Dutch translator rendered the word as "death" or "loss" in the midnight
rain. State (a) what the signed document establishes on its own, (b) what
each side's use of it needed the document to mean, and (c) two classes of
evidence a historian would seek to adjudicate the translation claim, and
what each could and could not settle.

<Solution>
(a) On its own the document establishes that Washington's force
surrendered on terms; that the French drafters chose the word
*assassinat*; and that Washington signed a French text containing it. It
does not establish what Washington understood himself to be signing: a
signature proves assent to a paper, not comprehension of a word.

(b) France needed the word to be a knowing admission: a signed confession
by a British officer that his government's agents murdered an envoy, which
is diplomatic ammunition. Washington and Virginia needed it to be a
translation failure: an exhausted colonel misled about one word in a
foreign-language text read aloud in rain by candlelight.

(c) First, testimony about the signing itself: accounts by Van Braam the
translator, by other officers present, and Washington's own statements,
which can establish what was said aloud but are all from interested
parties after the controversy began. Second, the physical and linguistic
context: the document's condition, whether a translation was written or
oral, and Washington's documented ignorance of French, which can establish
opportunity for error but not that error occurred. Neither class can
settle intent conclusively; what the historian can conclude firmly is
narrower: the French text says what it says, Washington's later career
shows no comparable admission, and the episode demonstrates how a document
can be simultaneously authentic and misleading about the intent of one of
its signers.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A survey sentence claims: "The Great Awakening caused the American
Revolution." Write the strongest defensible version of this claim and the
strongest objection to it, each in two or three sentences, using the
evidence in this lesson; then state what kind of evidence, if it existed,
would decide between them.

<Solution>
Strongest defensible version: the Awakening was a structural rehearsal.
It normalized, simultaneously and in every colony, the practice of judging
established authority and choosing alternatives, documented in
denominational splits, uninvited itinerancy, and congregations firing
ministers; it also built the first intercolonial public through the press.
A population with those habits and that infrastructure was measurably
better equipped for the resistance of 1765–75 than the population of 1730.

Strongest objection (Butler's): "the Great Awakening" is a retrospective
construction from scattered regional revivals of different dates and
intensities; the revival's concerns were salvation, not politics; and no
documented chain connects revival experience to revolutionary
commitment, so the causal claim rests on analogy between two kinds of
authority-challenging rather than on evidence of transmission.

What would decide: individual-level linkage evidence, such as systematic
records showing that New Light laypeople, congregations, or regions
disproportionately produced resistance leaders, committee members, or
early petition signers, controlling for region and wealth; or its absence
in records where it should appear. Lacking that, the defensible claim
stays structural (capacities and habits) rather than causal (the revival
produced the Revolution), which is how this lesson states it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The first intercolonial mass event happened in British America and not in
New France, though New France was older than Pennsylvania and its church
far more centralized. Using established facts from
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>,
<Ref lesson="middle-colonies">the middle-colonies lesson</Ref>, and this
one, explain why, naming at least three specific preconditions and the
lesson each comes from.

<Solution>
Three preconditions, with provenance. First, a mass reading public: New
England's 1642 and 1647 school statutes engineered near-universal male
literacy (covenant lesson), so newspaper publicity could reach ordinary
households, not just elites. Second, a commercial, competitive press:
Philadelphia, Boston, New York, Williamsburg, and Charleston printed their
own newspapers by 1740 (middle-colonies lesson for the print economy;
this lesson for the count), and Franklin's subscription editions show
print running as a business, willing to sell a revival the established
clergy resisted. Third, religious pluralism as working order: in colonies
with no effective single establishment (middle-colonies lesson), an
uninvited itinerant could find pulpits, fields, and audiences without the
state's permission. New France had none of the three: a small population
with no printing press in the colony, and one established church
controlling religious speech. A Whitefield was structurally impossible
there: the event required exactly the selected, literate, pluralist
society the module has been measuring.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating conditions for
Parliament's decision in 1764–65 to tax the colonies directly, and defend
each placement in a sentence: (1) the £133M debt at 13.5 times revenue;
(2) the 10,000-man American garrison; (3) Pontiac's War; (4) the colonies'
visible wealth and light tax burden. State one ranking a reasonable reader
could dispute, and why.

<Solution>
(1) The debt is **necessary**: without a fiscal emergency of this scale
there is no political need to breach precedent and tax the colonies from
Westminster; every act of 1764–65 was framed as debt service.

(2) The garrison is **precipitating** as well as enabling: it converted a
general debt problem into a specific, annual American line item (over
£200,000 a year) that Parliament could plausibly bill to America; the
Stamp Act was argued precisely as payment for American defense.

(3) Pontiac's War is **enabling**: it supplied the justification for
keeping the garrison at all (the interior demonstrably could not be held
for free), and so stands one causal step behind (2).

(4) Colonial wealth is **enabling**: the colonies' high wages and light
taxes (established in the colonial-societies lesson) made "they can
afford it" credible in Parliament, but wealth alone taxed nothing;
without (1) and (2) it had sat untaxed for a century.

Disputable placement: (2) can be argued as necessary rather than
precipitating, since without a standing American establishment Parliament
might have serviced the debt entirely at home; the counter is that the
debt pressure existed independently and would have sought an American
outlet regardless, the garrison merely naming the amount.
</Solution>

English North America was not one society but several: a tobacco-and-servants
Chesapeake, a family-and-church New England, and, by 1700, a plantation South
committed by law to hereditary African slavery. This lesson sets the two
English societies side by side on three axes and then traces the legal turn to
slavery to its causal core. The route: two demographies (who migrated and how
long they lived, read through one ordinary household's documents), two
economies (what each region produced, how its land was granted, and the wage
measurement that closes the settler-selection ledger), two polities (assembly
and county court against covenanted town), the mechanics of the indenture and
the planter's ledger, the statutes of 1662 and 1705 with Bacon's Rebellion as
their accelerant, and the single Atlantic economy holding all of it together
by 1750.

## Tobacco set the Chesapeake's demography

### A crop found its colony

Jamestown, founded in 1607, nearly failed: of the roughly 500 colonists present
in the fall of 1609, about 60 were alive the following spring. What saved the
venture was not gold but a plant.

John Rolfe's first cargo of West Indian tobacco reached London in 1614.
Virginia exported about 2,300 pounds in 1615, roughly 1.5 million pounds a year
by 1630, and on the order of 22 million pounds by 1700, per
[Encyclopedia Virginia's survey of the tobacco economy](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/tobacco-in-colonial-virginia/).

<Figure
  src="./figures/tobacco-exports.svg"
  alt="Log-scale plot of Chesapeake tobacco exports at three dates: 2,300 pounds in 1615, about 1.5 million pounds in 1630, and about 22 million pounds in 1700"
  caption="A ten-thousandfold rise in eighty-five years, on a log axis. Every point on this curve is demand for field hands."
/>

Tobacco is labor-intensive for ten months of the year, and land in Virginia was
cheap while hands were scarce. From 1618 the *headright* system converted
people directly into acreage: 50 acres to whoever paid a migrant's passage.

Importing laborers was the way to build an estate, so the Chesapeake imported
laborers and very little else.

### Who came, and how long they lived

Roughly 120,000 English migrants came to Virginia and Maryland over the
seventeenth century, and about three-quarters of them arrived as bound
servants. Most were single men between 15 and 25.

Men outnumbered women about six to one in the 1630s and still four to one at
mid-century, so most male ex-servants could not marry, and the colony could not
reproduce itself.

The disease environment finished the arithmetic. Malaria, typhoid, and
dysentery in the tidewater settlements meant that a man who survived to 20
could expect to die around 45; a quarter of children died in their first year,
and nearly half failed to reach 20.

<Figure
  src="./figures/chesapeake-survival.svg"

  caption="The tidewater funnel: a quarter of children dead in the first year, nearly half before age 20."
  float="right"

/>

The result is the century's tally: about 120,000 migrants in, a population of
roughly 90,000 by 1700. Only in the century's last decades, as survival
improved, did births begin to exceed deaths in the Chesapeake's white
population; that rise in survival returns below as the hinge of the
planter's ledger.

<KeyFact>
For most of the seventeenth century the Chesapeake did not grow by births:
until natural increase finally arrived in its last decades, it grew only by
importing people faster than the tobacco coast killed them, which is why its
history is a history of labor supply.
</KeyFact>

## Two demographies: importing people against raising them

New England's side of the comparison is established in
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>: about 21,000
migrants crossing in family households during the Great Migration of
1630–1642, a near-balanced sex ratio, a man of 20 reaching about 70, and six
to eight surviving children per family. When the flow stopped in 1642, natural
increase took over, doubling the population roughly every generation: about
91,000 New Englanders by 1700 from an immigration stream shut for sixty years.

### One household as the record: the Danes of Ipswich

The numbers above compress thousands of households; one of them left documents.
John Dane (c. 1612–1684), a tailor from Hertfordshire, emigrated around 1638
and settled in Ipswich, Massachusetts.

In 1682 he wrote a short memoir,
["A Declaration of Remarkable Providences in the Course of My Life"](https://archive.org/details/declarationofrem00dane),
recounting his decision to leave England: his parents opposed the move until
the family opened a Bible at random and lit on the verse "Come out from among
them, touch no unclean thing, and I will be your God."

His father and mother then not only consented but followed him across.

<Figure
  src="./figures/dane-family-pedigree.jpg"

A nineteenth-century genealogist printed a pedigree chart alongside the
narrative, and the chart is the demographic argument made visible: from one
immigrant couple, generations of children who survived, married within a
network of neighboring Ipswich and Rowley families, and produced children of
their own.

[MIT's survey course](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/21h-101-american-history-to-1865-fall-2010/)
opens with exactly this pair of documents because an ordinary, non-elite family
carries the whole New England pattern: family migration, longevity, natural
increase, and town networks.

</Remark>

<KeyFact>
New England is the demographic inversion of the Chesapeake: 21,000 migrants
became 91,000 colonists in sixty years without further immigration, while
120,000 migrants to the Chesapeake left a smaller population than had arrived.
</KeyFact>

## Two economies: a staple with bound labor against diversified farms

### The land systems: headright against town grant

The two regions distributed land through different machines, and the machines
reproduced the societies. Virginia's headright turned people into riverfront
acreage claimed by individuals, so the countryside organized itself as
dispersed plantations along the tidal rivers, each loading tobacco at its own
wharf, and the seventeenth-century Chesapeake built almost no towns.

New England granted land to town corporations instead: the covenanted town of
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>, which laid out
family farms around a meetinghouse, required a church, and taxed for a school.
One system rewarded whoever imported laborers; the other rewarded planting
households.

### What each region sold

The Chesapeake was a monoculture: one crop, grown for one export market, on
bound labor. Tobacco paid for everything the region consumed, priced every
debt (court judgments were reckoned in pounds of tobacco), and absorbed
nearly all of its labor.

New England's soil and winters ruled out any such staple. It sold diversity:
mixed farms worked by the household's own six to eight children, the cod
fishery, timber, and the ships and shipping services its ports built and
operated. The region grew rich not on a crop but on carrying, catching, and
building.

### The wage measurement: dear labor on both shores

The comparison is not rich against poor.
[Lindert and Williamson](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19861) reconstructed
colonial incomes from surviving tax lists, probate records, and occupational
counts, and their finding runs against the older assumption of colonial
poverty: the American colonies led Great Britain in purchasing power per
capita from 1700, and possibly from 1650, until 1774, even counting the
enslaved in the population. Among free households the result is sharper
still: free colonists had markedly more equal incomes than England and Wales
and greater purchasing power at every income rank except the top few percent.
Measured prosperity did not sort by labor system: per free capita, the
plantation South ranked richest of the colonial regions and New England
poorest.

The claim is about *free* colonists, and it has one cause with two readings.
Land was abundant relative to hands, so land was cheap and labor dear: a free
worker could demand the best ordinary earnings in the world because 50 acres
of his own was always within reach, and for exactly the same reason anyone
who wanted a large labor force could not hire it at English wages and turned
to labor that was bought and bound. High wages for the free and bound labor
for the rest were one price structure, read from two sides.

  caption="One ratio, two prices: the land-to-labor ratio that paid free colonists the world's best ordinary earnings is the ratio that made bound labor worth buying."

This completes the settler-selection ledger begun with
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">literacy and print</Ref>: by the
eighteenth century the free population of English America was unusually
literate, unusually propertied, and among the best paid in the world — in
both regions.

<KeyFact>
By the eighteenth century free colonists in the Chesapeake and New England
alike were among the best-paid ordinary people in the world; the regional
comparison explains the two labor systems, not a difference in prosperity.
</KeyFact>

## Two polities: assembly and county court against town meeting

Both regions elected legislatures that voted their own taxes, a concession
the other American empires did not make: New France was ruled by governor,
intendant, and appointed council and never seated an elected assembly, and
Spanish America's cabildos were town councils whose seats were appointed or
purchased, not colony-wide representative bodies. The contrast between the
two English regions is therefore not self-government against subjection; it
is who participated, and at what level.

### Virginia: governor, Council, Burgesses, county court

Virginia's [first elected assembly](https://www.americanyawp.com/text/02-colliding-cultures/)
met in the Jamestown church in July 1619: the House of Burgesses, the oldest
continuously meeting legislature in America. After the Crown took the colony
from the Virginia Company in 1624 the structure settled into three parts: a
royal governor appointed in London, a Council of about twelve great planters
appointed for life, and the elected Burgesses, who won the practical power
that mattered — no tax without their vote.

Below the assembly, government was appointed, not elected. The county court's
justices of the peace were commissioned by the governor from each county's
leading planters and handled nearly everything: lawsuits, probate, roads,
licenses, punishments. The parish vestry, a self-perpetuating board of twelve
gentlemen, ran poor relief and church affairs. An ordinary free Virginian's
participation in his government was one act: voting for two burgesses.

### Massachusetts: General Court and town meeting

Massachusetts inverted the weight. The General Court became a representative
assembly in 1634, when the towns began sending elected deputies; under the
original charter the freemen elected even the governor, until the royal
charter of 1691 made the office a Crown appointment. The working government,
however, sat below the colony level: the covenanted town, where the male
householders met several times a year, elected selectmen and a slate of minor
offices, divided the town's land, and taxed themselves for minister and
school.

### One week of governance, read in parallel

The county studies behind the demography also record the governance, and the
two records can be read side by side.

**Middlesex County, Virginia: court day.** The Rutmans reconstruct the
monthly session at the courthouse: the county's justices — the same great
planters — sitting as a bench, before a general audience for whom court day
was the county's public occasion. A session's docket ran debt suits reckoned
in pounds of tobacco, wills proved, orphans' estates audited and orphans
bound out, bastardy presentments, penalties for runaway servants, road
orders, tavern licenses, and the annual county levy. Nothing on the docket
was voted by the audience; everything was decided by the appointed bench.

**Dedham, Massachusetts: town meeting.** Lockridge's study of Dedham reads
the town records the same way. The householders who signed the town covenant
of 1636 met in general meeting, elected seven selectmen each year plus fence
viewers, highway surveyors, and the rest of the minor offices, voted
divisions of the town's land, admitted or refused new inhabitants, and set
the rates for the minister and for the school Dedham voted to support by
taxation in 1644, among the first tax-supported schools in English America.

### The boundaries, stated with the institutions

Neither polity was a democracy. Virginia's electorate was white male
freeholders: women, servants, and the enslaved were out, and a 1723 act made
the race line explicit, barring free Black men, mulattoes, and Indians from
voting. Massachusetts tied the colony franchise to church membership until
1691 and to property after; the town meeting seated male householders the
town had admitted, and <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant
lesson</Ref> records what happened to dissenters. Within those boundaries the
franchise was nonetheless broad by the standards of the age: because land was
cheap, a freehold requirement reached a majority of free adult white men, a
proportion no European state approached.

The imperial condition that let both systems grow was inattention. England
enforced its trade laws loosely and governed rarely; the assemblies used the
interval to win the power of the purse, including the governors' own
salaries. Edmund Burke, looking back from 1775, named the policy "a wise and
salutary neglect." It ran for a century and a half, and what happened when
London ended it after 1763 is the subject of
<Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">the Seven Years' War lesson</Ref> and
<Ref lesson="american-revolution">the imperial-crisis lesson</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
By London's standards both regions were self-governing; the
assembly-and-county-court against town-meeting contrast is a difference in
who participated and at what level, not in whether colonists ruled
themselves.
</KeyFact>

## Indentured servitude: unfreedom with an expiration date

### The contract: four to seven years, sold like property

The Chesapeake's first labor system was not slavery. An *indenture* was a
written contract: the master paid the servant's passage (about £6) and owned
the servant's labor for a fixed term, usually four to seven years.

During the term the servant was genuinely unfree: the contract could be sold,
discipline was corporal, terms were extended for running away or pregnancy,
and marriage required the master's consent.

  caption="The life of one contract. Both load-bearing limits are on the drawing: the term expires, and a child born during it never enters it."
/>

### The two limits: the term ends, and it does not descend

The unfreedom had two limits that matter for everything in the next section.
It expired: at term's end the servant received *freedom dues*, fixed by the
1705 statute at ten bushels of corn, thirty shillings, and for men "one well
fixed musket or fuzee" worth at least twenty shillings.

And it did not descend: a servant's child was born free, because the contract
bound a term of labor, not a lineage.

In the early decades the expiration was often theoretical. Roughly 40 percent
of servants in the first half of the century died before their terms ended,
which made the system, in practice, a lottery in which the master held most
tickets. The same mortality also shaped what masters were willing to pay for.

### The ledger: falling mortality reversed the purchase

<Example title="the planter's ledger">
Compare the two purchases a Chesapeake planter could make, at representative
late-seventeenth-century prices: an indentured servant with a five-year term
for about £11, or an enslaved African, held for life, for about £23.

Cost per expected labor-year, under the early-century mortality regime, where
a new laborer of either status could be expected to survive about five years:

$$
\text{servant: } \frac{£11}{5 \text{ yr}} = £2.2/\text{yr},
\qquad
\text{slave: } \frac{£23}{5 \text{ yr}} = £4.6/\text{yr}.
$$

The servant is half the price per year of labor, and the "lifetime" premium
buys nothing: a life expectancy of five years makes a life term and a five-year
term the same asset. Planters bought servants.

Now let survival improve, as it did in the Chesapeake after mid-century, so
that a purchased laborer can be expected to live twenty more years:

$$
\text{servant: } \frac{£11}{5 \text{ yr}} = £2.2/\text{yr},
\qquad
\text{slave: } \frac{£23}{20 \text{ yr}} \approx £1.15/\text{yr}.
$$

The ranking reverses. And after the 1662 statute of the next section, the
enslaved laborer's children belong to the owner as well, so the £23 buys a
lineage, not a person-term. Falling mortality made lifetime slavery the better
purchase before any planter changed his mind about anything else.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
An indenture and enslavement differ in exactly two load-bearing places: the
term ends, and the children are born free. The statutes of 1662–1705 are the
record of Virginia removing both properties from one class of laborers.
</KeyFact>

## The statute core: term and heredity removed for one class of laborers

### Before the statutes: forty years of ambiguity

In late August 1619 the English privateer *White Lion* landed "20. and odd
Negroes," seized from a Portuguese slave ship, at Point Comfort, Virginia. The
date is famous; the legal status of the people is not recoverable from it,
because Virginia in 1619 had no law of slavery.

The record of the next four decades shows a boundary being felt out case by
case. Anthony Johnson, an African who arrived in 1621, gained his freedom,
acquired 250 acres, and in 1655
[won a lawsuit](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/court-ruling-on-anthony-johnson-and-his-servant-1655/)
confirming his own lifetime claim to a Black laborer, John Casor. In 1640
[a court gave three recaptured runaways asymmetric sentences](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/general-court-responds-to-runaway-servants-and-slaves-1640/):
added years for the two Europeans, and for John Punch, "being a negro,"
service "for the time of his natural Life." In 1656
[Elizabeth Key](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/key-elizabeth-fl-1655-1660/),
daughter of an enslaved mother and a free English father, sued for her freedom
and won it on three grounds: her father's status, her baptism, and a
completed term.

<Figure
  src="./figures/pre-statute-cases.svg"

Casor (1655, no exit for Casor, lifetime claim upheld), and Elizabeth Key (1656, out through a free father, baptism, and a completed term)"
  caption="Four verdicts, no statute: the same courts let two people out and locked two in. Key's three doors out are the ones the assembly closed next."
/>

Key's victory is the hinge. Every argument that freed her was a door out of
bondage that the common law had left open, and the General Assembly spent the
next half-century closing those doors by statute. Two acts carry the whole
construction; the rest of the ladder extends them.

### 1662: heredity

English common law made a child's status follow the father, which made
children like Elizabeth Key free. The assembly reversed the rule,
[enacting](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/negro-womens-children-to-serve-according-to-the-condition-of-the-mother-1662/)
that "all children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only
according to the condition of the mother." The doctrine is *partus sequitur
ventrem*, the offspring follows the womb. From this act forward, slavery was
hereditary, and every child born to an enslaved woman was an asset of her
owner regardless of the father.

Two further acts closed the doors Key had used and the one owners still held:
in 1667 the assembly declared that "the conferring of baptisme doth not alter
the condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome," detaching
slavery from religion and leaving ancestry as its only criterion, and in 1691
it [made every manumission contingent](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/an-act-for-suppressing-outlying-slaves-1691/)
on deporting the freed person from Virginia within six months.

### 1705: consolidation

The [general act "concerning Servants and Slaves"](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/an-act-concerning-servants-and-slaves-1705/)
consolidated the regime. Imported servants "who were not christians in their
native country" — the operative description of Africans — "shall be accounted
and be slaves," baptism afterward notwithstanding. A companion act of the same
session declared slaves "real estate," inherited like land, and the code held
that a slave who resisted and "shall happen to be killed in such correction"
produced no felony: the master stood "free and acquit of all punishment."

The same code guarded white servants: whipping one naked now required a
justice's order, and the freedom dues, musket included, were written into
statute. The intermediate rungs — an impunity act of 1669 for a slave killed
under a master's correction, and a 1682 import act that fixed status at the
dockside — are walked one frame at a time in Exercise 3.

<Remark title="1619 is a beginning, not a completion">
The shorthand "slavery began in 1619" fails in both directions. It is too
late: enslaved Africans had been in Spanish Florida and the Caribbean for a
century. And it is too early: the people landed in 1619 entered a colony with
no slave law, where some of them became free landowners. What has a date range
is the legal regime, and the range is roughly 1640–1705, with the decisive
statutes in 1662, 1667, 1691, and 1705. Hereditary racial slavery was not
imported whole; it was legislated, rung by rung, by men who left the statute
book as a record of each decision.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Hereditary racial slavery in English America was constructed by identifiable
statutes over about forty years: 1662 made it hereditary, 1667 made it
religion-proof, 1691 made it exitless, and 1705 codified the whole and armed
the white servant walking out of it.
</KeyFact>

## Why the turn came when it did: a dried-up servant stream, longer lives, and Bacon's Rebellion

### Three economic pressures, each with a date

Nothing in 1620 made this outcome inevitable; three pressures dated it.

**The servant stream dried up.** English real wages rose after 1660 while new
colonies, Pennsylvania above all after 1681, competed for the same migrants;
fewer of the English poor had reason to gamble five years on the tobacco
coast.

**Survival rose.** The planter's ledger flipped: as the worked example shows,
a lifetime claim is only worth its premium when lifetimes are long, and after
mid-century they were.

**The supply of captives opened.** The Royal African Company lost its monopoly
in 1698, and separate traders multiplied direct shipments, so Virginia's
enslaved population climbed from about 7 percent of the colony in 1680 toward
two-fifths by 1750.

### The fourth pressure: Bacon's Rebellion, 1676

The fourth pressure was political, and it has a date. In 1676 Nathaniel Bacon
led backcountry planters, freed ex-servants, servants, and enslaved men in an
armed rising against Governor William Berkeley's government, demanding war
against neighboring Indian nations and relief from taxes; the rebels burned
Jamestown on September 19.

Bacon died of dysentery in October, the rebellion collapsed, and 23 rebels
were hanged. The detail that concentrated planter minds appears in the
surrender records: the last band under arms was about eighty enslaved Black
men and twenty English servants, holding out together.

Edmund Morgan's [*American Slavery, American Freedom*](https://wwnorton.com/books/9780393324945)
(1975) states the structural reading this figure compresses: a colony whose
bottom class was armed, English, and due to go free every few years was
ungovernable, and the assembly's answer was to shrink that class and split it.

Statute by statute after 1676, poor whites got lower taxes, statutory dues,
and a legal rank above every Black Virginian; Africans, imported in growing
numbers, got the code of 1705. The turn was already underway in 1662; the
rebellion told the planter elite what the alternative looked like, and the
legislation accelerated.

<KeyFact>
The turn to African slavery was overdetermined by economics and demography and
dated by politics: the statute record after 1676 diverges by race, legal
privileges for poor whites against a hardening code for Black Virginians, a
pattern Morgan reads as the planter elite replacing a temporary, armed, English
underclass with a permanent, disarmed, African one.
</KeyFact>

## One Atlantic economy by 1750

### The endpoints: planter gentry south, farm-and-merchant north

By the mid-eighteenth century the divergence was complete at the top of
society as well as the bottom. Virginia and South Carolina were ruled by a
gentry of great planters atop smallholders, tenants, and an enslaved workforce
that was over 40 percent of Virginia's population and a majority of South
Carolina's.

New England remained a region of family farms, merchants, and artisans, with
enslaved people about 2 to 3 percent of its population.

  caption="One economy, four shores. Arrow width tracks trade value: the largest exchange was the direct Britain–mainland pair, and every route touched slave-grown goods."

### The connections: fish, sugar, rum, and captives

The two regions were nonetheless shores of one economy. New England's fish,
grain, lumber, and horses fed the Caribbean sugar islands, whose enslaved
majorities grew the sugar and molasses that New England distilled into rum.

Rhode Island merchants alone mounted roughly a thousand slaving voyages in the
eighteenth century, carrying over 100,000 captives. The difference between the
regions was the place of slavery in them: central and constitutive in the
South, peripheral but profitable in New England. It was never absence.

<Aside>
What the "triangle trade" diagram gets wrong. The schoolbook triangle (rum to
Africa, captives to the islands, sugar home) describes a real but minor
circuit. Most ships sailed shuttles, not triangles: Boston to Barbados and
back, or the tobacco fleet direct to London. And the largest flow by value was
the bilateral Britain–mainland exchange of colonial staples for manufactures
and credit. The figure above weights its arrows accordingly; the triangle
survives in it as the thin rum line, not the trunk route. The system's
integration, not its shape, is the point: sugar, tobacco, fish, and people
priced one another across every leg.
</Aside>

### The payoff ahead: liberty defined against unfreedom

The eighteenth-century payoff is a hierarchy each region would defend in its
own vocabulary. English colonists in both regions understood themselves as
uniquely free, and by 1750 that freedom was defined in practice against the
unfree labor beneath it: the servant's term, and the slave's lifetime.

The colonies between the two societies of this lesson, New York and
Pennsylvania, are the subject of <Ref lesson="middle-colonies">the next
lesson</Ref>; how the vocabulary of liberty detonated after 1763 is the
subject of <Ref lesson="american-revolution">the imperial-crisis
lesson</Ref>.

<Exercise>
Using the figures in this lesson: about 120,000 migrants entered the
Chesapeake across the seventeenth century, and its population in 1700 was
about 90,000; about 21,000 migrants entered New England by 1642, essentially
none after, and its population in 1700 was about 91,000. Compute the ratio of
1700 population to cumulative migrants for each region, and state the
demographic fact each ratio compresses.

<Solution>
Chesapeake: $90{,}000 / 120{,}000 = 0.75$. New England:
$91{,}000 / 21{,}000 \approx 4.3$.

A ratio below 1 means the region held fewer people than had entered it: deaths
(plus some return migration) consumed the entire century's immigration and part
of the natural increase besides, so the population existed only because the
inflow continued. A ratio of 4.3 with the inflow shut by 1642 means the growth
was internal: births exceeded deaths strongly enough to double the population
roughly every generation. The two ratios are the two labor systems in embryo:
a society that must import workers, and a society that raises them.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Redo the planter's ledger with different terms: a servant costs £12 for a
four-year term, and an enslaved laborer costs £25. Compute the cost per
labor-year for the servant, then find the break-even survival time above which
the enslaved laborer is the cheaper purchase, ignoring heredity. Then state
how the 1662 statute changes the comparison without changing any price.

<Solution>
Servant: $£12 / 4 = £3$ per labor-year.

Break-even for the enslaved laborer: cost per labor-year is $£25/E$ for
expected survival $E$; setting $£25/E = £3$ gives $E = 25/3 \approx 8.3$
years. If a purchased laborer can be expected to survive more than about 8
years, the lifetime purchase is cheaper per year of labor; under early-century
mortality (about 5 years) it is not.

The 1662 statute makes the child of every enslaved woman the owner's property.
The £25 then buys not one stream of labor-years but a potentially growing set
of them, so the effective break-even falls below 8.3 years even if the
purchased laborer's own survival is unchanged. Heredity converts the purchase
from a wasting asset into a compounding one; no price needs to move.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The full ladder ran more rungs than the two the prose quotes. Step through the
sequence below — each frame adds one statute to what Virginia law says — and,
after each frame, state in one sentence which route out of bondage the
assembly has just closed. Then place the two rungs the sequence and the prose
compress, 1669 and 1682, on the ladder: state what each did and which quoted
rung it extends.

<FigureSequence
  name="statute-ladder"
  alt="Four frames adding one Virginia statute at a time to a panel titled 'What Virginia law now says': 1662 children follow the mother's status, 1667 baptism does not alter bondage, 1691 manumission requires deporting the freed person, 1705 non-Christian imports are slaves and killing a slave under correction is no felony"
  caption="Four rungs, 1662–1705: each statute closes one exit. Read in order, the sequence is the argument."
/>

<Solution>
Frame by frame. **1662**: the exit through a free father closes — status
follows the mother, so the child of every enslaved woman is born enslaved
regardless of paternity. **1667**: the exit through conversion closes —
baptism "doth not alter the condition of the person as to his bondage or
ffreedome," so Christianity no longer marks the boundary of enslaveability.
**1691**: the exit through an owner's grant closes inside Virginia — a master
may still free a slave, but only by paying "for the transportation of such
negro or negroes out of the countrey within six moneths," so freedom cannot
accumulate in the colony. **1705**: the remaining gaps close — imports "who
were not christians in their native country" are slaves on arrival whatever
they profess later, and the whole is consolidated into one code.

The two compressed rungs. **1669**, the act "about the casuall killing of
slaves": a slave who died under a master's correction produced no felony,
"since it cannot be presumed that prepensed malice … should induce any man to
destroy his owne estate." It removed legal protection of life rather than an
exit from bondage, and the 1705 code absorbed it (the master "free and acquit
of all punishment"), so it extends the 1705 rung. **1682**, the
[import act](https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/an-act-to-repeale-a-former-law-makeing-indians-and-others-ffree-1682/):
servants "whether Negroes, Moors, Mollattoes or Indians" whose "parentage and
native country are not christian at the time of their first purchase" were
slaves on arrival. It moves the 1667 logic from the font to the dockside —
religion at purchase, not baptism after, fixes status — and the 1705 act
restates it as the general rule.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 1667 act declares that "the conferring of baptisme doth not alter the
condition of the person as to his bondage or ffreedome," and its preamble
states that masters, "freed from this doubt," may now permit their slaves to
be baptized. State the problem the statute solved for slaveowners, the legal
assumption it overrode, and the role Elizabeth Key's 1656 case plays in
explaining why the assembly acted.

<Solution>
The problem: under the inherited assumption that Christians do not enslave
fellow Christians, every baptism was a potential freedom suit, so masters
rationally blocked conversion, which collided with the official claim that
colonization was a Christianizing mission. The statute removed the cost of
conversion by severing baptism from status: masters could now allow baptism
precisely because it changed nothing.

The overridden assumption: religious status, not ancestry, marked the boundary
of enslaveability. After 1667 the boundary had to rest on something baptism
could not change, and the statutes settled on descent, completing the shift
begun in 1662.

Key's case supplies the motive: she had won freedom in 1656 partly on the
ground of her baptism, demonstrating in court that the religious door out of
bondage was real. The 1662 act closed her paternity argument; the 1667 act
closed her baptism argument. The ladder's rungs answer specific, named
victories.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
State the two properties that distinguish indentured servitude from slavery as
legal institutions, and for each, name the Virginia statute (year and
operative rule) that stripped it from enslaved people. Then explain why
"slavery is permanent indentured servitude" fails as a definition even after
those statutes.

<Solution>
The two properties: the term (an indenture expires; slavery is for life) and
heritability (a servant's child is born free; after the 1662 act, "all
children borne in this country shalbe held bond or free only according to the
condition of the mother," a slave's child is enslaved). The 1662 statute
established heredity; the lifetime term was fixed case by case from 1640
(John Punch, "for the time of his natural Life") and generalized by the 1682
import act and the 1705 act's rule that non-Christian imports "shall be
accounted and be slaves."

The definition still fails because the institutions diverged in legal
personhood, not only duration. A servant could sue a master, and stripping a
servant for whipping required a justice's order under the 1705 code; the same
code held that a slave killed under "correction" produced no felony (a rule on
the books since 1669), and a companion act classed slaves as real estate.
Permanence plus heredity plus the loss of legal personhood is a different
institution, not a longer contract.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Construct the argument that Bacon's Rebellion accelerated the turn to racial
slavery, using two pieces of evidence from this lesson: the composition of the
last rebel band, and the divergent content of post-1676 legislation. Then state
one fact from the statute record that blocks the stronger claim that the
rebellion caused the turn.

<Solution>
Composition: the last band under arms was about eighty enslaved Black men and
twenty English servants. The rebellion therefore demonstrated to the planter
elite not merely unrest but a coalition: the armed poor of both statuses
fighting together against the government, which is the precise coalition a
labor system built on temporary English servitude kept reproducing, since it
released armed, land-hungry freedmen every year.

Divergence: the legislation that followed treated the coalition's two halves
in opposite directions: taxes and dues favorable to poor whites, including the
statutory musket of 1705, against the 1682 import act, the 1691 manumission
bar, and the 1705 code for Black Virginians. Splitting the coalition by race
was the legislature's recorded response, which is what "accelerated and
directed" means.

The blocking fact: the two foundational statutes, 1662 (heredity) and 1667
(baptism), predate the rebellion by a decade or more. The legal construction
was underway before 1676 for economic and demographic reasons, so the
rebellion cannot be its cause; it fixed the pace and the political logic, not
the direction.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the three-question method from the lesson on Indigenous America
(records, argues, omits) to John Dane's "Declaration of Remarkable
Providences," treating it as a primary source for New England colonization.

<Solution>
Records: the mechanics of one family's migration decision (parental
opposition, its resolution, the family's subsequent emigration), the trades
and towns of an ordinary settler's life (a tailor, Roxbury, then Ipswich), and
by extension the family network the pedigree chart documents: marriages,
births, and survivals across generations in one county.

Argues: that a life is a sequence of divine interventions. The genre is the
providence narrative, so every recorded event is selected and framed as
evidence of God's steering, including the Bible-opening that settled the
migration. The document's thesis is theological, not demographic.

Omits: everything that does not read as providence. Economic motives (land
prices, wages, the cloth trade's condition), the political circumstances of
the 1630s, the Indigenous people on whose emptied coast Ipswich stood, and
the ordinary years in which nothing interpretable happened. The demographic
value historians extract from Dane, longevity, family migration, and marriage
networks, is carried in details incidental to his argument, which is exactly
why it is credible: he had no thesis about demography to defend.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Chesapeake system rested on tobacco, an American cultivar. Explain the
colony's labor system as a second-order consequence of the Columbian Exchange
from the lesson on Indigenous America, naming one eastbound transfer and one
westbound transfer in the causal chain.

<Solution>
Eastbound: tobacco itself. The plant crossed to Europe after contact, created
a mass consumer market there, and that market's demand is what made Virginia
land worth working at scale; without an eastbound American crop Europeans
wanted, there is no export economy requiring imported labor.

Westbound: people as labor, moving along the channels the Exchange opened,
and, before them, the pathogens of the contact lesson. Old World diseases had
emptied the coasts that made cheap land available (Plymouth on Patuxet's
fields is the New England case), and the labor to work that land was itself a
westbound transfer: first 120,000 English migrants, then captive Africans in
growing numbers. The Chesapeake's sequence, cheap land plus scarce labor plus
an eastbound cash crop, is the Columbian Exchange operating as an economic
system: the exchange of plants and pathogens in the first generation set the
prices of land and labor that the exchange of people answered in the next.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

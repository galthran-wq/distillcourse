The Articles of Confederation created a league that could neither tax nor enforce its own decisions; the Constitution of 1787 replaced it with a national government assembled out of compromises.

The course's argument has established a selected, self-governing population
that compounded institutions from shipboard compact to colonial assembly, a
war for independence won at measurable odds, and, in
<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the previous lesson</Ref>, the design
vocabulary the framers carried into the room: Polybius's mixed triad, the
English rights clauses, Montesquieu's separated powers, and the interest
machine that replaced 1776's virtue premise. What stands open is how that
vocabulary became a working government. This lesson supplies the machining,
in order: the Articles' failure, demonstrated in revenue and rebellion; the
state constitutions as the rehearsal; the Virginia and New Jersey plans and
the July 16 vote that traded between them; the clauses that wrote slavery
into the apportionment arithmetic; the seven-article machine the drafting
committees produced; and the September 17 signing, with Article V, the
designed escape hatch, as the closing check. Whether the states would
accept the design is <Ref lesson="ratification-and-bill-of-rights">the next
lesson's</Ref> subject.

## A league of states: what the Articles could and could not do

The first national constitution was drafted alongside the Declaration and
ratified in March 1781, when Maryland, the last holdout, signed on. The
[Articles of Confederation](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/artconf.asp)
say what they are in their own text: Article II reserves to each state "its
sovereignty, freedom, and independence," and Article III calls the whole "a
firm league of friendship." The government of the league was a one-house
Congress in which each state cast one vote, with no executive, no courts,
and a nine-of-thirteen supermajority required for anything important: war,
treaties, borrowing, spending.

What Congress could not do defined the decade. It could not tax: it could
only send the states *requisitions*, invoices for their shares of the
common expense, which the states paid late, partially, or not at all. It
could not regulate commerce, so states taxed each other's trade. It could
not act on individuals at all: every decision had to be executed by
thirteen state governments the league had no means to compel.

<Figure
  src="./figures/articles-ledger.svg"
  alt="Two-column ledger comparing the Articles of Confederation and the Constitution of 1787 across five powers: taxation, commerce regulation, executive, courts, and amendment — the Articles column shows requisitions only, no power, none, none, and unanimity; the 1787 column shows all five filled"
  caption="The ledger the delegates carried to Philadelphia: five powers a government needs, and what the Articles gave the league."
/>

The amendment rule turned these defects into permanent ones. Article XIII
required the consent of every state legislature for any alteration, and
the rule was tested twice on the same repair. In 1781 Congress proposed the
*impost*, a five percent duty on imports that would have given the league
its own revenue; twelve states approved and Rhode Island's refusal killed
it. A redrafted impost in 1783 died the same way when New York attached
conditions no other state would accept. The league's only revenue plan was
vetoed by one state, twice.

<KeyFact>
Amending the Articles required all thirteen states, and the rule bit: the
impost, the Confederation's only plan for an independent revenue, was
approved by twelve states and killed by one — Rhode Island in 1782, then
New York in 1786.
</KeyFact>

The record was not uniformly bleak, and the exception is instructive. In
July 1787 the Confederation Congress passed the
[Northwest Ordinance](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/nworder.asp),
organizing the territory northwest of the Ohio River into future states
admitted as equals, with slavery banned north of the river. The league
could legislate for land it owned outright; what it could not do was
govern the thirteen sovereignties it served.

### Not the Iroquois: the confederation's pedigree

The Articles have a documented paper lineage, and it runs through the
colonists' own prior confederations. The chain starts with the United
Colonies of New England (1643), the four-colony defense league that
<Ref lesson="natives-and-english">the course last met</Ref> directing
King Philip's War, and continues through Benjamin Franklin's Albany Plan
of Union (1754), drafted at a congress convened to manage the alliance
with the Iroquois Six Nations and never adopted.

A popular counter-claim, endorsed by a congressional resolution in 1988,
holds that the Iroquois Confederacy was the model for the Articles and
the Constitution. The structural record rejects it.

The structures diverge on every design choice. The Iroquois Grand
Council, the confederacy's governing body, was composed of members
appointed for life by designated clan families, acted only by unanimity,
and apportioned no seats by population. The Albany Plan proposed a
council elected by colonial legislatures to three-year terms,
apportioned by contribution, deciding by majority; the Articles kept
election and majority-or-nine-state rule.

<Figure
  src="./figures/iroquois-albany-articles.svg"
  alt="Three-column table comparing the Iroquois Grand Council, the Albany Plan of 1754, and the Articles of Confederation on three rows — selection: life appointment by designated clan families, election by colonial assemblies to three-year terms, annual appointment by state legislatures; apportionment: fixed per nation, by contribution, one state one vote; decision rule: unanimity, majority, majority with nine of thirteen for major powers — with the Grand Council's unanimity and the Articles' unanimous-amendment rule linked as the one shared feature"
  caption="The structural comparison, row by row: the Articles descend from the Albany Plan's design choices, not the Grand Council's — and the one rule they share with the Longhouse is the one that killed them."
/>

What the paper trail does document is descent from the 1643 league and
from English plans of union, William Penn's of 1697 and Joseph
Galloway's of 1774 among them, not from the Longhouse. The defensible
version of the claim is acquaintance and admiration: Franklin knew the
Iroquois system from treaty diplomacy and praised its endurance while
promoting his own plan. And the one feature the Articles did share with
the Grand Council (unanimity, confined to amendment) is the feature that
killed them.

### Springfield: the demonstration

Diagnosis on paper moved few people; an armed rebellion moved the
political class. Through the fall of 1786, indebted farmers in western
Massachusetts, squeezed between hard-money taxes and court judgments that
took farms for arrears, closed county courthouses at musket-point to stop
the foreclosures. The movement gathered under Daniel Shays, a decorated
Continental captain, and on January 25, 1787 his column advanced on the
federal arsenal at Springfield, on the Connecticut River in western
Massachusetts. The militia defending the arsenal fired two cannon rounds
into the ranks; four insurgents died, the column broke, and a state army
under Benjamin Lincoln scattered the remnant ten days later at Petersham,
a hill town some thirty-five miles to the northeast.

  caption="January 25, 1787: Shays's column walks into the militia holding the federal arsenal at Springfield. The detail that condemned the Articles was fiscal, not military. Congress
could raise no force worth the name, and Massachusetts itself could not
fund one: Lincoln's army marched on private money, over six thousand
pounds subscribed by some 125 Boston merchants. A government that has to
pass the hat to put down a rebellion has failed a defining test, and the
men who would sit at Philadelphia said so in those months. Washington, to
Henry Lee in October 1786: "Let us have a government by which our lives,
liberties, and properties will be secured, or let us know the worst at
once." The alarm was not universal. Jefferson, watching from Paris,
refused it even in retrospect: "the tree of liberty must be refreshed
from time to time with the blood of patriots and tyrants," he wrote to
William Stephens Smith on November 13, 1787, two months after the
convention had adjourned. But in the winter of 1786–87 the alarmed were
the ones organizing.

Machinery for a response already existed. Four months before Springfield,
a twelve-delegate trade conference at Annapolis, Maryland's capital on
the Chesapeake, had adjourned for want of attendance after recommending a general convention at Philadelphia to
consider the state of the union. The rebellion turned that memorandum
into a summons. On February 21, 1787 Congress endorsed a convention "for
the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation,"
and every state but Rhode Island eventually sent delegates. The mandate
was revision, and only revision.

## The rehearsal: thirteen constitutions on the shelf

The government the Articles lacked already existed in working form,
thirteen times over, at state scale. Between 1776 and 1780 eleven states
had written new constitutions, and the differences among them amounted to
a decade-long comparative experiment in republican design. Three documents
mark the range: Virginia and Pennsylvania at the experiment's start,
Massachusetts, drawing on their results, at its end.

### Three documents, three answers per question

<Figure
  src="./figures/state-constitutions-compared.svg"
  alt="Three-column table comparing the Virginia 1776, Pennsylvania 1776, and Massachusetts 1780 constitutions on three rows — suffrage: freehold qualification, all taxpaying men, property qualification; church establishment: Anglican establishment until 1786, none, town-supported ministers; executive: governor chosen by legislature with no veto, no governor but a 12-member council, popularly elected governor with a veto"
  caption="Three answers to each of three design questions, 1776–1780: the framers had comparative evidence, not theory alone."
/>

The columns disagree on every row. Virginia kept the colonial freehold
suffrage and a governor chosen annually by the legislature with no veto;
Pennsylvania enfranchised every taxpaying man and abolished the governor
outright in favor of an elected council; Massachusetts in 1780 kept a
property test but gave its popularly elected governor a veto that
two-thirds of the legislature could override. Establishment split the same
way: Virginia disestablished its Anglican church in stages, completed by
the 1786 Statute for Religious Freedom; Pennsylvania had never had an
establishment; Massachusetts kept town-supported ministers until 1833.
The previous lesson established what a decade of practice did to these
designs: the unchecked assemblies produced the paper-money and
debtor-relief record Madison catalogued as the "vices" of the system, and
Massachusetts supplied the repair, a constitution drafted by a special
convention and ratified by the towns, standing above the legislature it
created (<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the sovereignty relocation</Ref>).
Philadelphia would reuse both the findings and the device.

### The delegates had written the record they used

The delegates knew this record because they had made it. Of the 55 men
who attended, 26 had served in state legislatures, most during the
constitution-writing years; 44 had sat in the Continental Congress; and
8 had signed the Declaration.
Donald Lutz's textual analysis in *The Origins of American
Constitutionalism* measures the dependence in the product itself: the
Constitution refers to the state constitutions, directly or by
implication, more than fifty times across forty-two of its sections; the
document assumes their existence in everything from voter qualifications
(the federal franchise is defined as each state's own) to the selection
of senators and electors. "The United States was not founded in 1787,"
Lutz concludes; "the nation was refounded upon a base that had been laid
earlier."

<KeyFact>
The 1787 text presumes the state constitutions: more than fifty
references across forty-two of its sections. The dependence begins with
the franchise itself: who may vote for the House is defined as whoever
each state lets vote for its own lower house.
</KeyFact>

## Philadelphia: two plans and one vote

The convention that assembled in the Pennsylvania State House, the same
Philadelphia room where the Declaration had been signed eleven years
earlier, reached a quorum of seven states on May 25, 1787. Fifty-five
delegates from twelve states attended in all. Washington was elected
president of the convention unanimously, and the delegates voted a rule
of secrecy: doors closed, no publication, which let positions change
without public commitment. For what happened inside, the principal record
is James Madison's notes, taken daily from a seat at the front.

<Remark title="Madison's notes are a record, not a transcript">
Madison reconstructed each day's debate from shorthand in the evenings,
and he revised the manuscript on and off for decades; it was published
only in 1840, after his death, and it is the best record of the
convention, not a verbatim one. Its credibility rests partly on what it
records against its author: the notes document Madison's own defeats in
detail. Quotations from the convention in this chapter are quotations
from Madison's notes.
</Remark>

On May 29 Edmund Randolph, Virginia's governor, presented the fifteen
resolutions drafted largely by Madison: the **Virginia Plan**. It
ignored the Articles entirely: a national legislature of two houses, both
apportioned by population or contribution; a national executive and
judiciary; and a congressional veto over any state law. On the third day
of business the convention resolved "that a national Government ought to
be established consisting of a supreme Legislative, Executive and
Judiciary." A body convened for the sole and express purpose of revising
the Articles had, within a week of getting a quorum, voted to replace
them.

  alt="Half-length oil portrait of James Madison as an old man: powdered grey hair, a pale face turned slightly toward the viewer, a black coat and waistcoat over a white stock and ruffled shirt front, against a dark olive ground with a green curtain at the right"
  caption="James Madison near seventy, painted by Gilbert Stuart some thirty-four years after the convention. In 1787 he was thirty-six — principal draftsman of the Virginia Plan, and the man taking the notes this chapter quotes."

Proportional representation in both houses would have handed control to
Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts, and on June 15 William
Paterson of New Jersey answered for the small states with the **New
Jersey Plan**. It kept the
one-house, one-state-one-vote Congress of the Articles but granted it
what the league had lacked: import duties, a stamp tax, power over
commerce, a plural executive, a federal judiciary, and a clause declaring
national law supreme over state law. The comparison is diagnostic: even the small
states' plan conceded taxes and supremacy. By mid-June nobody in the room
was defending the Articles as they stood; the fight was over
representation.

<Figure
  src="./figures/two-plans-bargain.svg"
  alt="Three-panel diagram: the Virginia Plan with two houses both proportional to population; the New Jersey Plan with one house of equal state votes; and the Connecticut Compromise merging them — a House apportioned by population and a Senate of equal state votes, with the Senate cell highlighted as the changed element"
  caption="Two representation schemes and the trade that merged them; the Senate's equal vote is the small states' price."
/>

The design space was wider than either plan. On June 18 Alexander
Hamilton, one of New York's three delegates, held the floor for six hours praising the British constitution as "the best in the
world" and proposing an executive and senate serving during good
behavior, in effect for life. No delegation supported it; the speech
survives as a measure of how open the question still was in June.

### July 16: the trade

Through late June and early July the convention deadlocked on the Senate,
with threats of walkout from both sides. The resolution was the
**Connecticut Compromise**, the split Roger Sherman of Connecticut had proposed early
and a committee reported on July 5: a House apportioned by population, in
which money bills would originate, and a Senate in which each state kept
an equal vote. It passed on July 16 by five states to four with
Massachusetts divided: a majority of one state, with the two largest
delegations voting no.

This was a deadlock-breaking trade, not a theory. No delegate argued that
equal state suffrage in one house followed from a principle of
representation; the small states had the votes to block everything, and
the price of a union that included them was an equal Senate. Madison
never accepted the logic and kept pressing his congressional veto over
state laws until it died in committee. By Forrest McDonald's count, of 71
proposals Madison introduced or backed at the convention, 40 lost: the
"Father of the Constitution" was on the losing side of most of his own
convention, which is the cleanest evidence that the document had no
single architect. It is the residue of a negotiation.

## Slavery in the arithmetic

A House apportioned by population forced a question the Articles had
never had to answer: who counts. Delegates from states where enslaved
people were two-fifths of the population wanted them counted for
representation, while the counted themselves would be neither voters nor
constituents; delegates from the
North objected that people held as property could not be counted as
constituents.

### The ratio off the shelf

The convention reached for a ratio it did not have to invent: a
failed 1783 amendment to the Articles had proposed apportioning
requisitions by population with three-fifths of the enslaved counted.
The same fraction was written into Article I, Section 2: representation
and direct taxes apportioned by "the whole Number of free Persons"
plus "three fifths of all other Persons."

The clause is an apportionment formula, not a statement that a person is
three-fifths of one; enslaved people got no vote and no representation
under any ratio. Its practical content was a transfer of federal power to
slaveholding states, and the transfer is computable.

### The bonus, computed

<Example title="the three-fifths bonus, computed for Virginia">
The 1790 census counted 454,983 free inhabitants and 292,627 enslaved
people in Virginia. Under the 1792 apportionment act, each state received
one House seat per 33,000 of apportionment population, fractions dropped.

With the three-fifths clause, Virginia's apportionment base is

$$
454{,}983 + \tfrac{3}{5}\times 292{,}627 \approx 630{,}559,
$$

giving $630{,}559 / 33{,}000 = 19.1$, so **19 seats**, the delegation
Virginia actually seated. On free population alone the base is 454,983,
giving $13.8$, so **13 seats**. The clause was worth six House seats to
Virginia, and, since electoral votes equal seats plus two senators, six
electoral votes as well.
</Example>

Summed across the South the bonus set the balance of the House: in the
apportionment of 1793, slaveholding states held 47 seats where free
population alone would have given them 33. The margin compounded in the
electoral college for seventy years; Akhil Amar's accounting concludes
that without it John Adams, not Jefferson, would have won the presidency
in 1800. The <Ref lesson="sectional-crisis">sectional-crisis lesson</Ref>
prices the full political consequences.

### The two clauses that bought the South

Two more clauses completed the bargain, both from the convention's final
weeks. In late August, South Carolina and Georgia, whose delegates said
plainly they would not join without it, traded votes with New England:
Congress was forbidden to prohibit "the Migration or Importation of such
Persons as any of the States now existing shall think proper to admit"
before 1808 (twenty years of protected slave trading), and in exchange
navigation acts would pass by simple majority, the commercial regime New
England wanted. Days later, with little debate, the convention added the
fugitive clause of Article IV: a "Person held to Service or Labour" who
escaped into another state "shall be delivered up," a guarantee adapted
from the Northwest Ordinance passed that same summer. Congress banned
the Atlantic trade at the first legal moment, effective January 1,
1808; the other clauses stood until the Civil War amendments.

<KeyFact>
In the House of 1793, the three-fifths clause gave slaveholding states
47 seats where free population alone would have given 33, a
fourteen-seat bonus that ran for seventy years. The word *slavery* never
appears in the text that grants it: the document says "other Persons,"
"such Persons," and "Person held to Service or Labour."
</KeyFact>

The euphemisms were deliberate; delegates on both sides said the word
aloud and kept it off the parchment. The
<Ref lesson="american-revolution">revolutionary paradox</Ref> the course
met in the Declaration is here in its constitutional form: the same
document that begins "We the People" counts persons it does not
represent, and *slavery* enters the Constitution's text only in 1865, in
the amendment that abolishes it.

## Seven articles: the machine on paper

Turning resolutions into a text was committee work. The Committee of
Detail's draft of early August ran to twenty-three articles; the
Committee of Style, whose pen was Gouverneur Morris, compressed them in
mid-September into seven.

### "We the People": a drafting necessity

Morris also rewrote the opening. The August
draft had begun "We the people of the States of New Hampshire,
Massachusetts…," a roll of thirteen names; Article VII's own rule made
the roll impossible, since no one could know which nine states would
ratify first, and Morris replaced it with "We the People of the United
States." A drafting necessity produced the text's most quoted phrase,
and made the <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">constituent device</Ref> of
the previous lesson, the people ordaining fundamental law over their
governments' heads, the document's first announced fact.

  caption="Shallus wrote the argument into the letterforms. The engrossed parchment was penned over a weekend by Jacob Shallus,
assistant clerk of the Pennsylvania assembly; his name appears nowhere on
it. The oversized "We the People" is his calligraphy: the emphasis is the
scribe's, and it has carried the document's argument ever since.

**The engrosser.** Shallus was paid thirty dollars for the weekend's

</Aside>

### Three armed branches on four rails

<Figure
  src="./figures/seven-articles-machine.svg"
  alt="Block diagram of the seven articles: three branch blocks for Article I legislative, Article II executive, and Article III judicial with check arrows between them — veto, override, appointments and treaties, tenure during good behavior — set on four framing rails labeled Article IV states' relations, Article V amendment, Article VI supremacy, and Article VII ratification by nine state conventions"
  caption="The machine's floor plan: three armed branches on four structural rails. Article I is the engine — it holds the taxing, spending, and war powers the Articles withheld."
/>

The seven articles sort into machine and frame. Articles I through III
build the branches (legislative, executive, judicial) as the mutually
armed powers whose logic <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the previous
lesson</Ref> traced: veto and override, Senate consent to appointments
and treaties, judges on tenure during good behavior. *Separation of
powers* here means separated and interlocked, not isolated; each branch
holds a piece of the others' function as its weapon. The proportions
record the framers' expectations: Article I runs ten sections and
roughly half the text of a document totaling about 4,500 words. It holds
everything the Confederation had lacked: tax, commerce, war, and the
power to legislate for individuals. Articles IV through VII are the
frame: interstate obligations (IV), amendment (V), supremacy of federal
law "any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary
notwithstanding" (VI), and ratification (VII).

The whole is *federalism*: two levels of government, each acting directly
on the same citizens, each supreme in its assigned sphere, in Madison's
later formula in
[Federalist 39](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed39.asp),
"neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of
both." The word appears nowhere in the text; the structure is the
definition. Article VII applied the Massachusetts procedure at
continental scale: ratification not by the state legislatures the
document would constrain but by elected conventions of the people. Nine
of thirteen would suffice, the Confederation's own working
supermajority, chosen in part because everyone expected Rhode Island to
refuse.

<KeyFact>
Article I is half the machine: ten sections holding the tax, commerce,
and war powers the Articles withheld. The framers built the branch they
expected to dominate first, then armed the other two against it.
</KeyFact>

## September 17: signed, entrenched, and amendable

<Figure
  src="./figures/franklin-rising-sun-chair.jpg"

  caption="The carving Franklin was reading: the gilded half-sun on the crest rail of the president's chair (John Folwell, Philadelphia, 1779). The sun sits exactly on its carved horizon, rays up — a design that reads equally as rising or setting, which is what gave the remark its point."

The convention's product was signed the way it was made: by compromise,
with visible dissent. Of the 55 delegates who had attended, 14 had
already gone home; of the 41 in the room on September 17, three refused
to sign: Edmund Randolph, who had introduced the Virginia Plan; Elbridge
Gerry of Massachusetts; and George Mason of Virginia, whose circulated
objections opened with the sentence "There is no Declaration of Rights."
Thirty-eight delegates signed in the room, and George Read of Delaware
added a thirty-ninth name for John Dickinson, gone home ill, who had
authorized Read to sign for him. The thirty-nine names stand under a
formula drafted by Morris to paper over the dissent, "Done in Convention
by the Unanimous Consent of the States present": unanimous by
delegation, not by delegate. Franklin, watching the president's chair,
said by Madison's account that he could now tell the sun carved on it
was "a rising and not a setting sun"; the remark reaches us through
Madison's notes and carries their authority, no more.

### Article V: the escape hatch, entrenched twice

The document's slavery clauses and its Senate bargain were not merely
adopted; they were bolted down. Article V, the amendment article,
corrects the Articles' unanimity rule: two routes to propose (two-thirds
of both houses, or a convention on application of two-thirds of the
states), two routes to ratify (three-quarters of legislatures or of
conventions). But it contains two entrenchments: no amendment could
touch the slave-trade clause before 1808, and no state may lose its equal
Senate vote without its own consent. The convention's two hardest-bought
compromises were written into the escape hatch itself.

The figure is the closing check on the lesson's argument. The
Constitution's durability (the oldest written national constitution
still in force, per the measurement in
<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the previous lesson</Ref>) is not the
durability of a perfect text. Seven of its clauses, including everything
it conceded to slavery, were later struck or superseded through the
procedure of Article V; the document has run more than two centuries on
twenty-seven amendments precisely because amendment requires broad
consensus but not unanimity. The framers' one unambiguous verdict on the Articles was
written into the repair rule.

What is now established: the Articles failed on revenue and enforcement
and could not be amended; the state constitutions supplied a decade of
tested parts and the ratifying device; the Virginia and New Jersey plans
were resolved by the July 16 trade; the three-fifths, slave-trade, and
fugitive clauses bought the South's adherence at a computable price; and
the result was a seven-article machine, signed by 39 of 55, entrenched in
two places and amendable everywhere else. The document now had to survive
contact with the public. <Ref lesson="ratification-and-bill-of-rights">The
next lesson</Ref> follows the nine-month fight in the state conventions,
Federalist against Anti-Federalist, vote by vote, and the Bill of Rights
that Mason's one-sentence objection grew into.

<Exercise>
Without looking back, place these eight items in chronological order and
state in one clause what each contributed to the road from Confederation
to Constitution: the Springfield arsenal confrontation; the Articles of
Confederation ratified; the Connecticut Compromise vote; Rhode Island's
impost veto; the Annapolis convention; the Virginia Plan presented;
Congress's "sole and express purpose" resolution; the signing.

<Solution>
Articles ratified (March 1781): the league constituted, with requisitions
and unanimous amendment. Rhode Island's impost veto (1782): one state
kills the only revenue plan, proving Article XIII unworkable. Annapolis
convention (September 1786): a failed trade conference recommends a
general convention at Philadelphia. Springfield arsenal (January 25,
1787): Shays's column broken by a privately funded militia, the
demonstration that moved the political class. Congressional resolution
(February 21, 1787): Congress endorses a convention "for the sole and
express purpose of revising the Articles." Virginia Plan (May 29, 1787):
Randolph presents Madison's fifteen resolutions; within days the
convention votes for a national government, exceeding the mandate. The
Connecticut Compromise (July 16, 1787): proportional House, equal
Senate, carried five states to four with one divided. Signing (September
17, 1787): 39 of 55 delegates sign; Mason, Randolph, and Gerry refuse.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the worked computation of this chapter for South Carolina. The 1790
census counted 141,979 free inhabitants and 107,094 enslaved people; the
1792 apportionment gave one seat per 33,000, fractions dropped. Compute
the state's House seats with and without the three-fifths clause, state
what the difference establishes, and note one limit of the calculation.

<Solution>
With the clause: $141{,}979 + \tfrac{3}{5}\times 107{,}094 \approx
206{,}235$; $206{,}235/33{,}000 = 6.2$, so 6 seats, the delegation
South Carolina actually seated. Free population only: $141{,}979/33{,}000
= 4.3$, so 4 seats. The clause was worth two of South Carolina's six
seats (a third of its delegation) and two electoral votes. What it
establishes: the three-fifths clause was not symbolic; it moved measured
shares of federal power to slaveholding states, and summing states gives
the 1793 House figure of 47 seats against 33. One limit: the computation
inherits the 1792 method (a fixed ratio with fractions dropped), and
other contemporary methods shift individual states by a seat; the
direction and rough size of the transfer are robust, the exact seat count
is method-dependent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing drill to Madison's notes of the convention. Metadata:
taken daily in shorthand by a leading participant, written out in the
evenings, revised intermittently by the author for five decades,
published posthumously in 1840. State (a) what this provenance permits
the notes to establish, (b) what it cannot, and (c) one feature internal
to the notes that raises their credibility, drawn from this chapter.

<Solution>
(a) The notes can establish the sequence of proposals and votes, the
positions delegates took, and the approximate arguments made: no other
source covers the closed sessions comparably, and the vote tallies can be
corroborated against the convention's official journal. (b) They cannot
establish exact wording (speeches were reconstructed, not transcribed),
and on points where Madison revised decades later, they cannot by
themselves distinguish 1787 from the older Madison's editing; they are
also one participant's selection, taken from a partisan of the Virginia
Plan. (c) The notes record their author losing: the Virginia Plan beaten,
the congressional veto killed, forty of Madison's seventy-one proposals
defeated. A record that documents its own author's defeats in detail is
unlikely to be a self-serving fabrication, which is why historians treat
it as the authoritative record while flagging it as a reconstruction.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A museum panel claims: "The United States Constitution was modeled on the
Iroquois Confederacy." Name two structural facts established in this
lesson that the claim fails to account for, state what would have to be
true for it to survive, and write the defensible version.

<Solution>
Two facts. First, the documented lineage runs elsewhere: the Albany Plan
and the Articles descend on paper from the New England Confederation of
1643 and from English plans of union (Penn 1697, Galloway 1774), and the
Constitution descends from the state constitutions, referenced more than
fifty times in forty-two sections. Second, the structures diverge feature
by feature: the Grand Council's members were appointed for life by
designated clan families, decided by unanimity, and were not apportioned
to population, while the American confederations used election to fixed
terms, majority or nine-state rule, and (in the Albany Plan and the 1787
House) proportional representation. For the claim to survive, the record
would need convention or drafting documents citing the Iroquois system as
a source and institutional features traceable to it; it shows neither.
Defensible version: colonial leaders, Franklin above all, knew and
admired the Iroquois Confederacy's endurance from treaty diplomacy, and
the Albany congress that produced Franklin's plan was convened to manage
that alliance. Acquaintance and respect are documented; institutional
descent is not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Article VII ratifies the Constitution through elected state conventions,
not state legislatures. Using the sovereignty relocation established in
the previous lesson, explain why this choice was load-bearing rather than
procedural: what claim about the Constitution's legal status depends on
it, and what would ratification by legislatures have implied instead?

<Solution>
The previous lesson traced the relocation: Massachusetts in 1780 had its
constitution drafted by a convention elected for that sole purpose and
ratified by the towns, so the fundamental law owed nothing to the
government it created and stood above it. Article VII generalizes the
device. If state legislatures had ratified, the Constitution would be
their act: legally a treaty among governments, repealable the way the
Articles were amendable, by the parties that made it; a later legislature
could plausibly un-ratify. Ratification by conventions of the people
makes the Constitution the people's own ordinance, which is what lets the
Preamble's "We the People do ordain and establish" function as a legal
mechanism rather than rhetoric: the document binds legislatures because
they never enacted it. The choice also mattered practically
(conventions could be won where sitting legislatures, whose power the
document cut, were hostile), but the legal claim is the load-bearing
part: supremacy over state law (Article VI) is coherent only if the
Constitution's author is the sovereign people, not the subordinated
governments.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Read the signing formula as a source: "Done in Convention by the
Unanimous Consent of the States present the Seventeenth Day of September
… In witness whereof We have hereunto subscribed our Names." State what
the sentence records, what it argues, and what it omits, and what the
omissions establish about the convention's final weeks.

<Solution>
Records: the date, and that every state delegation present voted to
approve; by-state voting was the convention's rule, and each delegation
present did consent. Argues: unanimity. The phrase was drafted by
Gouverneur Morris precisely to let dissenting individuals sign as
witnesses to their states' consent, projecting a united front for the
ratification fight to come. Omits: that three delegates in the room
refused their names (Mason, Randolph, Gerry); that fourteen of the 55 had
left, some in opposition; and that one state, Rhode Island, was not
"present" at any point. The omissions establish that the framers expected
ratification to be contested and were assembling rhetorical weapons for
it: the formula is the first document of the ratification campaign, drafted
before the convention adjourned. Used carefully, the sentence is strong
evidence of the delegations' formal positions and of Federalist strategy,
and no evidence at all of individual delegates' opinions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the following as causes of the Philadelphia convention (necessary,
enabling, or precipitating) and defend the ranking in a short paragraph:
(a) the Articles' unanimity rule and the impost vetoes; (b) the state
legislatures' 1780s record that Madison catalogued as the "vices"; (c)
Shays's Rebellion.

<Solution>
One defensible ranking. Necessary: (a). Without the demonstrated
impossibility of amendment inside the rules (twelve states for the
impost, one against, twice) there is no case for an extralegal general
convention at all; every other grievance could in principle have been
fixed by amendment. Enabling: (b). The vices supplied the reform elite
with its diagnosis and its design agenda (checks on popular assemblies,
supremacy, an independent revenue) and a decade of state-level evidence;
they made a replacement thinkable and specifiable, but they had produced
only the sparsely attended Annapolis meeting on their own. Precipitating:
(c). Springfield fixed the timing: within a month of the rebellion's
collapse Congress endorsed the convention, and states that had ignored
Annapolis appointed delegates. The rebellion changed few minds that the
vices had not already changed; it changed calendars. The ranking's test:
remove (a) and amendment suffices, no convention; remove (b) and there is
no program for one; remove (c) and a convention likely still comes, later
and less attended.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
From memory: name three clauses of the 1787 text that were later
superseded by amendment, the amendment that did it in each case, and one
sentence on why the entrenchment clauses of Article V did not prevent any
of the three.

<Solution>
Any three of: the three-fifths clause (superseded by the 14th Amendment,
1868); state-legislature election of senators (17th, 1913); direct taxes
apportioned by population (16th, 1913); the electoral tie procedure
(12th, 1804); presidential succession left undefined (25th, 1967);
state-citizen suits in federal courts (11th, 1795); the fugitive-slave
clause (13th, 1865). Article V's entrenchments were narrow and
time-limited: the slave-trade protection expired by its own terms in
1808, after which that commerce was banned by ordinary statute, and the
only permanent entrenchment, equal state suffrage in the Senate, has
never been touched. Everything else in the text, the slavery clauses
included, sat inside the ordinary amendment procedure of two-thirds
proposal and three-quarters ratification.
</Solution>

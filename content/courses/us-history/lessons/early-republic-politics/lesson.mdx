The founders regarded organized political parties as illegitimate; within a decade of Washington's inauguration they had built two.

The course's argument has established the design: a compound republic machined from classical and English materials, ratified by margins of 19, 10, and 3, with the Bill of Rights paid as <Ref lesson="ratification-and-bill-of-rights">the price of ratification</Ref>. What stands open is whether the paper machine survives operation. The Constitution supplies no parties, no cabinet, no rules for organized opposition, and its authors classed durable faction as the disease the whole structure existed to control. This lesson runs the machine through its first decade and its first three live-fire tests: a fiscal program that split the government in two, a war panic that criminalized the opposition, and a tied election thrown into a hostile House. It establishes, in order: how Washington's government manufactured its own dignity; how Hamilton's system produced the split; how the French Revolution sorted the halves into parties; how the crisis of 1798 turned party conflict into prosecution; and how the election of 1800 ended with the first peaceful transfer of national power between enemies.

## A monarchical republic: dignity borrowed from the form just rejected

The first problem the new government faced was not policy but standing. On April 30, 1789, George Washington took the oath on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York City, then the temporary capital, and told Congress that "the sacred fire of liberty" was staked on "the experiment entrusted to the hands of the American people."

The experiment's managers did not believe the experiment looked like a government. Every state that mattered in the world was a monarchy; the republics on record were small, and most were dead. A national government that expected obedience from four million people scattered along 1,500 miles of coastline had to look like something people obey.

### Ceremony as deliberate policy: borrowing the grammar of majesty

Washington's answer was ceremony. He held weekly *levees*, formal receptions at which he appeared in black velvet with a dress sword and bowed rather than shook hands; Martha Washington held her own weekly drawing rooms; the president traveled in a coach behind four to six horses, and toured New England in 1789 and the South in 1791 the way monarchs made progresses through their provinces. His birthday was publicly celebrated in the style previously reserved for the king's.

Gilbert Stuart's 1796 Lansdowne portrait, at right, is the program stated in paint: the column, the drapery, the sword, and the orator's gesture are the standard furniture of European state portraiture, applied to an elected magistrate. None of this was drift. The Revolution had removed the crown that made authority legible, and the government's friends were deliberately borrowing the old grammar of majesty to teach obedience to a new and untested state.

### The titles fight: monarchical manner, republican words

The Senate tested how far the borrowing should go. In May 1789 a Senate committee proposed that Washington be addressed as "His Highness the President of the United States of America, and Protector of their Liberties." Vice President John Adams, presiding, pressed the case: when a senator observed that "president" was a common word, Adams answered from the chair that "there are presidents of fire companies and of a cricket club."

The House, led by Madison, refused any title beyond the constitutional one, the Senate conformed, and the address became, permanently, "President of the United States."

<KeyFact>
The first administration deliberately dressed republican legitimacy in monarchical form (levees, progresses, state portraiture, a proposed royal style) because its members doubted a republic could command obedience on its own. The titles fight of 1789 marks the boundary the House drew: monarchical manner, republican words.
</KeyFact>

The ceremony question was a proxy for the real one: how much consolidated power the new government would actually exercise. That question arrived with the Treasury's first report.

## Hamilton's system: the fiscal program that split the government

<Figure
  src="./figures/hamilton-trumbull-1792.jpg"
  alt="Half-length oil portrait of Alexander Hamilton against a dark brown ground: powdered hair swept back, head turned to his right, wearing a buff-olive coat with covered buttons over a white lace-frilled cravat"
  caption="Alexander Hamilton, painted from life by John Trumbull about 1792. Look at what the canvas does not contain: no uniform, no papers, no emblem of office — a man in his middle thirties in a fashionable coat and lace-frilled cravat, dressed like the merchants and creditors his funding system was binding to the union."
  float="left"
  width="m"
/>

The split that became the party system began as an accounting problem. The Revolution had left about \$54 million of federal war debt and roughly \$25 million more owed by the states, much of it trading at a fraction of face value because nobody expected payment. Alexander Hamilton, the first secretary of the treasury, proposed in his January 1790 *Report on Public Credit* to fund the whole at face value: the federal government would **assume** the states' debts, exchange all the old paper for new federal bonds paying reliable interest, and service the interest with import duties and a new excise on whiskey.

A national **Bank of the United States**, chartered in 1791 for twenty years and owned four-fifths by private investors, would hold the government's deposits and issue a stable national paper. The design's point was openly stated: creditors paid at face value acquire a direct interest in the government that pays them. Hamilton was binding the moneyed class to the union's survival.

### Assumption: the first sectional vote, settled over dinner

Each stage of the program cost Hamilton part of his original coalition. Assumption struck Virginia and the states that had already paid their war debts as a tax on the provident to reward speculators, many of whom had bought veterans' paper for pennies. The measure passed the House in July 1790, 34 to 28, only after the famous dinner at Jefferson's lodgings traded Southern votes for a permanent capital on the Potomac.

### The Bank: the split acquires a constitutional theory

The Bank raised a sharper objection: chartering corporations appears nowhere in Congress's enumerated powers. Jefferson and Attorney General Edmund Randolph advised Washington that the bill was unconstitutional; Hamilton answered with the doctrine of *implied powers*, reading the "necessary and proper" clause to cover any means conducive to an enumerated end. Washington signed in February 1791, and the two readings of the Constitution argued in that exchange, strict and broad, have organized American constitutional conflict ever since.

### The excise: the program's one armed rising

The excise was the only part of the system resisted with weapons. In 1794 western Pennsylvania farmers, for whom whiskey was the only way grain crossed the mountains profitably, closed the courts to tax collectors, and Washington marched 13,000 militia across the Alleghenies, a force larger than most he had commanded in the Revolution, against rebels who scattered before it arrived. The *Report on Manufactures* (December 1791), proposing bounties and tariffs to build American industry, Congress declined to enact at all.

### The opposition gets a name

  float="right"

By 1792 the opposition had a shape and a name. Madison, Hamilton's co-author in the ratification campaign, organized the congressional resistance; Jefferson, inside the cabinet, organized the rest, down to hiring the poet Philip Freneau as a State Department clerk so that his *National Gazette* could attack the Treasury the administration's own paper defended.

They called themselves **Republicans**, asserting that the fiscal system and the quasi-monarchical style were one project of consolidation; the administration's men took the name **Federalists**, carried over from the ratification fight. One naming note prevents a standing confusion: these Republicans, later called Democratic-Republicans and eventually Democrats, have no connection to the Republican Party founded in 1854.

<KeyFact>
The first parties formed around a fiscal program, not around abstractions: assumption, the Bank, and the excise sorted Congress by section and interest before any foreign question arrived, and the constitutional argument over implied powers gave each side a theory of what the other was doing wrong.
</KeyFact>

Neither side, at this stage, admitted to being a party. In the received theory a party was a conspiracy against the public good; each side therefore understood itself as the country's defense against the other's faction — Federalists guarding the union against demagogues, Republicans guarding the republic against monarchists.

These were not modern parties in any case: no primaries, no membership rolls, no permanent machinery, only congressional blocs, newspapers, and correspondence networks. The organization came later, and a foreign revolution forced it.

## The French Revolution sorts the halves into parties

A fiscal split among officeholders became a national division when France gave both sides a world-historical stake. The French Revolution of 1789 was at first universally popular in America; after the king's execution and the outbreak of general European war in 1793, it became the sorting question. Republicans read France as the American Revolution continuing; Federalists read the *Terror* — the revolutionary government's campaign of political executions in 1793–94, which sent thousands to the guillotine — as where Republican principles led. When the new French envoy Edmond-Charles Genêt landed in April 1793, he was greeted by crowds, commissioned privateers from American ports against British shipping, and threatened to appeal over Washington's head to the people; Washington proclaimed American neutrality and demanded his recall. Dozens of *Democratic-Republican societies*, political clubs on the French model, organized in his wake, and Washington publicly blamed these "self-created societies" for the whiskey insurrection. The line between government and opposition was becoming a line between two publics.

### Jay's Treaty: the split goes public

The treaty fight of 1795 completed the sorting. John Jay's treaty with Britain secured evacuation of the northwestern forts and preserved peace and trade, but said nothing about impressment and aligned the United States commercially with the power fighting France. The Senate approved it 20 to 10, exactly the two-thirds the Constitution requires with nothing to spare, and when the terms leaked the reaction was the first national mass politics: Jay burned in effigy, Hamilton stoned addressing a New York crowd, and petition campaigns on both sides. In 1796 the House came within three votes, 51 to 48, of refusing the money to implement a ratified treaty. Debate over the treaty, more than any prior event, turned loose factions into two named, disciplined, permanent organizations.

Washington's response was the most famous document of the decade. His Farewell Address, published September 19, 1796 in a Philadelphia newspaper rather than delivered as a speech, warned "in the most solemn manner against the baneful effects of the spirit of party," which "agitates the community with ill-founded jealousies and false alarms, kindles the animosity of one part against another," and is "itself a frightful despotism"; abroad, the country's policy should be "to steer clear of permanent alliances." The advice described a disease the patient already had. The address was issued by the head of what was functionally the Federalist interest, drafted with Hamilton's help, from a government the party fight had already split; its immediate political effect was to announce that the presidency was open. The election of 1796 then produced the constitutional design's first misfire: Adams won with 71 electoral votes, and because the original Constitution gave each elector two undifferentiated votes with the runner-up becoming vice president, the opposition's leader, Jefferson, entered the administration at 68. The government now contained both parties, one of them in the chair of the Senate, waiting.

## The crisis of 1798: opposition becomes a crime

Party competition next met a war scare, and the governing party reached for the criminal law. France, treating the Jay Treaty as a betrayal, turned its privateers on American shipping; Adams sent three envoys to Paris in 1797, where agents of the foreign minister Talleyrand, code-named X, Y, and Z in the published dispatches, demanded a bribe and a loan before negotiation could begin. Publication of the XYZ dispatches in April 1798 detonated the decade's fiercest war fever: "millions for defense," ran the toast of the season, "but not one cent for tribute." An undeclared naval war with France, the Quasi-War, was soon running in the Caribbean, and a new Navy Department was building frigates to fight it.

How completely the parties had stopped regarding each other as legitimate is on the record of the House floor. In January 1798 Representative Matthew Lyon, a Vermont Republican and Irish-born former indentured servant, spat in the face of the Connecticut Federalist Roger Griswold after a taunt about his war record; an expulsion vote failed, and on February 15 Griswold walked to Lyon's desk and beat him about the head with a hickory cane while Lyon defended himself with the fireplace tongs. The anonymous etching *Congressional Pugilists*, printed within weeks and reproduced above, sold the scene as partisan propaganda; its doggerel caption invited readers to enjoy Congress as a cockpit. The first brawl in the House was already a media event, which is itself the evidence: each side's public now consumed the other's disgrace as entertainment.

### Four laws against the opposition

In June and July 1798, the Federalist Congress converted the war scare into statute. Four laws passed in five weeks: a Naturalization Act raising the residency requirement for citizenship from five years to fourteen (immigrants voted Republican); an Alien Friends Act letting the president deport any foreigner he judged dangerous, no trial required; an Alien Enemies Act providing for wartime internment of enemy nationals; and, on July 14, the Sedition Act. The first three targeted foreigners. The fourth targeted the domestic opposition, and it repays reading as a document.

<Example title="the Sedition Act, read as a statute">
**Metadata.** An act of the Fifth Congress, Federalist majorities in both houses, signed July 14, 1798, at the height of the war fever; the full text is two pages of *Statutes at Large*. Given the authorship and the moment, the prediction from provenance: a war-powers measure written to reach the government's domestic critics. Now the text.

**What it forbids.** Section 2 criminalizes "any false, scandalous and malicious writing or writings against the government of the United States, or either house of the Congress of the United States, or the President of the United States," published with intent to defame them or bring them "into contempt or disrepute." Penalty: a fine of up to \$2,000 and up to two years' imprisonment.

**What it protects — and omits.** The protected officers are the government, the Congress, and the President: the offices Federalists held. The Vice President, Thomas Jefferson, is not in the list. Libel the president and the statute reached you; libel his chief rival and it did not.

**What it concedes.** Section 3 makes truth a defense and gives the jury the right "to determine the law and the fact." On paper this was more liberal than the English common law of seditious libel, which admitted no truth defense; the concession did little in practice, since proving the truth of an opinion before a Federalist judge and a hand-picked jury was not an available operation.

**When it dies.** Section 4 expires the act on March 3, 1801 — the last day of the presidential term then running. A permanent security principle does not carry a sunset synchronized to an election; a partisan instrument does. The statute's own calendar is the confession of its purpose.
</Example>

Enforcement matched the design: seventeen verifiable indictments and ten convictions, every defendant a Republican. The showpiece was Matthew Lyon, of the fire tongs, convicted in October 1798 for writing that Adams displayed "an unbounded thirst for ridiculous pomp, foolish adulation, and selfish avarice": four months in an unheated Vermont cell and a \$1,000 fine. He ran for reelection from the cell and won by nearly two to one. Other targets were the Republican press itself, editors including Thomas Cooper, James Callender, and Charles Holt: an attempt, in effect, to shut the opposition's communications down for the election year.

<Remark title="what never happened to the Alien and Sedition Acts">
A modern reader assumes an unconstitutional statute meets its end in court. These did not: no court ever struck down the Alien and Sedition Acts, and Federalist judges enforced the Sedition Act without embarrassment, because judicial review of acts of Congress did not yet exist in practice — <Ref lesson="jeffersonian-america">the next lesson</Ref> takes up its arrival in 1803. The acts were defeated politically instead: the Sedition and Alien Friends Acts expired on their own terms, Jefferson pardoned the convicted and Congress eventually refunded fines, and the electorate delivered its verdict in 1800. The Supreme Court's own accounting came 166 years later, in *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964): the Sedition Act had been found invalid "in the court of history." Before judicial review, that court was the only one available — constitutional limits were enforced, when they were enforced at all, by elections.
</Remark>

### Kentucky, Virginia, and the doctrine that outlived them

The opposition's counterattack came from the state legislatures, and its authorship was a secret of state. Jefferson, the sitting vice president, secretly drafted resolutions that Kentucky's legislature adopted in November 1798; Madison drafted Virginia's, passed in December. Both argued that the Constitution is a compact among states, that the federal government cannot be the final judge of its own powers, and that a state confronting a plainly unconstitutional act may, in Virginia's word, "interpose"; Kentucky's follow-up resolution of 1799 named the remedy "nullification." Two members of the government, writing anonymously because the statute they were attacking criminalized what they were doing, were asserting a state veto on federal law. No other state concurred; ten legislatures answered with rejections.

The resolutions failed at their immediate object and succeeded at something their authors did not intend. As an emergency defense of a free press they changed no policy in 1798. As constitutional theory they entered the permanent arsenal: South Carolina's nullification of the federal tariff in 1832 cited the "principles of '98" expressly, and the secession arguments of 1860–61 stand in the same line of descent, a thread the course takes up with <Ref lesson="sectional-crisis">the sectional crisis</Ref>. Madison spent his last years denying the paternity, insisting interposition had meant protest, not veto. The record of 1798 supports him; the words did not care.

<KeyFact>
The crisis of 1798 put the design's worst case on the table: the governing party criminalized opposition speech with a statute set to expire on March 3, 1801, the last day of the term it protected, and the opposition answered with a state-veto doctrine that would be quoted at every later secession crisis. Both weapons outlived the emergency that forged them.
</KeyFact>

<Aside title="what became of the other three acts">
The Naturalization Act's fourteen-year rule was repealed in 1802. The Alien Friends Act expired in 1800 without a single deportation, though some French nationals left rather than test it. The Alien Enemies Act, the one wartime measure of the four, never expired: it remains in force, and it is the statutory authority that was invoked against British subjects in 1812, against German nationals in 1917, and in the incarceration of Japanese nationals after 1941, a thread the course meets again at <Ref lesson="wwii-home-front">the WWII home front</Ref>.
</Aside>

## 1800: the machine transfers power between enemies

Everything the decade had built now converged on a single election, conducted under rules written for a world without parties. The campaign of 1800 was fought at full venom: a leading Federalist paper warned that a Jefferson victory would make "murder, robbery, rape, adultery, and incest" openly taught and practiced, while a Republican paper described Adams as having "neither the force and firmness of a man, nor the gentleness and sensibility of a woman." Adams's own party split beneath him after he ended the Quasi-War by negotiation in 1800, peace being the one policy the Hamilton wing could not forgive. The Republican ticket, Jefferson for president and Aaron Burr of New York for vice president, out-organized the Federalists in the states that mattered.

Then party discipline broke the Constitution's arithmetic. Every Republican elector dutifully cast his two undifferentiated votes for Jefferson and Burr, and the intended running mates finished tied at 73 electoral votes each, with Adams at 65. The Constitution sends a tie to the House of Representatives, voting by state delegations, nine of the sixteen states required to elect. The House that would choose was the outgoing one, controlled by Federalists, many of whom preferred anyone to Jefferson.

The House balloted for six days, February 11 to 17, 1801, through a snowstorm, members sleeping in committee rooms; the Maryland Republican Joseph Nicholson had his sickbed carried to the Capitol so his vote could hold his delegation even. Thirty-five consecutive ballots returned the identical result: Jefferson eight states, Burr six, two divided, one short of election. Outside the chamber the talk was not academic. Republican leaders let it be known that a Federalist statute handing the presidency to anyone else would be treated as usurpation; the Republican governors of Pennsylvania and Virginia made preparations to have their militias ready. The course has a name for outcomes balanced this finely: the same <Ref lesson="war-of-independence">contingency</Ref> that ran through Trenton ran through the House chamber, with the difference that this time the weapons stayed racked.

Hamilton, Jefferson's oldest enemy in the government, worked the outcome for Jefferson. Burr, he wrote his party, was unprincipled; Jefferson's principles were at least principles, and wrong ones were safer than none. On February 17, on the 36th ballot, the Federalist holdouts chose to stop voting rather than to switch: James Bayard, Delaware's lone representative, filed a blank ballot, South Carolina's Federalists did the same, and the Federalists of Vermont and Maryland absented themselves, letting those delegations fall to Jefferson. Final count: Jefferson ten states, Burr four, two blank. No Federalist ever cast a vote for Jefferson; the transfer was accomplished by abstention, which is what made it politically survivable for the men performing it.

### March 4, 1801

The inauguration was staged as the ceremony section of this lesson in reverse. Jefferson walked from his boardinghouse to the unfinished Capitol in the raw new federal city of Washington, dressed, a local paper noted, as "a plain citizen without any distinctive badge of office"; no sword, no coach. His address offered the losers an olive branch that was also a claim about the system: "every difference of opinion is not a difference of principle. We are all republicans, we are all federalists." Adams had left the President's House before dawn rather than attend, a graceless exit that was also, in its way, the point: the defeated head of state's resistance consisted, in total, of skipping a party.

Margaret Bayard Smith, a Washington resident whose husband's paper printed the address, recorded the day's meaning as contemporaries measured it: "The changes of administration, which in every government and in every age have most generally been epochs of confusion, villainy and bloodshed, in this our happy country take place without any species of distraction, or disorder." That was the correct baseline. Political theory and the entire available record of republics predicted that an armed, organized faction losing executive power would fight; the militias readying in Pennsylvania and Virginia show how close the prediction came to testing. What actually transferred power was a procedure, followed by men who despised each other, to the end.

<KeyFact>
The election of 1800 produced the first peaceful transfer of national power between hostile parties, after a 73–73 tie, thirty-six House ballots, and militia preparations in two states. Contemporary theory expected republics to fail exactly this test; the machine passed it, by abstention, with no shot fired.
</KeyFact>

Two corrections keep the result in focus. First, the crisis was the Constitution's own doing: the tie was manufactured by the elector rule of 1787 meeting parties the framers had not provided for, and the repair was structural — the Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, put presidential and vice-presidential votes on separate ballots so that the deadlock could not recur. Second, Jefferson's own later name for the event, "the revolution of 1800," needs its qualifier: it was, as he said, a revolution "not effected by the sword" but "by the rational and peaceable instrument of reform, the suffrage of the people": a revolution in personnel and in governing principle, while every institution, the debt, the Bank, the courts, the Constitution itself, carried over intact. The Federalists lost the government and kept their property, their presses, and their party.

What is now established: the design of 1787 survived its first decade of operation: a fiscal program that split the government into two parties neither side would admit to founding, a war panic in which the governing party criminalized opposition and the opposition drafted a state-veto doctrine in reply, and a tied election that a hostile House resolved by procedure rather than force, at the price of a constitutional amendment. The transfer of March 1801 joins Trenton and the ratification margins in the course's chain of near-run events. <Ref lesson="jeffersonian-america">The next lesson</Ref> follows the victors: judicial review arriving by strategic retreat in 1803, a continental purchase Jefferson doubted he had the power to make, and a second war with Britain that consolidated the republic it nearly bankrupted.

The XYZ dispatches published (April 1798): the war fever that gave the Federalists their emergency. The Sedition Act signed (July 14, 1798): opposition criminalized, with expiry set to the term's last day. The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (November–December 1798): the interposition doctrine drafted secretly by Jefferson and Madison in reply. The 36th ballot (February 17, 1801): the deadlock broken by Federalist abstention — the first peaceful transfer between hostile parties.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing drill to Washington's Farewell Address, following the worked reading of the Sedition Act. Metadata: published September 19, 1796, in a Philadelphia newspaper, never delivered as a speech; drafted with Hamilton's assistance; issued by a president declining a third term whose administration functioned as the Federalist interest. Before analyzing, predict from the metadata what the document is likely to argue and omit; then state what it records, what it argues, and what its provenance means it can and cannot be used to establish.

<Solution>
Prediction: a retiring executive positioned above party, but drafted inside one party, will condemn party in general terms and will not name his own administration's role in building one. Records: that by 1796 the first president judged parties and foreign attachments the republic's chief dangers, and that he chose print over speech — an address to readers, designed for reprinting, not to Congress. Argues: that the union precedes party ("the baneful effects of the spirit of party"), that party spirit is "itself a frightful despotism," and that policy should "steer clear of permanent alliances." Omits: that the administration issuing the warning was itself a party government; that Hamilton, the Federalist chief, helped write it; and that its publication served a party purpose — opening the succession on Federalist terms and, by its timing, warning against Jefferson. Can establish: elite anxiety about faction, the self-understanding of the founding generation, and the official ideal of nonpartisanship. Cannot establish: that the government of 1796 was actually nonpartisan, or that the warning described anyone's practice, including the author's — for that, the treaty fight and the election that followed are the evidence.

<Exercise>
Two omissions in the Sedition Act's text carry most of its meaning: the Vice President is absent from the protected list, and Section 4 expires the act on March 3, 1801. State precisely what each omission establishes about the statute's purpose, and explain why the truth-defense clause in Section 3 does not rescue the act as a neutral security measure.

<Solution>
The protected list — government, Congress, President — matches exactly the offices the Federalists held in 1798; omitting the Vice President left Jefferson, the opposition's leader, open to every libel the act punished when aimed the other way. A statute protecting officials as such would protect the second officer of the government; a statute protecting a party protects only that party's officers. The sunset date makes the same point in the calendar: a genuine security principle has no natural expiry, while this act died on the last day of the term it shielded, so its authors themselves priced it as an electoral instrument they would not want aimed at them after a loss. Section 3's truth defense was formally more liberal than the common law of seditious libel, but it shifted the burden to the defendant to prove the truth of political characterizations ("ridiculous pomp") before Federalist judges and juries — an impossible standard in practice, as the enforcement record — seventeen indictments, ten convictions, every defendant a Republican — attests. A liberal clause administered by one party against the other is process, not protection.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing drill to the *Congressional Pugilists* etching. Metadata: an anonymous print, Philadelphia, 1798, sold within weeks of the Griswold–Lyon fight, with a mocking verse caption. State what the print records, what it argues, and what it can and cannot be used to establish about the crisis of 1798.

<Solution>
Records: that the February 15, 1798 brawl happened and was public knowledge in detail — Griswold's cane, Lyon's fire tongs, the House sitting — and that a commercial market existed for images ridiculing Congress. Argues: that the people's legislature had become a cockpit; the doggerel invites contempt rather than alarm, and the composition makes both fighters absurd. Can establish: the tone of partisan public culture in 1798 — that the parties' mutual delegitimation had reached the point of physical violence on the House floor, and that printers could profitably sell that fact as entertainment; it is strong evidence of how contemporaries consumed the split. Cannot establish: the fight's precise sequence or fault (an anonymous satirist is not a witness deposition), or which party the printer favored — the mockery lands on both. Its evidentiary value is as an artifact of opinion, not as a record of the event; for the event itself, the House's own investigation records corroborate the essentials.

<Exercise>
Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating causes of the first party system, and defend the ranking in a short paragraph: (a) Hamilton's fiscal program; (b) the French Revolution and the Jay Treaty; (c) the Constitution's silence on parties and its two-vote elector rule.

<Solution>
One defensible ranking. Necessary: (a) — the fiscal program created the durable split itself; the blocs of the 1790s formed on assumption and the Bank before any foreign question arrived, and they sorted members by section and interest, the raw material of permanent parties. Without a substantive division, no party system. Precipitating: (b) — the French Revolution and the treaty fight converted congressional blocs into national organizations with publics, presses, and mass mobilization; the sequence in the seating-chart figure shows the sorting completed on the Jay Treaty vote. It supplied timing and organization, not the underlying cleavage. Enabling: (c) — the Constitution neither forbade nor structured parties, so nothing stopped the formation; and the elector rule turned party discipline into the 1800 tie, escalating the system's first transfer into a constitutional crisis. The silence shaped the form and the crisis, but produced neither the split nor its organization. The ranking is arguable at the margin — one can defend the French Revolution as necessary for mass parties rather than congressional factions — but any defensible answer must put the fiscal program before the foreign crisis in the causal order, because the record puts it first in time and in voting behavior.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
In Federalist No. 10, Madison argued that the extended republic would control faction: a large, diverse country makes it improbable that any single interest can form a durable national majority (<Ref lesson="ratification-and-bill-of-rights">the ratification lesson</Ref>). By 1798 Madison was secretly drafting the Virginia Resolutions as an organizer of a durable national party. Using the extended-republic argument's own terms, explain what the 1790s falsified in it, what survived, and why Madison could regard his party as consistent with his theory.

<Solution>
What was falsified: No. 10's implicit model of faction as a local interest that scale would dilute. The 1790s produced exactly what the essay called improbable: two coalitions spanning every state, stable across issues, organized from the capital outward. The mechanism the essay missed: national institutions themselves. A Treasury program, a foreign-policy choice, and a national press created shared stakes that aggregated local interests instead of leaving them to cancel; the extended sphere contained the faction-forming machine at its center. What survived: the argument that scale forces majorities to be coalitions. The Republican party was not a single interest but a bargained alliance of Southern planters, backcountry farmers, and Northern urban Republicans, which is No. 10's coalition-majority operating under a party label; and no faction achieved the permanent, rights-invading majority the essay feared — power changed hands in 1800. Madison's own reconciliation: he distinguished faction, a group adverse to the rights of others, from a party defending the Constitution against consolidation; on his account the Federalists were the faction and the Republican organization the extended republic's self-defense. The distinction is self-serving, but it is also the reason both sides could build parties while sincerely condemning party: each read No. 10 as a description of the other.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A claim: "The election of 1800 proves the constitutional design worked as intended." Name two facts from this chapter the claim fails to account for, state what would have to be true for it to survive, and write the defensible version.

<Solution>
Two facts. First, the crisis was design-caused: the framers' two-vote elector rule, written for a partyless world, converted Republican discipline into a 73–73 tie between running mates, and the deadlock it produced required a constitutional amendment (the Twelfth, 1804) to repair — a system working as intended does not need immediate structural surgery. Second, the resolution ran outside the design's contemplation: thirty-five deadlocked ballots, militia preparations in Pennsylvania and Virginia, and a final ballot decided by Federalist abstention brokered partly through Hamilton's private campaign against Burr — contingent political choices, not mechanical constitutional operation. For the claim to survive, the record would need to show the framers anticipated party tickets and provided for them, and that the transfer followed the anticipated path; it shows the opposite. Defensible version: the constitutional order survived a failure mode its designers had not foreseen — the machinery channeled a potentially violent succession dispute into a procedure that hostile partisans chose to follow, at the cost of one amendment. That is a weaker claim about the design and a stronger one about the practice: what 1800 established was less that the machine could not jam than that, jammed, it would be repaired rather than broken — the precedent every later transfer stood on.
</Solution>

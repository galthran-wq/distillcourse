Before an English colonist survived a single winter in North America, Spain had governed a continental empire from Mexico City for eighty years, France had built a fur-trade alliance system up the St. Lawrence, and a Dutch company had planted a trading post at the mouth of the Hudson.

The previous lesson established the precondition all three empires worked on: disease had emptied the continent before serious conquest began, and the crossing ran east to west because only one side had oceangoing ships. What stands open is why none of these head starts held — why the continent ended up speaking the language of the empire that arrived last and smallest. This lesson supplies the comparison class the course's argument needs: three colonization models — Spanish *rule*, French *exchange*, Dutch *commerce* — each stated as a system, none of them built to produce a self-reproducing settler society, together with what each left in the later United States and one piece of period propaganda, the Black Legend, read as a source rather than a fact. The closing arithmetic states the result in numbers and leaves the English colonies' compounding population as the anomaly the rest of the module explains.

## The Iberian rehearsal: the system was a century old before it went north

The comparison begins with the model that arrived first, and it arrived finished. Spanish colonization did not improvise its methods in the Americas. Portugal and Castile had rehearsed them on Atlantic islands for seventy years: claim, plant a cash crop, import bound labor, ship the product home under crown license.

Madeira — a volcanic island 600 kilometers off the Moroccan coast, settled by the Portuguese from the 1420s — became Europe's largest sugar producer by 1500, the first rehearsal of Atlantic settlement down to the founding gesture: the settlers christened the first boy and girl born on the island Adam and Eve. São Tomé, a smaller island on the equator in the Gulf of Guinea reached in the 1490s, added the component Madeira lacked at scale: sugar worked by enslaved Africans, the full plantation template. After 1492 the template crossed the ocean intact — first to Hispaniola, the Caribbean island where Columbus planted his base (today's Haiti and Dominican Republic), then to the Brazilian coast. The route is the figure below.

  caption="Where the rehearsal happened: the plantation template moved island to island down the African coast, then crossed whole."

The ocean itself was partitioned before it was mapped. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) split the non-European world between Spain and Portugal along the meridian that <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the Cantino map</Ref> ruled so confidently across unexplored water. European diplomacy then fenced the partition off from European law: by the amity-lines convention of the sixteenth century, peace treaties signed in Europe did not bind ships west of the line. "No peace beyond the line" meant the Caribbean stayed a war zone regardless of what was signed at home.

What the mainland added to the island system was silver. In 1545 prospectors opened Potosí — in effect a mountain of silver ore high in the Andes of present-day Bolivia — and the Zacatecas strikes in northern Mexico followed a year later; together they made Spanish America the source of most of the world's silver for over a century. The *peso de ocho* minted from that silver became the first global trade currency. Silver, not settlement, was the empire's product; the fleet that carried it home was the empire's spine.

<KeyFact>
Spain did not bring an experiment to the Americas; it brought a finished system, rehearsed on Atlantic islands since the 1420s: crown license, cash crop, bound labor, partitioned ocean.
</KeyFact>

## New Spain: a colonization of rule

On the mainland, the rehearsed island system met an asset the islands never had. Recall the two campaigns whose mechanics <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the previous lesson</Ref> took apart: Cortés took the Mexica capital Tenochtitlán in 1519–21, and Pizarro seized the Inca state after 1532. Those conquests left Spain governing something no other European empire in the Americas would have: tens of millions of surviving Indigenous subjects. The Spanish model followed from that fact. It was a colonization of rule: extract tribute and labor from the people already there, convert them, and stratify the result by law.

Three institutions carried it, one at a time. The *encomienda* (from *encomendar*, "to entrust") granted a colonist not land but people: the tribute and labor of a named Indigenous community. Its abuses drove the crown to abolish it in law in 1542; the *repartimiento* labor draft that replaced it reproduced most of them in practice.

The *missions* were the frontier instrument: fortified compounds where friars — mostly Franciscans — gathered converts to live and work under church discipline, the advance guard wherever soldiers were few.

The *sistema de castas* then graded the resulting society by ancestry, in descending legal rank: Spaniards born in Spain; *criollos*, Spaniards born in America; *mestizos*, of mixed Spanish–Indigenous parentage; Indigenous people; and the enslaved. The ranking mattered because the society was hybrid from the start: about 225,000 Spaniards migrated in the whole sixteenth century, most of them single men, into a subject population in the millions — and their capital was built literally on Indigenous foundations, Mexico City standing on the filled ruins of Tenochtitlán.

The parts of New Spain inside the later United States were the empire's far edge, held thinly. St. Augustine, founded in 1565 after Spain expelled France's Huguenot outpost from Florida, is the oldest continuously occupied European-founded city in the continental United States; Sir Francis Drake burned it in 1586 and Spain rebuilt. A chain of Franciscan missions ran west from it among the Timucua, Guale, and Apalachee.

New Mexico began worse. Juan de Oñate led about four hundred colonists north in 1598; when the pueblo of Acoma resisted, his men killed nearly half of its roughly 1,500 inhabitants, and Oñate sentenced the surviving men to the amputation of a foot. Santa Fe followed in 1610 as the first permanent European seat of government in the Southwest. Growth then stalled: by 1680 about 3,000 colonists lived in Spanish New Mexico, while the Puebloan population around them fell from perhaps 60,000 in 1600 to about 17,000, by the epidemic mechanism <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the previous lesson</Ref> counted. Texas and California came later still, as mission chains rather than colonies.

New Spain's model needed a large surviving Indigenous population to rule; where there was none to govern profitably, as on its northern frontier, the model planted missions and garrisons, not settlers.

## The Pueblo Revolt: twelve years without Spain

In New Mexico the mission system met organized resistance with a measurable result. Franciscan friars enforced Catholic practice by force: sacred kivas closed, katsina masks and prayer sticks burned, traditional dances banned. In 1675 the governor arrested dozens of Pueblo religious leaders for "sorcery"; several were hanged and the rest publicly whipped. One of the whipped men, a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh named Popé, spent the next five years building a coalition.

The coordination problem was severe: some two dozen autonomous pueblos, speaking six mutually unintelligible languages, spread over hundreds of kilometers, with no writing. Popé's solution was a deadline carried by hand: runners took knotted cords to each pueblo, one knot to untie per day until the rising. When two runners were captured, the leaders moved the date up, and on August 10, 1680, the pueblos rose together.

<Figure
  src="./figures/pueblo-revolt.svg"
  alt="Timeline from 1675 to 1696: the arrests and whippings of 1675, the knotted-cord coordination and the coordinated rising of August 1680 in which about 400 colonists including 21 of 33 Franciscan friars were killed and some 2,000 survivors retreated to El Paso, twelve years of restored Pueblo independence, the Spanish return under Vargas from 1692, and a branching arrow marking the dispersal of Spanish horse herds toward the Plains nations"
  caption="The Pueblo Revolt, 1675–1696. The branching arrow is the revolt's longest consequence."
/>

The numbers: several thousand Puebloan fighters razed the colony and besieged Santa Fe; about 400 colonists were killed, including 21 of New Mexico's 33 Franciscan friars; some 2,000 survivors were allowed to retreat south to El Paso. "The God of the Christians is dead," Popé declared, and the pueblos washed off their baptisms in the rivers. For twelve years New Mexico had no European government: this was the most successful Indigenous rising against a European empire in North American history. Diego de Vargas began the reconquest in 1692, and the Spain that returned demanded less: the encomienda was not restored in New Mexico, and Franciscan enforcement never regained its pre-1680 reach.

The revolt's longest consequence ran east. The horse herds scattered in 1680 passed by trade and capture to the Plains nations, seeding the mounted societies <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the previous lesson</Ref> flagged and a later lesson meets in arms. Popé's own afterlife is institutional: New Mexico's statue of him ([Po'pay, by Cliff Fragua](https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/art/popay), 2005) stands in the National Statuary Hall of the US Capitol.

<KeyFact>
The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 expelled Spain from New Mexico for twelve years — and its scattered horse herds built the Plains powers the United States would fight two centuries later.
</KeyFact>

## New France: a colonization of exchange

France's model ran on a different asset: not subject populations or silver, but the beaver. Traders founded Port Royal in Acadia in 1603 and Quebec, under Samuel de Champlain, in 1608. The fur trade set the colony's whole shape. Pelts were trapped, processed, and carried by Native hunters; the French bought them at posts with cloth, metal, and guns; profit therefore depended on keeping Native partners willing, skilled, and armed. Champlain accepted the logic immediately: in 1609 he marched with his Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat trading partners against the Mohawk, buying the alliance with his arquebus.

  src="./figures/european-empires-champlain-1609.jpg"
  alt="Copperplate engraving from Champlain's 1613 Voyages: a lone armored European stands between two masses of warriors, firing an arquebus at the chiefs leading the right-hand ranks; allied warriors draw bows at left, arrows cross overhead, beached canoes line the shore in the foreground, and the Mohawk palisade fort stands at upper right"
  caption="The arquebus moment, drawn by the man holding it: Champlain's engraving of the July 1609 battle with the Mohawk, from his Voyages (1613). He is the armored figure in the center."

Exchange shaped everything the Spanish model coerced. Jesuit missionaries lived among Native nations rather than concentrating them into mission compounds, the deliberate opposite of the Franciscan method. French traders married into Native families so routinely that the colony had a word, *métis*, for their children. In the Great Lakes country, French officers learned to give gifts, mediate feuds, and speak in the diplomatic forms Native politics required; the historian Richard White named the resulting negotiated space the *middle ground*. The alliance system, not territory, was the empire: a thin French line of posts embedded in Native nations who did the trapping, much of the fighting, and most of the dying, since the pathogens of <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the Columbian Exchange</Ref> traveled the trade routes with the goods.

What the model did not produce was people. Migration to New France stayed a trickle: the colony was closed to Protestants, the land grew furs better than export crops, and traders needed partners, not tenants. The St. Lawrence settlements held about 3,000 colonists in 1666, about 15,000 in 1700, and about 55,000 by the 1750s; with Acadia, the interior posts, and Louisiana included, French North America still counted under 70,000 people by 1750.

<Aside>
The crown tried to fix the demography directly. After Louis XIV took New France from the trading companies as a royal colony in 1663, the state recruited and shipped roughly 770 marriageable women, the *filles du roi*, between 1663 and 1673, paying dowries from the treasury. The program worked as genealogy (most French Canadians descend from these women) and failed as geopolitics: natural increase from a base of a few thousand could never race a rival base of hundreds of thousands. The model's exceptions ran south: in Louisiana (New Orleans, 1718) and above all in the Caribbean sugar islands, France ran plantation slavery under the Code Noir as brutally as any empire; "France traded, Spain ruled" is a first-order description of North America, not of the French Atlantic.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
New France was an alliance network with a colony attached: under 70,000 French by 1750, embedded among Native nations whose labor and diplomacy were the empire's working capital.
</KeyFact>

## New Netherland: a colonization of commerce

Where the Spanish model ran on subjects and the French on alliance, the third ran on the ledger: the Dutch model was the trading company itself. The Netherlands in 1600 was Europe's most sophisticated commercial economy: the Amsterdam exchange, the joint-stock company, and a post-Reformation regime of practical toleration that made it the refuge of Europe's dissenters, including, for a decade, the English Pilgrims. Henry Hudson, an Englishman under Dutch commission hunting a passage to Asia, charted the Hudson River in 1609; a fur post followed upriver in 1614, and the West India Company (chartered 1621) planted New Amsterdam on Manhattan, which Peter Minuit "purchased" from Munsee people in 1626, a transaction the two parties almost certainly understood in incompatible terms.

New Netherland was run as an *entrepôt*: a port whose business is other people's goods. Furs came down the Hudson from the company's Iroquois trading partners at Beverwijck (Albany), bought with wampum the Dutch acquired on Long Island Sound; the harbor served the company's Caribbean ventures. Settlement was an afterthought that kept failing: the *patroon* land-grant scheme drew few tenants, so the company imported labor it could compel: the first eleven enslaved Africans arrived in 1626, built the town's roads, port, and the wall that named Wall Street, and by 1660 New Amsterdam held [the largest urban enslaved population on the continent](https://www.americanyawp.com/text/02-colliding-cultures/). The commercial ideal of peaceful purchase also failed at the edges: where Dutch farms spread into Munsee land, Kieft's War (1643–1645) was as murderous as any English conflict of the century.

What the model built that lasted was the port's character. The Jesuit Isaac Jogues, visiting in 1643, reported eighteen languages spoken in and around a town of a few hundred; the company, wanting customers rather than communicants, tolerated in practice most of the dissenters its Reformed clergy complained about. When an English fleet took the colony without a shot in 1664, the articles of surrender guaranteed Dutch property, inheritance customs, and worship. The English inherited a working polyglot commercial colony of about 9,000 people, some 2,500 of them in New Amsterdam itself, and, finding it profitable, changed little but the name. <Ref lesson="middle-colonies">A later lesson</Ref> follows that inheritance into New York's pluralism.

<KeyFact>
New Netherland lasted forty years and left a permanent artifact: a commercial, polyglot, tolerant port that England annexed intact in 1664. Manhattan's character predates English rule.
</KeyFact>

The frame explains the legacies. Spain's is biological and cartographic: the horses and cattle now loose on the continent, the mission chains, and the place names from St. Augustine to Santa Fe. France's is diplomatic: an alliance system among the interior nations that would hold the balance of the continent until <Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">the Seven Years' War</Ref>. The Dutch legacy is institutional: Manhattan. What none of the three left is the thing the rest of this module studies: a large, self-reproducing, self-governing settler population. Each model had a structural reason not to: New Spain ruled the labor it needed, New France's business partners were the people already there, and New Netherland's company wanted margins, not migrants.

<KeyFact>
None of the three models produced a settler society of self-governing families — not by failure but by design. The English colonies' population is the anomaly, and the next lesson measures it.
</KeyFact>

## The Black Legend: reading a polemic as a source

The empires competed in print as well as on the ground, and the English and Dutch case for colonizing rested partly on one Spanish book. The era's most consequential text about Spanish America was written by a Spanish bishop to reform his own empire, and became, in other hands, the founding advertisement for Spain's rivals. Reading it is this lesson's exercise in the <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">records / argues / omits</Ref> method.

<Figure
  src="./figures/european-empires-las-casas-portrait.jpg"
  alt="Oil portrait of Bartolomé de las Casas as an old man in the black and white habit of a Dominican friar, a pectoral cross at his chest, seated at a desk and writing on a sheet of paper with a quill."
  caption="The Black Legend has an author: Las Casas at the desk, writing the brief his empire's rivals would reprint for two centuries."
  float="left"
  width="m"
/>

Bartolomé de las Casas reached Hispaniola in 1502, held an encomienda, renounced it in 1514, and spent the following half-century as the empire's most persistent internal critic. His *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias* (the [*Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20321), written in 1542 to move the court, printed at Seville in 1552) is an itemized atrocity brief addressed to the future Philip II. Its opening summary sets the register:

> The *Spaniards* first assaulted the innocent Sheep … like most cruel Tygers, Wolves and Lions hunger-starv'd … that of Three Millions of Persons, which lived in *Hispaniola* itself, there is at present but the inconsiderable remnant of scarce Three Hundred.

The lobbying worked inside Spain: the crown's New Laws of 1542 abolished the encomienda in law (the substitute repartimiento reproduced much of it in practice). Then the text escaped its purpose. Dutch printers translated it from 1578 as ammunition for their revolt against Philip II; a London edition of 1583, *The Spanish Colonie*, appeared as England drifted into war with Spain; in 1598 the Frankfurt publishing house of Theodor de Bry, a Protestant exile who never crossed the Atlantic, issued a Latin edition with seventeen copper plates (engraved by his son Johann Theodor after designs by Jodocus van Winghe) that fixed the book's images in Europe's head; *The Tears of the Indians* (London, 1656) was dedicated to Cromwell as his fleet sailed against Spanish Jamaica; and a 1689 London reprint retitled the book *Popery Truly Display'd in its Bloody Colours*. Each edition kept the atrocities and swapped the argument.

### The method applied: records, argues, omits

**Records.** Eyewitness atrocities by a participant with standing to know, corroborated in kind by the demographic collapse <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the previous lesson</Ref> counted from Spain's own tribute registers. Las Casas's Hispaniola figure of three million is not absurd by modern lights (scholarly estimates for the island's pre-contact population run from under one to as many as eight million), and the near-extinction of the Taíno by mid-century is documented independently of him.

**Argues.** Two different things, by two different publishers. The author argued to his own crown that the encomienda damned Spain and must end: a reform brief, with numbers chosen for maximum force. The Protestant editions argued that Spain was *uniquely* cruel, and therefore that Dutch and English colonization was rescue. That second claim is the *Black Legend*: real atrocities, selectively printed, in service of rival empires.

**Omits.** The reprints omit the text's own provenance: that Spain produced, debated, and legislated on this indictment (no comparable English self-inquiry existed), and that De Bry's images were drawn from prose by an engraver who had never seen America. And the rescue argument omitted its own future: the empires that printed the book as an indictment of Spain went on to run the Mystic massacre, Kieft's War, and the Atlantic slave trade. <Ref lesson="natives-and-english">Later lessons</Ref> let you test the "humane alternative" claim against the English record.

Keep the two findings separate, because the propaganda's failure does not restore Spain's defense: the mortality and coercion are documented facts; the claim of unique cruelty is a print artifact. Debunking the second does not soften the first.

<Aside>
Spain's internal debate went further than legislation. In 1550–1551 the crown suspended new conquests and staged a formal disputation at Valladolid: Las Casas against the humanist Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda, on whether war against the Indians could be just and whether they were, in Aristotle's category, natural slaves. No verdict was published, conquests resumed, and the laws kept being evaded. But the fact of the debate is itself evidence for the records/argues/omits reading above: the empire that the Black Legend painted as uniquely conscienceless was the only sixteenth-century empire that put its own conquest on trial.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The Black Legend is a lesson in sourcing, not a verdict to adopt or reverse: the atrocities Las Casas recorded were real, and the claim that Spain was uniquely cruel was manufactured in Protestant print shops selling colonization as rescue.
</KeyFact>

## The arithmetic that decided the continent

Set the three models against the English colonies on one axis, population, and the outcome of the imperial contest stops being mysterious.

  name="population-race"

<Task>
Check the compounding yourself: the British series runs 50,000 → 250,000 → 1,170,000 over two 50-year steps. Verify that each step is between four and five doublings' worth of growth per century: a doubling time near 22 years.
</Task>

The numbers (British figures from the census bureau's colonial series; French and Spanish figures are estimates with the ranges stated above): in 1750 the British mainland colonies held about 1.17 million people, New France under 70,000, and the Spanish borderlands north of Mexico on the order of ten to twenty thousand. The ratio to New France alone was better than fifteen to one, and the British total was doubling roughly every generation while the others crept.

Benjamin Franklin published the mechanism in 1751, in [*Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind*](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-04-02-0080): cheap land made early marriage affordable, so colonial families were large: "we may here reckon 8 [births to a marriage], of which if one half grow up … our People must at least be doubled every 20 Years." And the projection: "there are suppos'd to be now upwards of One Million English Souls in North-America, (tho' 'tis thought scarce 80,000 have been brought over Sea) … This Million doubling, suppose but once in 25 Years, will in another Century be more than the People of England, and the greatest Number of Englishmen will be on this Side the Water." Both claims held: the doubling rate matched the census for the next century (Malthus later borrowed the estimate), and the United States passed England's population in the 1850s.

The mechanism was structural, which is the module's running argument. Spain's model sent soldiers, friars, and administrators to govern others; France's sent traders to live among partners; the Dutch company sent employees. Only the English model — for reasons the next lesson measures — shipped *families*, and families on cheap emptied land compound. Battlefields would eventually ratify the outcome, but the outcome was being decided in birth registers: North America speaks English because of arithmetic like Franklin's, not because English armies were better than French ones.

<KeyFact>
By 1750 the British colonies outnumbered New France more than fifteen to one and were doubling every 20–25 years by natural increase alone: demography, not battlefield superiority, is why the continent ended up English-speaking. The comparison class is now on the record — rule, exchange, and commerce, none of them built to produce a self-reproducing settler society — and the English colonies' compounding population stands as the anomaly to explain. <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">The next lesson</Ref> takes it up at its source: the selected, literate migrants who wrote self-government into their founding documents.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
Take Las Casas's own Hispaniola figures from the quoted passage: three million people reduced to "scarce Three Hundred." Compute the implied percentage decline. Then recompute using the lowest modern estimate of the island's pre-contact population (about one million) and a mid-century survivor count in the low hundreds. State which conclusion survives the factor-of-several dispute over the starting figure, and compare the logic to the central-Mexico exercise of the previous lesson.

<Solution>
From Las Casas's figures: decline $= (3{,}000{,}000 - 300)/3{,}000{,}000 = 99.99\%$.

From the low modern estimate: $(1{,}000{,}000 - 300)/1{,}000{,}000 = 99.97\%$.

The starting figure is disputed by a factor of eight (scholarly estimates run from under one million to eight million), so the absolute death toll is uncertain by millions. The proportion is not: every reconstruction ends in the near-extinction of the Taíno within about fifty years, so any starting estimate above a few thousand yields a decline above 99%.

This is the same structure as the Cook–Borah exercise in the previous lesson: disputes over pre-contact baselines move the absolute count, not the catastrophic proportion. It is also why Las Casas's rhetorical rounding, whatever it did for his brief's force, does not matter to the demographic finding — the finding rests on the endpoint, which is documented independently of him.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Franklin assumed one million colonists in 1750 doubling every 25 years. Project the population for 1800 and 1850 under his rule, then compare with the census results (5.3 million in 1800; 23.2 million in 1850). State whether the deviations confirm or refute his mechanism, and why.

<Solution>
Doubling every 25 years from $P_0 = 1{,}000{,}000$ in 1750:

1800 (two doublings): $1{,}000{,}000 \times 2^2 = 4{,}000{,}000$.
1850 (four doublings): $1{,}000{,}000 \times 2^4 = 16{,}000{,}000$.

Census: 5.3 million in 1800 and 23.2 million in 1850 — above the projection both times.

The overshoot confirms rather than refutes the mechanism. Franklin's rule priced natural increase only, from his premise that scarcely 80,000 people had ever been "brought over Sea"; the actual population grew by natural increase at roughly his rate *plus* immigration, which accelerated sharply after 1815. A projection undershooting reality because it excluded a known additional inflow is evidence that its core rate was about right; the census growth rate for 1750–1850 works out to a doubling time near 22–24 years, inside Franklin's own 20-to-25-year band.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The British colonial population rose from about 50,000 in 1650 to about 1,170,000 in 1750. Compute the implied doubling time, and check it against Franklin's estimate.

<Solution>
Growth factor over the century: $1{,}170{,}000 / 50{,}000 = 23.4$.

Number of doublings: $\log_2 23.4 = \ln 23.4 / \ln 2 = 3.153 / 0.693 \approx 4.55$.

Doubling time: $100 / 4.55 \approx 22$ years.

Franklin's 1751 band was 20 to 25 years ("doubled every 20 Years" from the birth arithmetic; "suppose but once in 25 Years" in the cautious projection). The measured 22 years sits inside it. Note that this century of growth includes immigration as well as natural increase, so the two are not identical quantities — but at these rates the qualitative conclusion is insensitive to the split.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Classify each case under the three models, and state in one sentence why the first-order model needs an exception clause there: (a) French Louisiana in the 1730s, growing sugar and rice with enslaved African labor under the Code Noir; (b) the Franciscan missions of Spanish Florida; (c) the fur post at Albany after the English took New Netherland in 1664.

<Solution>
(a) By geography, New France; by structure, a colonization of rule and plantation extraction — Louisiana is the standing exception to "France traded," proof that the French model followed the available profit, not a national temperament. Where sugar paid, France ran slavery as hard as any empire.

(b) Cleanly the Spanish model: missions as the frontier instrument of a colonization of rule, converting and concentrating Native populations where there was no silver or tribute base worth garrisoning.

(c) The Dutch model operating under an English flag: the wampum-for-pelts trade with the Iroquois continued at the same post with much of the same personnel, because the English kept the commercial machinery they annexed. Institutional inheritance, not conquest-and-replacement — the pattern the middle-colonies lesson develops.

The general point: the models classify systems of extraction, not nations. Each empire ran a different model wherever its incentives differed.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Apply the records / argues / omits method to Richard Hakluyt's *Discourse of Western Planting* (1584), a confidential memorandum to Elizabeth I arguing for English colonization: it promises the spread of Protestantism, employment for England's idle poor, naval strength, commodities to end dependence on foreign trade, and the deliverance of the Indians from Spanish cruelty. Write one sentence for each of the three questions.

<Solution>
Possible answers.

Records: the state of English colonial ambition in 1584 — what arguments a promoter believed would move the crown (religion, employment, naval power, trade balance, anti-Spanish rescue), and therefore what England's anxieties were on the eve of war with Spain.

Argues: that colonization is simultaneously profitable and humane — specifically, that the Black Legend's Spanish cruelty obliges England to colonize as rescue, which converts an indictment of empire into a license for one.

Omits: any account of what English settlement would require from the people already there — land, above all; any Native voice; and England's contemporary record in Ireland, the actual rehearsal for its colonial methods. The document argues rescue while omitting the mechanism of dispossession, which is exactly the omission the following lessons document at Mystic in 1637 and in King Philip's War.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Pueblo Revolt succeeded where most resistance failed. Identify the specific coordination problem Popé faced, the mechanism that solved it, and two measurable outcomes of the revolt — one immediate, one running into the nineteenth century. Use a concept from the previous lesson for the second outcome.

<Solution>
The problem: a simultaneous rising by about two dozen politically autonomous pueblos, across six mutually unintelligible languages and hundreds of kilometers, with no writing and under surveillance — any pueblo rising alone would be destroyed piecemeal, so the plan's whole value lay in simultaneity.

The mechanism: knotted cords carried by runners, one knot untied per day to a common deadline — a distributed calendar requiring no literacy and no further messaging. When Spanish authorities captured two runners, the leadership advanced the date, and the rising of August 10, 1680 was general anyway.

Immediate outcome: about 400 colonists killed, including 21 of 33 friars; roughly 2,000 survivors expelled to El Paso; no European government in New Mexico for twelve years; and a post-1692 Spanish regime that dropped the encomienda and tolerated more Pueblo practice.

Long-run outcome: the revolt scattered Spanish horse herds into Native trade networks — the Columbian Exchange's livestock flow from the previous lesson, redirected by Native action. Horses spreading north and east after 1680 built the mounted Plains nations, the powers the United States fought into the 1870s.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
State what, concretely, the English inherited when they took New Netherland in 1664, and the mechanism by which it survived the change of flag. Then state why this inheritance matters to a course about English colonial institutions.

<Solution>
The inheritance: a colony of about 9,000 people, roughly 2,500 of them in the entrepôt of New Amsterdam itself — the harbor and its Caribbean trade connections, the Hudson fur corridor and the Iroquois commercial relationship at Albany, a polyglot population (eighteen languages reported in the 1640s), practical religious toleration, Dutch property holdings, and the continent's largest urban enslaved workforce.

The mechanism: the 1664 articles of surrender. Because the colony yielded without a fight, the English guaranteed Dutch property titles, inheritance customs, and freedom of worship; annexation preserved the city's institutions instead of replacing them, and the English governed a going concern they had every commercial reason not to break.

Why it matters: it means one of the thirteen English colonies was commercial, pluralist, and tolerant *before* it was English — New York's character is a Dutch artifact under an English flag. When later lessons treat pluralism and commerce as engines of colonial growth, part of that machinery traces to a forty-year Dutch experiment, not to English design.
</Solution>

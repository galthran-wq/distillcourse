In 1492 two halves of the planet that had developed in isolation for more
than ten thousand years came into permanent contact; every later structure of
American history descends from that collision.

This is the course's first
move, and nothing precedes it: the standing question — how thirteen Atlantic
colonies became the most powerful state in history — opens here with its
precondition. The lesson establishes that precondition in two parts: the
land the colonies would grow on was cheap because contact had
demographically emptied it, and the crossing ran east to west because only
one side had ships. It also installs the method the rest of the course
applies to every document — what a source records, argues, and omits — on
one artifact, a Portuguese world map of 1502.

## The ground floor: what stood on the continent in 1491

For this course's standing question — how a strip of colonies became a
continental power — this section supplies one precondition and one baseline.
The baseline: the Americas in 1491 were not empty. The precondition for
everything after: within about 150 years of contact they largely became so,
and the cheap land of every later chapter — colonial headrights (land
granted per settler transported), the frontier, the Homestead Act of 1862
(free federal land to whoever farmed it) — is that emptying, inherited.

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="s"
  src="./figures/cahokia-vs-london.svg"
  alt="Two bars of equal height, Cahokia about 1100 and London about 1100, each with a whisker marking the 10,000-to-20,000 band where most published estimates cluster"
  caption="Both cities, about 1100: the same order of magnitude."
/>

The baseline, measured once: Cahokia, the Mississippian city on the Illinois
side of the Mississippi River opposite present-day St. Louis (~1050–1350),
peaked between 10,000 and 20,000 people —
contemporaneous London's band — around a 30-meter earthen pyramid holding
roughly 700,000 cubic meters of hand-carried fill. No settlement north of
Mexico would match Cahokia's peak population again until after the American
Revolution (1775–1783). Further south the scale was larger still:
Tenochtitlán, the island capital of the Mexica (the people better known as
Aztecs) in the lake basin where Mexico City now stands, held on the order of
200,000 people when the Spanish expedition under Hernán Cortés reached it in
1519 — larger than any city in Spain.

That is the whole load this section needs to bear: population baselines for
the collapse the lesson will count, and a continent organized enough that
its emptying changed everything. The engineering details are genuinely
interesting and causally marginal — they are behind the fold.

<Aside>
The labor arithmetic, for the curious. Monks Mound holds about 700,000 cubic
meters of earth moved in carrying baskets. At 0.02 cubic meters (roughly 30
kilograms) per load:

$$
\frac{700{,}000 \text{ m}^3}{0.02 \text{ m}^3 \text{ per load}}
= 35{,}000{,}000 \text{ basket loads}.
$$

Read as one campaign: 1,000 carriers at 25 loads a day move 25,000 loads a
day — about four years of continuous work. Read as the sustained program the
excavated construction stages show, the same total spreads over roughly
three centuries at about 47 full-time carriers. Either reading requires a
stored agricultural surplus and an authority that allocates labor — which is
what "city" means when the builders left no writing.

<Figure
  src="./figures/cahokia-plan.svg"
  alt="Plan of Cahokia's central precinct showing Monks Mound at the north end of a 20-hectare plaza, about a dozen smaller mounds, and the palisade enclosing them, with a 500-meter scale bar"
  caption="Cahokia's central precinct: a planned layout, not an accreted village."
/>

Chaco Canyon, a semi-arid canyon in the high desert of present-day New
Mexico, makes the same point in timber and stone: Pueblo Bonito, the largest
of its "great houses," with 650
rooms, some 240,000 construction beams felled 75 kilometers away and carried
by hand, and engineered roads running straight over mesas to about 150
outlying settlements (roughly 850–1150).

"Indigenous America" names a category of convenience, not a nation: the
Mississippian chiefdoms, the Ancestral Puebloans of the Chaco region, the
Haudenosaunee confederacy (the Iroquois league of five nations south of Lake
Ontario), and the Pacific Northwest salmon societies were separated by
thousands of kilometers, dozens of language families, and incompatible
political forms. This course names specific peoples wherever the evidence
supports the name.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Around 1100, Cahokia's population sat in the same band as contemporaneous
London's — and no city north of Mexico reached that size again until after
the American Revolution.
</KeyFact>

## The world that sailed: Europe in 1492

The 1491 baseline is one half of the collision; the other half is the Europe
that crossed, and the direction of the crossing is the first fact this
section has to explain.

### One year, three events

In January 1492 the armies of Castile and Aragon, the two crowns whose union
was becoming Spain, took Granada, the last Muslim kingdom on the Iberian
peninsula, ending seven
centuries of intermittent war there and freeing the
soldiers, the financing machinery, and the crusading self-conception that had
sustained it. In April the Crown signed its contract with Columbus. In August
he sailed — not into an unknown ocean, but into an Atlantic that Iberian
sailors had been working methodically for seventy years.

Portugal had built that Atlantic system deliberately: sugar plantations on
Madeira, 600 km off the Moroccan coast, by the 1450s; the fortified trading
post of Elmina on the West African Gold Coast, in modern Ghana,
in 1482 — drawn in miniature on the map this lesson closes with — and
Bartolomeu Dias's voyage around the Cape of Good Hope, Africa's southern
tip, in 1488.

<Figure
  src="./figures/iberian-atlantic.svg"
  alt="Schematic Atlantic locator: Lisbon on the Iberian peninsula, Madeira offshore, the Cape Verde islands off Africa's westernmost point, Elmina on the Gold Coast, and the Cape of Good Hope at Africa's southern tip, joined by an amber Portuguese route down the coast; a dashed blue arrow from Madeira's latitude to the Caribbean marks Columbus's 1492 crossing"
  caption="The Atlantic the Iberians had learned by 1492: Portugal's stations ran south along the African coast; Columbus turned the same system west."
/>

The working tools were
the *caravel*, a small ship rigged to sail close to the wind, and the *volta
do mar*, the learned maneuver of swinging wide into the open ocean to catch
the returning westerlies. Ocean crossing was a solved problem in Lisbon,
Portugal's Atlantic port capital, before
Columbus proposed pointing it west.

### News at print speed

The movable-type printing press, which Johannes Gutenberg had introduced in
the German city of Mainz around 1450, was four decades old in 1492.
Columbus's report of his
first voyage was printed within weeks of his return in 1493 and ran through
more than a dozen editions across Europe within five years. The contrast with
the previous crossing is the point: when Norse voyagers reached Newfoundland, a
large island off North America's northeastern coast,
around the year 1000, the information stayed in sagas for centuries; when
Columbus returned, the information became a printed commodity that every port
in Europe could act on.

### Disjoint toolkits

Set the two inventories side by side, and the comparison refuses to become a
ranking.

<Figure
  src="./figures/disjoint-toolkits.svg"
  alt="Two inventory columns: the Americas held maize, potato, chinampa agriculture, cities of ten to twenty thousand, and monumental earthworks, but no iron, wheels in use, draft animals, or ocean ships; Europe held ocean-going ships, iron and steel, gunpowder, wheels and draft animals, and the printing press, but neither maize nor potato. The ships entry is highlighted, with an arrow crossing to the Americas labeled 1492"
  caption="Two inventories that barely intersect. The highlighted line is the one that set the direction of contact."
/>

Europe held ocean-going ships, iron and steel, gunpowder, wheeled transport,
draft animals, and the press; none of these existed in the Americas. The
Americas held maize — a grain returning several times more food per seed
planted than Old World wheat — the potato, the *chinampa* wetland agriculture
that fed Tenochtitlán, and the cities and earthworks the previous section
measured, built without any of Europe's mechanical inventory. Neither list
contains the other.

Direction followed from one line of the inventory. The side with ships
crossed; the side without was crossed to. Everything else in this lesson —
the exchange, the collapse, the conquests — happened on American ground
because the Atlantic could be crossed from east to west, and re-crossed to
report it.

<Remark title="why the inventories differ">
The standard explanation is geographic rather than biological. Eurasia's
east–west axis let crops, animals, and techniques travel along constant
latitudes, and the continent held most of the world's domesticable large
animals; the Americas run north–south through climate barriers and held
almost none (Crosby 1986, Diamond 1997). The hypothesis is contested in its
details; this lesson needs only its weak form — the inventories differed for
reasons of geography and species, not of capacity.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The toolkits of 1492 were disjoint, not ranked: Europe held the ships, the
iron, and the press; the Americas held maize and cities of European scale
built without them. The ocean-going ship is the single line that decided who
crossed to whom.
</KeyFact>

## The Columbian Exchange: reciprocal transfer, unequal outcome

The ships settled who crossed to whom; this section takes up what the
crossing moved — a two-way biological transfer between hemispheres that had
been separated for ten thousand years.

### The cargo of 1493

Columbus's second voyage sailed in September 1493 with seventeen ships and
about 1,200 men, and with horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, wheat, chickpeas,
melons, and sugarcane. It carried, unrecorded, the pathogens its passengers
were incubating. Return cargoes carried maize and other American cultivars
east.

Alfred Crosby named the whole two-way movement the *Columbian Exchange* in
1972: the transfer of people, crops, animals, and diseases between hemispheres
after 1492. The term is biological and agricultural. It is not a synonym for
cultural mixing.

<Figure
  src="./figures/exchange-ledger.svg"
  alt="Two arrows between Afro-Eurasia and the Americas: a westbound arrow carrying smallpox, measles, typhus, influenza, livestock, wheat and sugarcane, and an eastbound arrow carrying maize, potato, cassava, tomato, cacao and tobacco, each labeled with its outcome"
  caption="The ledger: the transfer runs both ways, the consequences do not."
/>

### The eastbound flow raised carrying capacity

American cultivars were not luxuries in the Old World; they were caloric
staples that outperformed local crops on marginal land. The potato yields more
calories per hectare than wheat or rye on cold northern soils, cassava
tolerates drought and poor soil in west and central Africa, and maize took over
dry uplands in southern China.

The size of that effect has been measured. [Nunn and Qian (2011)](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/126/2/593/1868756)
compared regions by their suitability for potato cultivation before and after
its introduction and attribute about one quarter of Old World population
growth and urbanization between 1700 and 1900 to the potato alone.

### The westbound flow emptied the land, then refilled it

Westbound the ledger is dominated by pathogens; the next section counts what
they did. The second entry is livestock. Cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses had
no American analogues at comparable density; released onto grasslands that
the epidemics were emptying of their farmers, they multiplied and reshaped
the vegetation.

Horses in particular were re-appropriated. Animals dispersed from Spanish New
Mexico, above all after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, when the Pueblo peoples
drove the colonists from the upper Rio Grande valley for twelve years and
the abandoned herds scattered; the horses reached the nations of the Great
Plains, the continent's grassland interior,
and produced the mounted bison-hunting societies that the United States would
later fight, a story taken up in
<Ref lesson="conquering-the-west">the lesson on the conquest of the West</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
The exchange was reciprocal in direction and radically unequal in effect: the
eastbound flow raised how many people Europe, Africa, and Asia could feed,
while the westbound flow removed nine in ten of the people already in the
Americas.
</KeyFact>

## The emptying: the precondition, counted once

The previous section stated the westbound flow's effect as a ratio — nine in
ten; this section establishes where that number comes from. Spanish colonial administration taxed Indigenous communities by household and
recorded the counts. Those tribute registers are the closest thing the
sixteenth century offers to a census, and
[Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/essays-in-population-history-vol-iii-mexico-and-california.html)
reconstructed central Mexico's population from them: 25.2 million in 1519,
2.65 million in 1568. For the hemisphere,
[Koch and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.quascirev.2018.12.004)
pool the regional estimates to about 60.5 million people in 1492, of whom
roughly 90 percent died within the following century.

<Figure
  src="./figures/collapse-4.svg"
  alt="Bar chart of central Mexico's Indigenous population falling from 25.2 million in 1519 to 16.8 million in 1532, 6.3 million in 1548, and 2.65 million in 1568, with an 89 percent decline bracket and a dashed mark carrying the contested starting estimate"
  caption="Central Mexico, 1519–1568, from the tribute registers. Stated once; the rest of the course builds on what it left."
/>

The mechanism fits in three sentences. The major Afro-Eurasian pathogens had
never circulated in the Americas, so no one carried acquired immunity; a
disease that in Europe struck mostly children struck every age at once,
removing in one wave the people who plant, harvest, and govern; and waves
arriving every few years prevented recovery between them. Colonial labor
drafts and forced resettlement raised mortality further and blocked recovery
— the machinery of that is <Ref lesson="european-empires">the next
lesson's</Ref> subject.

What matters for this course is what the emptying left: a continent's
agricultural land available to newcomers at token prices. Virginia's
headrights, the squatter's clearing, the frontier line, and the Homestead Act
are all downstream of the fact established in this section — stated here
once, and referenced from now on rather than retold.

<KeyFact>
The percentage is far better constrained than the absolute count: sources
disagree about how many people lived in the Americas in 1492 by a factor of
two, and agree that roughly nine in ten of them were gone within a century.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
The demography, for the curious: the full series, the dispute, and an
independent check from ice cores.

The interval detail is worth stepping through:

<FigureSequence
  name="collapse"
  alt="Four-frame bar chart of central Mexico's Indigenous population falling from 25.2 million in 1519 to 16.8 million in 1532, 6.3 million in 1548, and 2.65 million in 1568, ending with an 89 percent decline bracket"
  caption="The three intervals decline by about 33, 63, and 58 percent; the steepest brackets the epidemic of 1545–48 that Nahuatl-language records call cocoliztli, 'pestilence.'"
/>

Cook and Borah's 1519 figure is the high end of a long dispute: Sanders and
colleagues, working from archaeological settlement survey rather than tribute
lists, put the contact population at roughly half of it, and Ángel Rosenblat
argued for 4.5 million. The disagreement moves the starting height, not the
direction or the steepness — every reconstruction, minimalist or maximalist,
shows the same collapse over the same five decades. Three estimation methods
dominate, and they disagree because each rests on a different unmeasured
constant: back-projection with an assumed depopulation ratio (Dobyns, the
high figures), tribute and mission registers (Cook and Borah), and settlement
survey counting house floors (Sanders, Denevan).

Koch and colleagues added a check from outside history entirely. If tens of
millions of farmers stopped farming, their fields reverted to forest and
scrub, and the regrowth pulled carbon out of the atmosphere: their land-use
reconstruction gives 55.8 million hectares abandoned and a drop of about 3.5
parts per million in atmospheric carbon dioxide — visible in Antarctic ice
cores, dated to the late 1500s. The ice-core record documents the collapse
independently of every colonial count.
</Aside>

## Conquest followed collapse rather than causing it

A collapse of that scale invites the inference that conquest caused it; the
chronology runs the other way, and the two best-documented campaigns show
the actual mechanism.

### Two campaigns, two small armies

Hernán Cortés landed on the Mexican coast in 1519 with roughly 500 men and
16 horses. Tenochtitlán, the Mexica capital he marched toward, held on the
order of 200,000 people, larger than any city in Spain.

Francisco Pizarro seized the Inca ruler Atahualpa at Cajamarca, a highland
town in what is now northern Peru, on 16 November
1532 with 168 men. The Inca state ran from present-day Ecuador to Chile and
fielded armies of tens of thousands.

<Figure
  src="./figures/conquest-mechanisms.svg"
  alt="Causal diagram with three inputs to Spanish victory: thick arrows from epidemic mortality and Indigenous allies, and a deliberately thin arrow from the Spanish force of about 500 and 168 men, all feeding the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 and Cuzco in 1533"
  caption="Arrow width is causal weight: the small army is the smallest of the three inputs."
/>

### What actually carried the two campaigns

Smallpox reached central Mexico in 1520, arriving during the fighting with
the expedition Cuba's governor had sent under Pánfilo de Narváez to arrest
Cortés. It killed the ruler Cuitláhuac and a large share of
Tenochtitlán's defenders and food producers during the months before the final
siege, which ended on 13 August 1521.

In the Andes, the mountain chain the Inca state ran along, the sequence ran
earlier and cut deeper. Smallpox travelled ahead
of any European, reaching the Inca heartland between about 1524 and 1528. It
killed the ruler Huayna Capac and his designated heir, and the succession war
between Atahualpa and Huáscar that followed was still unresolved when Pizarro
landed. The state he confronted was one faction of an ongoing civil war, not a
unified empire.

The second mechanism is alliance. Cortés reached Tenochtitlán at the head of
tens of thousands of Indigenous troops, above all from Tlaxcala, an
unconquered state east of the capital with its own
reasons to fight the Mexica; Pizarro recruited from the losing faction of the
Inca succession war. Both campaigns were, in troop numbers, overwhelmingly
Indigenous.

<Remark title="the myth and the two mechanisms">
The claim that a few hundred Europeans defeated empires by superior arms does
not survive contact with the numbers. Steel and horses gave a tactical edge in
open-field engagements and no strategic one: 168 men cannot garrison the Andes.
The explanation that fits the evidence has two parts, epidemic mortality and
Indigenous alliance, and the Spanish force is the third and smallest input.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Conquest did not cause the collapse; it followed and exploited it.
</KeyFact>

## Reading a source: the Cantino planisphere of 1502

Every claim in this lesson rests on a source produced by an interested party
— tribute registers, conquerors' letters, a rival crown's chart; the course
therefore needs a standing method for reading such artifacts, and this
section installs it on one map.

### The artifact

<Figure
  src="./figures/cantino-planisphere-1502.jpg"
  alt="The Cantino planisphere of 1502: a Portuguese world chart on parchment. Africa is drawn as a complete continent with dense red place-names along its western coast and the castle of Elmina picked out in miniature; the Americas appear only as ragged unfinished coastal fragments at the left edge; a single straight vertical line runs the full height of the Atlantic, labelled as the marker between Castile and Portugal; compass roses and rhumb lines cover the sheet."
  caption="Portuguese world chart, c. 1502. Africa is mapped coast by coast; the Americas are ragged edge fragments; the straight vertical line down the Atlantic is the Tordesillas partition."
/>

The map above is a Portuguese world chart of about 1502, drawn on six glued
parchment sheets 220 by 105 centimeters. It is named for Alberto Cantino, the
agent who bought it in Lisbon for the Duke of Ferrara, in northern Italy,
and moved it out of
Portugal; it has been in the
[Biblioteca Estense in nearby Modena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantino_planisphere)
ever since. It is the earliest surviving map to show the line agreed at
Tordesillas in 1494, when Spain and Portugal, by treaty signed in that
Castilian town, divided all future overseas claims between them along a
mid-Atlantic meridian.

A primary source is an artifact made inside the period under study. Reading one
means separating three different things it can tell you, which is the method
this course applies to every document, photograph, map, and cartoon that
follows.

<Figure
  src="./figures/source-reading.svg"
  alt="Three boxes labeled what it records, what it argues, and what it omits, each with the corresponding reading of the Cantino planisphere"
  caption="The standing method: records, argues, omits. Applied here to the Cantino map."
/>

### What it records, argues, and omits

**Records.** The African coastline is drawn in detail from the Cape Verde
islands, off Africa's westernmost point,
around the Cape of Good Hope to the Indian Ocean, with named landings.
That is the accumulated product of eight decades of Portuguese voyaging, and it
is drawn with confidence because it had been sailed.

**Argues.** A single straight vertical line runs down the Atlantic: the
demarcation agreed by Spain and Portugal at Tordesillas in 1494, 370 leagues
west of the Cape Verde islands. It divides territory neither party had
surveyed, and it is ruled with more precision than any coast on the sheet. A
legal claim is being presented in the visual language of measurement.

**Omits.** The Americas appear as disconnected fragments: a Caribbean island
group; a stretch of Brazilian coast, recorded in 1500 when Pedro Álvares
Cabral's Lisbon-to-India fleet, swinging wide into the Atlantic, struck
South America; and a northern
landmass from the voyages the Corte-Real brothers, Portuguese captains, made
toward Newfoundland in 1500–1502. There is no interior, no Pacific, and no
continuous coastline. A decade after 1492, Europeans had charted edges and knew
nothing behind them.

<Task>
Look at the map again and locate one thing the mapmaker drew firmly that he
could not have measured, and one thing he left blank that people were living
in at the time. The Tordesillas meridian and the interior of South America are
the intended answers; the point of the exercise is that the two are on the same
sheet.
</Task>

<Aside>
Where the documents are. The National Archives'
[Milestone Documents](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/list)
collection, which supplies primary sources for most of this course, begins in
1776. For the period in this lesson there is no American documentary series at
all. The evidence base is instead archaeological (Cahokia's construction
stages, Chaco's tree-ring-dated timbers), administrative (Spanish tribute
registers, written by the colonizing state for revenue purposes), cartographic
(charts like Cantino's), and narrative accounts written by conquerors and
missionaries. Every category has an interest, and the interests differ, which
is why claims in this lesson are stated with the method that produced them
attached.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
What a source omits is evidence: the blank interior of the Cantino map
documents the limits of European knowledge in 1502 as precisely as its
coastlines document European voyages. The lesson's contribution to the
course's argument is now on the record: contact emptied the continent, the
emptied land was cheap to newcomers, and the crossing ran east to west.
<Ref lesson="european-empires">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the three
rival European powers that colonized that ground — and why none of their
models produced a self-reproducing settler population.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Chaco's great houses consumed roughly 240,000 construction timbers, cut more
than 75 kilometers away and carried by hand. Assume a crew of 16 people per
beam and a ten-day round trip, and that construction ran from about 850 to
1150. Estimate the sustained labor force the timber haul alone required, and
state which assumption your answer is most sensitive to.

<Solution>
Person-days per beam: $16 \times 10 = 160$.

Total: $240{,}000 \times 160 = 38{,}400{,}000$ person-days.

Spread over 300 years: $38{,}400{,}000 / 300 = 128{,}000$ person-days per year.
At 365 days that is $128{,}000 / 365 \approx 350$ people working full time on
timber alone, for three centuries, in a semi-arid canyon that grows no timber.

The answer scales linearly with the crew size and with the trip length, so it
is most sensitive to the crew assumption: a crew of 8 halves the estimate to
about 175 people, and a crew of 30 raises it to about 660. No plausible
assumption brings it near zero, which is the conclusion that survives: Chaco
required a permanent organized labor force drawing on a region far larger than
the canyon.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Using the Cook and Borah series (25.2 million in 1519, 2.65 million in 1568),
compute the total decline as a percentage and the implied number of deaths.
Then repeat both computations under the low estimate for 1519 of 11 million,
keeping the 1568 figure. State which of the two quantities the dispute over the
starting figure actually threatens.

<Solution>
High series: decline $= (25.2 - 2.65)/25.2 = 22.55/25.2 = 89.5\%$; implied
deaths $= 22.55$ million.

Low series: decline $= (11 - 2.65)/11 = 8.35/11 = 75.9\%$; implied deaths
$= 8.35$ million.

The absolute death toll changes by a factor of $22.55/8.35 \approx 2.7$. The
percentage changes from about 90 percent to about 76 percent, and both remain
catastrophic: under either reconstruction, at least three quarters of the
population of central Mexico was gone in 49 years.

The dispute therefore threatens the absolute number far more than the
proportion, which is why the claim this lesson states as established is a
proportion. Note also that the two figures are not independent: a scholar
arguing for 11 million in 1519 generally also revises the later counts, so the
honest statement of the low position is "at least three quarters", not a
precise 75.9 percent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Columbian Exchange moved crops in both directions, yet its demographic
outcomes ran opposite ways. Explain in three or four sentences why a
reciprocal transfer produced an asymmetric result, naming the specific
mechanism on each side.

<Solution>
The two directions carried different kinds of cargo. Eastbound the significant
items were high-yield staple cultivars: the potato and maize deliver more
calories per hectare than existing Old World staples on cold or dry soils, so
they raised the population that a fixed area of farmland could support, and
Nunn and Qian attribute roughly a quarter of Old World population growth
between 1700 and 1900 to the potato alone.

Westbound the significant items were pathogens against which American
populations had no acquired immunity, because smallpox, measles, influenza,
and typhus had never circulated in the hemisphere. Crops raise a ceiling
slowly, over generations; epidemics remove a population within years. The
transfer was symmetric in direction and asymmetric in kind, and it is the kind
that determines the outcome.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Someone claims that the fall of Tenochtitlán in 1521 demonstrates European
military superiority. State two specific pieces of evidence from this lesson
that the claim fails to account for, and state what would have to be true of
the evidence for the claim to survive.

<Solution>
Two pieces of evidence. First, force ratios: Cortés commanded roughly 500
Spaniards against a city of about 200,000, and he took it at the head of tens
of thousands of Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous troops. In numbers the besieging
army was overwhelmingly Indigenous, so the outcome cannot be attributed to the
Spanish contingent alone. Second, timing: the smallpox epidemic of 1520 struck
Tenochtitlán, killing the ruler Cuitláhuac and a large fraction of the
defenders, in the months between the Spanish expulsion from the city and the
final siege. The defenders in 1521 were not the defenders of 1519.

For the superiority claim to survive, one would need evidence that Spanish arms
produced decisive results against undiseased forces without Indigenous allies.
The Andean case cuts the same way rather than the other: Pizarro's 168 men
entered a state already halved by an epidemic that had preceded them by several
years and split by a succession war, and they won by recruiting one side of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the three-question method to a different artifact: Hernán Cortés's
*Cartas de relación*, the five letters he wrote to Emperor Charles V between
1519 and 1526 describing the conquest of Mexico. State, in one sentence each,
what the letters record, what they argue, and what their silences reveal.

<Solution>
Records: an eyewitness itinerary of the campaign, with descriptions of
Tenochtitlán's markets, causeways, and scale that no other surviving European
source provides, plus named Indigenous polities and their alignments.

Argues: that Cortés was a loyal servant of the crown rather than a mutineer who
had defied the governor of Cuba who sent him, and that the territory he seized
was worth confirming in his hands. The letters are a legal brief for his own
authority, written to the one person who could ratify or destroy it, which
predicts inflation of his difficulties and of the enemy's strength.

Silences: the letters credit victory to Spanish arms and providence and are
correspondingly thin on the scale of the Tlaxcalan contribution and on the 1520
epidemic. A document arguing for the value of Cortés's own service has no use
for the two mechanisms that best explain the outcome.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Koch and colleagues estimate 55.8 million hectares of farmland reverted to
forest and scrub after the collapse, and a hemispheric death toll of about 54
million (60.5 million in 1492 falling to roughly 6 million by 1600). Compute
the implied cultivated area per person, and state what the figure would have to
look like for the land-use estimate to be implausible.

<Solution>
$$
\frac{55.8 \times 10^6 \text{ ha}}{54 \times 10^6 \text{ people}}
\approx 1.03 \text{ ha per person}.
$$

Roughly one hectare of cultivated or recently cultivated land per person. That
is a plausible figure for swidden maize agriculture, where a household farms a
plot for a few years and lets a larger area lie fallow in rotation, so the land
attributable to each person exceeds the area planted in any single year.

The estimate would be implausible if the arithmetic returned a value far
outside the range that pre-industrial agriculture can occupy: 0.05 hectares per
person would imply intensive irrigated farming everywhere in the Americas,
which the archaeology contradicts, and 10 hectares per person would imply that
nearly the entire habitable surface of two continents was under cultivation. A
result near 1 hectare is consistent with what is known independently about
American farming systems, which is why the carbon-cycle check is treated as
corroboration of the population estimate rather than as an independent
curiosity.

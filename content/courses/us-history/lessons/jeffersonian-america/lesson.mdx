Between 1801 and 1815 the United States doubled its territory, acquired a doctrine of judicial review, and fought Britain to a draw that Americans remembered as a victory.

The course's argument stands here: the design of 1787 survived its first decade of operation, and <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">the previous lesson</Ref> closed with the machine's hardest test passed — the tied election of 1800 resolved by procedure, the first peaceful transfer of national power between hostile parties. Two stress tests remained untried: the courts, which had never yet enforced a constitutional limit against Congress, and war, which had broken every earlier confederation that fought one. This lesson runs both, plus the acquisition no branch had planned for. It establishes, in order: how *Marbury v. Madison* made the third branch's check operational by strategic retreat; how the Louisiana Purchase doubled the republic's land base at about three cents an acre, and whose land it actually was; how the embargo tested commercial coercion to destruction; how a Native confederacy made the Ohio Valley the war's second cause; and how a war that settled nothing on paper consolidated the republic that survived it.

## Marbury v. Madison: the check arrives by strategic retreat

The first test grew directly out of the transfer of 1800. Between the election and inauguration day, the defeated Federalists moved to hold the one branch the voters could not reach: the Judiciary Act of February 1801 created sixteen new federal circuit judgeships, a separate act created forty-two justices of the peace for the new District of Columbia, and John Adams filled every seat with Federalists, signing commissions into his final hours in office. He had already given the party its longest-lived appointment: John Marshall, his secretary of state, confirmed as Chief Justice of the United States in January 1801. Marshall held both offices at once through the transition, and in the last-night rush his State Department clerks failed to deliver a stack of the justice-of-the-peace commissions — signed, sealed, and lying on a desk when Jefferson's people walked in.

Jefferson ordered the undelivered commissions withheld. One of the appointees, a Georgetown Federalist named William Marbury, sued in the Supreme Court in December 1801, asking for a *writ of mandamus* (a court order compelling an official to perform a legal duty) directed at the new secretary of state, James Madison. The Court ordered Madison to show cause why the writ should not issue. The Republican Congress answered institutionally: it repealed the 1801 Judiciary Act, and it abolished the Court's 1802 term outright, so that the justices did not sit for fourteen months. When the case was finally argued in February 1803, the administration did not even send a lawyer.

### The trap, and the inverted opinion

Marshall's position looked like a forced loss. Order the commission delivered, and Jefferson would ignore the order, exposing the Court as a committee with no army, at a moment when Republicans were already discussing impeachment as a tool for clearing Federalist judges. Refuse to order it, and the Court conceded that a cabinet officer could violate a legal right with impunity. Either exit subordinated the judiciary to the executive.

The opinion Marshall delivered on February 24, 1803 escaped through a third exit, and its structure is the strategy. A court ordinarily decides jurisdiction first, because without jurisdiction it has no business saying anything else. Marshall reversed the order: he decided the merits first and reached jurisdiction last, which let him put Jefferson's administration in the wrong on the record before holding that the Court could do nothing about it.

<Task>
Step through the four frames, and at each one ask what Jefferson could have defied. Links 1 and 2 declare his administration in the wrong but order nothing; links 3 and 4 order nothing either: they refuse the writ. Confirm that the finished opinion contains no command an executive could disobey; that absence is the design.
</Task>

The fourth link is the one that outlived the case. Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 had been read to let the Court issue mandamus as an original matter; Article III's list of original-jurisdiction cases does not include suits like Marbury's; a statute cannot add to what the Constitution fixes. Faced with a conflict between statute and Constitution, Marshall wrote, "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is," and "a law repugnant to the constitution is void." The Court struck down an act of Congress for the first time, in the course of announcing that it was powerless to help the plaintiff. Marbury never got his commission. Jefferson won the case and complained about it for the rest of his life, protesting that the merits discussion was a lecture the Court had no jurisdiction to deliver.

<Remark title="what Marbury did not do">
Marshall invented neither the doctrine nor its logic. State courts had refused effect to statutes before (Varnum's fiduciary argument in Trevett v. Weeden, 1786, is worked through in <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the Roman blueprint lesson</Ref>), and [Federalist No. 78](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed78.asp) had derived the power in print in 1788: judges enforcing the people's Constitution against the people's agents (the starred <Ref lesson="federalist-papers">Federalist Papers chapter</Ref> reconstructs the syllogism). Nor did the case establish routine judicial supremacy. The Supreme Court did not void another act of Congress for fifty-four years: the second instance is *Dred Scott* (1857), a case <Ref lesson="sectional-crisis">the sectional crisis lesson</Ref> meets in a far darker role. What Marbury established was narrower and durable: the power existed, exercised once, on the record, in a form its opponents could not overturn because it had ordered nothing.
</Remark>

### The impeachment test fixes the boundary

The check survived its counterattack immediately afterward. Republicans removed district judge John Pickering by impeachment in March 1804 (he was insane and drunk on the bench, a plausible "high crimes" case), and then went for Justice Samuel Chase, a genuinely partisan Federalist who had campaigned from the bench, on charges that amounted to judging while obnoxious. The Senate, though Republican by a wide margin, acquitted Chase on every article on March 1, 1805. The acquittal drew the line that has held since: impeachment removes judges for crimes, not for politics. Between them, Marbury and the Chase acquittal completed the machine <Ref lesson="constitutional-convention">the convention</Ref> had drawn on paper: a third branch armed with a check and secure enough in tenure to use it.

<KeyFact>
Marbury v. Madison (1803) established judicial review by strategic retreat: Marshall declared Jefferson's administration in the wrong, voided the statute that would have let the Court act, and issued no order anyone could defy. The power then lay unused against Congress for fifty-four years, until Dred Scott (1857).
</KeyFact>

## The Louisiana Purchase: the republic doubles at three cents an acre

The second consolidation arrived in the same season as Marbury, and no branch of the machine had planned it. The trigger was geography. Every farm west of the Appalachians moved its produce by river, and every river ran to the Mississippi and out through New Orleans, the port at the river's mouth; whoever owned that city held the western economy at discretion. The owner had been Spain: weak, accommodating, and bound by treaty to let American goods pass. In 1800 Spain secretly retroceded the Louisiana territory to Napoleon's France, and in October 1802 the Spanish administrator still running New Orleans suspended the Americans' right of deposit. Westerners talked of taking the city by force; Jefferson, who wanted no war and no alliance, sent instructions to Paris to buy New Orleans and West Florida, authorizing up to about \$10 million.

Napoleon's answer was shaped in the Caribbean, not in Paris. The army he had sent to reconquer Saint-Domingue (the French sugar colony whose enslaved population had risen in 1791 and was winning its war for what would become Haiti) was destroyed by the revolution and by yellow fever. Without the sugar island, a mainland granary had no imperial point; with war against Britain about to resume, an unfortified inland empire was a liability he could not defend.

In April 1803 his ministers offered the American negotiators, Robert Livingston and James Monroe, not the city but the whole territory: about 828,000 square miles, for \$15 million. That works out to roughly three cents an acre. The [treaty](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/louis1.asp), dated April 30, 1803, doubled the area of the United States, and the men who signed it had authorization for none of it.

### The strict constructionist buys an empire

From the party of enumerated powers one would expect the purchase to fail on principle: the Constitution nowhere mentions acquiring foreign territory, and Jefferson's Republicans had built their identity on refusing implied powers smaller than this: the Bank fight of 1791, argued in <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">the previous lesson</Ref>, was over less. Jefferson agreed. He drafted a constitutional amendment to authorize the acquisition retroactively, and privately conceded the point in plain words: the executive, he wrote to Senator John Breckinridge in August 1803, had "done an act beyond the Constitution," and must "throw himself on the justice of his country."

His cabinet talked him out of the amendment (Napoleon was showing signs of regret, and a ratification delay might unmake the deal), and the Senate approved the treaty 24 to 7 in October 1803. The administration that existed to police strict construction executed the broadest implied power yet claimed, and its constituents ratified the choice at every subsequent election. The lesson generalizes: parchment limits bind when some empowered actor has a motive to enforce them, and against 828,000 square miles at three cents an acre, no such actor existed.

Jefferson's justification was not a concession to Hamiltonianism but a doctrine of his own, with a name he had used since 1780: the **empire of liberty**. Expansion, in this theory, was not conquest but multiplication: each new tract of cheap land meant more independent citizen-farmers, and independent farmers were the republic's load-bearing material. Where European empires acquired subjects, the American empire would replicate citizens. The theory made expansion and republicanism mutually reinforcing, which is why a party suspicious of federal power could pursue continental growth with a clear conscience.

### Whose land it was

The theory's silence was its foundation. What France sold in 1803 was not possession of the Mississippi valley but a *claim*: the exclusive right, as against Britain and Spain, to acquire the land from the people living on it. The territory was inhabited and governed by Native nations: the Osage, Quapaw, and Caddo along the lower rivers; the Pawnee, Kansa, and Omaha on the plains; the Mandan towns of the upper Missouri; the Lakota and Comanche, expanding powers in their own right. Tens of thousands of people lived under those governments; none were party to the treaty, and most had never heard of it.

The purchase transferred a European legal fiction; converting the fiction into ownership would take three-quarters of a century of treaties, purchases, and wars, and the course follows that process through <Ref lesson="jacksonian-democracy">removal</Ref> and beyond.

Jefferson had begun converting it before the ink was dry; in fact he had begun before the purchase existed. In a [confidential message to Congress](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/jeffersons-secret-message-to-congress) on January 18, 1803, while Louisiana was still French, he requested \$2,500 for an expedition up the Missouri "for the purpose of extending the external commerce of the United States": a commercial cover story for a military reconnaissance of another power's territory. The party that resulted, under Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, left St. Louis in 1804, wintered with the Mandan, crossed to the Pacific, and returned in 1806 with maps, specimens, and diplomatic contacts: the empire of liberty's first survey of what it had bought the right to take.

<KeyFact>
The Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled the United States: 828,000 square miles for \$15 million, about three cents an acre. What was bought was France's claim against other empires, not clear title: the land itself was held by Native nations who were not party to the sale.
</KeyFact>

<Aside title="the Burr conspiracy: the treason clause holds">
The purchase's vast, loosely attached West produced the era's strangest episode. Aaron Burr, vice president until 1805 and a murderer in New Jersey's eyes after killing Hamilton in their 1804 duel, spent 1805–06 recruiting westerners for a project whose exact object he kept fluid: detaching the western states, invading Spanish Mexico, or both, depending on the listener. Betrayed by his partner General James Wilkinson (himself a paid Spanish agent), Burr was tried for treason at Richmond in 1807, with Marshall presiding on circuit and Jefferson publicly campaigning for conviction. Marshall held the prosecution to the letter of Article III: treason is levying war against the United States, provable only by two witnesses to the same overt act. No such act could be proved, and Burr walked. The clause had been written narrowly in 1787 precisely so that treason could not become what it was in England, a weapon against political enemies; its first great test, against a president's personal enemy, held the line.
</Aside>

## The embargo: commercial coercion tested to destruction

The purchase secured the republic's land base; its trade still moved on an ocean ruled by other people's navies. The Napoleonic wars had made neutral America the world's carrier (export values roughly quintupled between 1793 and 1807), and then the belligerents closed in on the carrier. Britain and France together seized about nine hundred American ships between 1807 and 1812 for trading with the other side.

Britain added a grievance France could not match: *impressment*, the boarding of American vessels to seize sailors as British subjects. British doctrine held allegiance perpetual (a subject could not naturalize out of it), and the Royal Navy, bleeding manpower, took men on that theory by the thousands: some six thousand American sailors between 1803 and 1812.

In June 1807 the practice reached an act of war. The British frigate *Leopard* stopped the American warship *Chesapeake* just off the Virginia coast, demanded four alleged deserters, and when the American captain refused, fired broadsides into her: three dead, eighteen wounded, the four men taken. The country's response was the fiercest war fever since 1798. Jefferson could have had a declaration of war for the asking; he asked instead for an experiment.

### Peaceable coercion, measured

The Embargo Act, signed December 22, 1807, closed American ports to all foreign trade. The theory, which Jefferson called *peaceable coercion*, held that Europe needed American food and cotton more than America needed European goods, so withholding trade entirely would force the belligerents to respect neutral rights without a shot. The theory was tested at full scale for fifteen months, and the chart records the result.

Exports fell from \$108 million in 1807 to \$22 million in 1808, down nearly 80 percent in a single year. Ships rotted at their wharves, sailors and dockworkers went unemployed by the tens of thousands, and farm prices fell across the South and West; New England, whose economy was the carrying trade, was hit hardest and radicalized fastest.

Europe, meanwhile, barely registered the pressure: Britain opened new markets in Spanish America, and Napoleon cheerfully confiscated American ships in port on the reasoning that, since the embargo forbade them to be there, they must be British smugglers.

### Enforcement: the small-government party at full coercion

The domestic enforcement problem completed the irony. Trade did not stop: it went overland into Canada, and the administration escalated for fifteen months to stop it.

  src="./figures/embargo-enforcement.svg"

A Republican government elected against standing armies, internal taxes, and federal coercion produced the opposite of its premise: an Enforcement Act (January 1809) authorizing seizure of suspicious cargoes without warrant, revenue officers backed by the army and navy, and in April 1808 a presidential proclamation declaring the Lake Champlain region, on the New York–Vermont border, in a state of insurrection. Jefferson's party was operating machinery indistinguishable from what it had organized to abolish.

Congress repealed the embargo on March 1, 1809, three days before Jefferson left office. Its replacement, narrower non-intercourse laws against Britain and France, leaked as badly and coerced as little.

<KeyFact>
The embargo cut US exports by nearly 80 percent in one year (\$108M to \$22M) without changing British or French policy; enforcing it drove a small-government administration to warrantless seizures and a declared insurrection zone. The failure of commercial coercion left war as the remaining instrument.
</KeyFact>

## The confederacy on the Wabash: the war's second cause

The maritime chain of seizure, impressment, and failed embargo was one road to 1812. The other ran through the interior, where the United States pressed on Native land faster than any treaty line could contain. The ground at issue, the Ohio Valley and the country north of it, had been opened to settlement by the Treaty of Greenville (1795), imposed the year after the Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794) broke the Northwestern Confederacy that had destroyed St. Clair's army on the upper Wabash in 1791.

In the opened country, federal and territorial officials extracted cession after cession from individual nations; the Treaty of Fort Wayne (1809) alone took about three million acres from signatories who did not live on most of it. The pattern, buying from whoever would sign against the interest of everyone who would not, produced the resistance it assumed away.

### The Prophet and Tecumseh: revival into program

Two Shawnee brothers organized the resistance. Tenskwatawa, called the Prophet, preached a religious revitalization in the tradition of Neolin, the Delaware prophet whose teaching had fed <Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">Pontiac's War in 1763</Ref>: the Master of Life required Native peoples to shed dependence on American goods, alcohol, and land sales and return to their own ways.

Tecumseh, his brother, converted the revival into a political and military program: a confederacy of all Native nations, holding the remaining land in common, on the principle that no single nation could sell what belonged to all. From their town of Prophetstown, at the junction of the Tippecanoe and Wabash rivers in present-day Indiana, Tecumseh traveled from Canada to Georgia recruiting, while British agents in Canada, hedging against the next American war, supplied the confederacy with goods and arms.

### Tippecanoe, November 1811: the strike that fused the roads

Indiana's territorial governor, William Henry Harrison, chose not to wait for the confederacy to finish forming. In November 1811, while Tecumseh was recruiting in the South, Harrison marched on Prophetstown; Tenskwatawa attacked his camp before dawn on November 7, and after a sharp fight, the **Battle of Tippecanoe**, the Americans held the field and burned the town. The battle wounded the movement without destroying it, and it welded the two roads to war into one: survivors moved toward open alliance with Britain, and westerners read every future frontier fight as British instigation. When Congress took up war the following June, the frontier and the ocean appeared in the same indictment.

<KeyFact>
From the Native side, the War of 1812's western theater was not a sideshow of a maritime quarrel. It was the war itself: the last realistic contest for the Ohio Valley, fought by a pan-Indian confederacy that allied with Britain because the United States was the power taking the land.
</KeyFact>

## The War of 1812: four theaters, three of them mismanaged

The two chains converged in Madison's [war message](https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/june-1-1812-special-message-congress-foreign-policy-crisis-war) of June 1, 1812, which indicted Britain on exactly the counts this lesson has assembled: impressment, the blockade system strangling neutral trade, and "the warfare just renewed by the savages" on the frontier, with Britain blamed for Tippecanoe's aftermath.

Congress obliged, but barely: 79 to 49 in the House, 19 to 13 in the Senate, the closest vote on a formal declaration of war in American history, with New England and the commercial Northeast voting no. Madison signed on June 18. In one of the era's signature ironies, London had already announced suspension of the offending trade orders two days earlier; the news, weeks out on the Atlantic, arrived after the declaration. The war began, as it would end, ahead of its own information.

The republic entered the fight with the military its governing theory had built: an army kept deliberately small and officered by Revolutionary veterans past their prime, a navy of a dozen-odd seagoing warships against the largest fleet on earth, and a treasury whose national bank Congress had let expire in 1811. What followed sorted into four theaters, and coherence into none of them.

### The border and the sea, 1812–13

The war's advertised objective, an easy conquest of Canada, collapsed first. General William Hull invaded from Detroit in July 1812, retreated without a battle, and in August surrendered Detroit itself, his army included, to a smaller British and Native force under Isaac Brock and Tecumseh. Two further thrusts, at Niagara and toward Montreal, died at the border, in part because New England and New York militia declined to leave their states.

At sea the script inverted: the frigate *Constitution* shattered HMS *Guerriere* in thirty-five minutes in August 1812 ("her sides are made of iron," a sailor shouted, naming her Old Ironsides), and American single-ship victories multiplied through the winter. They were morale-saving, strategically marginal, and mortal when the odds returned: the *Chesapeake*, taken by HMS *Shannon* in June 1813, gave the navy its epitaph-slogan in Captain James Lawrence's dying "Don't give up the ship." By 1814 the Royal Navy's blockade had sealed the coast; the export line in the embargo chart above touches \$7 million that year, the blockade accomplishing by force what the embargo had done voluntarily.

### The Thames and Horseshoe Bend: the interior decided

The Northwest was decided on fresh water. Oliver Hazard Perry's improvised squadron took Lake Erie in September 1813, cutting British supply; the British and the confederacy retreated east, and Harrison caught them at the Thames in Upper Canada on October 5. Tecumseh died in the battle, telling the British commander beforehand, by the account that survives, "we are determined to defend our lands, and if it is his will, we wish to leave our bones upon them." The confederacy did not survive him.

In the South the same war reached the Creek nation as civil war: the Red Sticks, the militant Creek faction aligned with Tecumseh's movement, fought accommodationist Creeks and then the United States; Andrew Jackson broke them at Horseshoe Bend, a fortified bend of the Tallapoosa River in present-day Alabama, in March 1814, and the Treaty of Fort Jackson that August took about twenty-two million acres, from Creek enemies and Creek allies alike. The cession opened the future cotton frontier, a consequence <Ref lesson="cotton-kingdom">the Cotton Kingdom lesson</Ref> will price.

### 1814: the empire turns around

Napoleon's abdication in April 1814 released veteran British regiments for America, and the war's character changed from frontier stalemate to invasion. In August a British force landed in the Chesapeake, scattered the militia defending the approaches at Bladensburg, and marched into Washington on August 24. That night the public buildings burned: the Capitol, the President's House, the navy yard. Madison and the government were fugitives on Virginia roads; Dolley Madison had waited in the President's House long enough to have the full-length Stuart portrait of Washington taken down and carried off: the same composition as the Lansdowne canvas that opened <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">the previous lesson</Ref>, saved from the fire that consumed the room it hung in.

The burning was the war's nadir and nearly its turning point in the same month. The same British force moved on Baltimore in September and failed: Fort McHenry, guarding the harbor, rode out a day and night of naval bombardment, and the fleet withdrew. That was the night a Washington lawyer named Francis Scott Key, watching from a truce ship, drafted the verses that became the national anthem.

  caption="Three weeks reverse the campaign: the capital burned, then both British offensives stopped within four days of each other."

On September 11 at Plattsburgh, on Lake Champlain, Thomas Macdonough's squadron destroyed the British flotilla and turned back the main invasion from Canada. Militarily the war had stabilized into what it would remain: a draw neither side could afford to continue. The American treasury was effectively empty by autumn (the government could not reliably service its loans), and Britain, at war for two decades, was negotiating the future of Europe at Vienna and wanted the American sideshow closed.

New England wanted it closed on different terms. The region's Federalists, their commerce destroyed by embargo, non-intercourse, and blockade in succession, met in secret at Hartford from December 15, 1814 to January 5, 1815. What the convention actually proposed was constitutional amendment, not secession: abolish the three-fifths clause that inflated Southern power (<Ref lesson="constitutional-convention">the convention lesson</Ref> priced that bargain), require two-thirds majorities for declarations of war, new states, and commercial restrictions, and limit presidents to one term. What the country believed it had proposed mattered more, and the difference between the two is the next section's story.

## Ghent and New Orleans: peace before the battle

The war ended twice, once on paper in Flanders and once in fact on the Mississippi, and in the wrong order. Diplomats had been meeting since August 1814 at Ghent, a city in the Flemish Low Countries, where the British opened by demanding a permanent Native buffer state in the Northwest and border cessions. American resistance, the news from Plattsburgh and Baltimore, and London's exhaustion stripped the demands away, and on December 24, 1814 the parties signed a [treaty](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/ghent.asp) that is best read against the document that started the war.

<Example title="the Treaty of Ghent, read against the war message">
**Metadata.** A peace treaty signed at Ghent, December 24, 1814, by British and American commissions, after four months of negotiation conducted while the war's 1814 campaigns ran. The prediction from provenance: a treaty ending a stalemated war between exhausted parties will restore the situation before the war and decide as little as possible. Now the comparison.

**What the war message demanded (June 1, 1812).** Madison's indictment had three counts: impressment of American sailors; the blockade-and-orders system against neutral trade; British involvement in the frontier war.

**What the treaty says.** Article I restores all conquered territory and the prewar boundaries: *status quo ante bellum*. Later articles exchange prisoners and refer the disputed stretches of the Canadian boundary to joint commissions. Article IX obliges both parties to restore to Native nations "all the possessions, rights, and privileges" they held in 1811, a clause the United States never enforced and Britain never returned to; the buffer-state demand is gone. Article X condemns the slave trade in a sentence that binds no one.

**What the treaty omits.** Impressment: unmentioned. Neutral rights and the orders: unmentioned. Every stated cause of the war is absent from the instrument that ended it, partly because, with Napoleon beaten, the practices had lapsed of themselves.

**What the comparison establishes.** On paper the war changed nothing: the United States achieved none of its declared aims, lost nothing it held in 1811, and signed a document that reads like a mutual agreement to stop. Whatever the war produced, the treaty is not where to look for it.
</Example>

British commanders in the Gulf did not yet know the war was over (could not know, at sailing-ship speeds), and the campaign against New Orleans proceeded. Andrew Jackson, fresh from the Creek War, entrenched his improvised force of regulars, militia, free Black volunteer battalions, Choctaw fighters, and Baratarian pirates behind a rampart on a dry canal below the city, flank anchored on the river, flank on a swamp.

On January 8, 1815, fifteen days into the peace, the British army assaulted the rampart frontally across open stubble fields. In about half an hour the attack was destroyed: roughly 2,000 British casualties, including the commanding general, Edward Pakenham, dead on the field, against about 70 on the American side. It was the most lopsided major battle of the war, and it decided nothing that Ghent had not already decided.

### The sequence of the news

What the battle did decide was how the war would be remembered, and it did that through the mail. The treaty needed 49 days to cross the winter Atlantic; the news from New Orleans needed four weeks to ride up the coast. The result, plotted below, is that Americans learned of the victory first (Washington had the news by February 4) and of the peace second (the treaty reached New York on February 11; the Senate ratified it 35–0 on February 16, and ratifications were exchanged the next day). Read in that order, the war ended as triumph: a stunning victory, then an honorable peace. The paper record shows something else, a stalemate already concluded before the famous battle was fought.

<Task>
Read the timeline twice: once left to right as events (peace, battle, ratification), once in the order the news reached Americans (battle won, then peace signed). Verify from the dates that the second ordering, the one every American experienced, inverts the first. The inversion is the mechanism of the section's last claim.
</Task>

The inversion had immediate casualties. The Hartford Convention's emissaries reached Washington in mid-February to present their amendments to a capital celebrating New Orleans and Ghent simultaneously. Proposals drafted against a losing war arrived in a country convinced it had won one; the convention, its actual moderate text notwithstanding, was fixed in public memory as a secession plot in wartime, and it killed its authors' party. The Federalists, the party of Washington's administration and of the Constitution's ratification campaign, contested one more presidential election, losing in 1816, and dissolved.

### A draw remembered as a victory

The record supports one claim firmly: the War of 1812 was a draw that resolved none of its stated causes, and the treaty text above is the proof. The popular account, that the war was a "second war of independence" which the United States won with New Orleans as the clinching battle, does not survive the documents: a war won does not end in *status quo ante*, and a clinching battle is not fought after the treaty.

But rejecting the account does not require pretending the belief did nothing. The belief was itself a historical force, and its production is traceable: the news arriving victory-first, the anthem written at Fort McHenry, the humiliations of 1812 overwritten by the last battle's arithmetic. Albert Gallatin, the treasury secretary who had financed the war and helped negotiate the peace, made the sober version of the claim in 1816: the war had made the people "more Americans; they feel and act more as a nation." The measurable sequels (a second national bank chartered in 1811's ashes, tariff and internal-improvement programs pushed by the war generation, a Republican party governing with Federalist instruments) belong to the next lessons. The qualified verdict stands: nothing won on paper, and a self-conception won permanently.

<KeyFact>
The Treaty of Ghent (December 24, 1814) restored the status quo ante and left impressment and neutral rights unmentioned; yet because news of New Orleans (January 8, 1815) reached Americans before news of the peace, the draw entered national memory as a victory, and the memory did the political work the treaty did not.
</KeyFact>

What is now established: the constitutional machine's remaining stress tests are passed. Judicial review is operational: asserted in 1803, sheathed for half a century, secured by the Chase acquittal. The republic's land base is doubled at three cents an acre, by a purchase that bought a claim against empires rather than title against nations. And the founding era closes with the institutions of 1787 having survived party division, a hostile transfer, and a war that burned the capital, while the Native confederacy that contested the interior is broken and its lands opened. One fact from this lesson names the next constraint: a peace treaty that needed 49 days to cross the Atlantic, and a battle fought two weeks into a peace nobody present had heard of. Distance was still the republic's governing condition. <Ref lesson="market-revolution">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the machinery that attacked it: the canals, steamboats, presses, and eventually the telegraph that turned the continental land base this lesson acquired into one market.

<Exercise>
Apply Marshall's four-step chain to a new case. Congress passes a statute providing that contractors with claims against the Post Office may sue the Postmaster General directly in the Supreme Court, as an original matter. A contractor holds a signed, completed federal contract; the Postmaster General refuses to pay; the contractor files in the Supreme Court under the statute. Work through the four links as the Marbury opinion orders them, and state the outcome. Then state one respect in which a modern court would structure the analysis differently, and why Marshall did not.

<Solution>
Link 1, the right: the contract is complete and signed, so the contractor holds a vested legal right to payment; the refusal violates it. Link 2, the remedy: paying an acknowledged contractual debt is a ministerial duty, not a discretionary political act, so the law affords a remedy against the officer. Link 3, the jurisdiction: Article III fixes the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction: cases affecting ambassadors, public ministers and consuls, and cases where a state is a party. A contractor's suit against a cabinet officer is neither. Link 4, the statute falls: the act purporting to add this suit to the Court's original jurisdiction contradicts Article III; a law repugnant to the Constitution is void; the case is dismissed for want of jurisdiction, whatever the merits. Outcome: the contractor is declared wronged and sent elsewhere (a lower court), and the statute is dead. The modern difference: a court today decides jurisdiction first and, finding none, says nothing about the merits; discussing them is dictum from a court that has just held it cannot speak. Marshall inverted the order deliberately: deciding the merits first let him put the administration in the wrong on the record while the jurisdictional holding guaranteed there would be no order to defy.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing drill to Jefferson's confidential message to Congress of January 18, 1803 — the request for \$2,500 to send an exploring party up the Missouri "for the purpose of extending the external commerce of the United States." Metadata: a sitting president to Congress; marked confidential and kept from publication; sent while the entire Missouri country belonged to France (the purchase was three months away and unforeseen); the request framed throughout as commercial and as Indian-trade policy. Predict from the metadata what the document will and will not say; then state what it records, what it argues, and what it can and cannot be used to establish.

<Solution>
Prediction: a confidential message about entering another power's territory will supply an innocent public rationale and omit the strategic one; expect commerce and science in the text and empire in the silence. Records: that the executive planned, priced, and requested funding for a transcontinental reconnaissance before the United States owned any of the ground (the expedition was not a consequence of the purchase but preceded it), and that Jefferson considered the true rationale unfit for open proceedings: the secrecy is itself data. Argues: that the project is a modest commercial measure ("extending the external commerce"), constitutionally comfortable and cheap at \$2,500. Omits: that the party would be a military unit mapping the rivers, passes, and nations of foreign territory; any mention of sovereignty, settlement, or the strategic value of the Pacific approach. Can establish: premeditated executive interest in the trans-Mississippi West before the purchase, and the administration's judgment that the interest needed a cover story, strong evidence for reading the later "empire of liberty" as a program rather than an improvisation. Cannot establish: what the expedition actually did or found (that evidence is in the journals, 1804–06), or that Jefferson foresaw the purchase itself; the message shows appetite, not foreknowledge.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Hartford Convention's report (January 1815) proposed constitutional amendments: abolishing the three-fifths clause, two-thirds congressional majorities for declarations of war, admission of new states, and commercial restrictions, and a one-term presidency. What the public came to believe was that New England had plotted secession in wartime. Explain (a) what in the convention's own conduct made the belief plausible, (b) why the timing of the report's arrival in Washington converted a moderate document into a political death sentence, and (c) what the episode establishes about the difference between what a document says and what it can be used to establish.

<Solution>
(a) The convention met in secret, kept no public record of its debates, was called by the section that had obstructed the war (militia refusals, blockade-era trade with the enemy), and convened while the war was going badly; and some New England radicals outside the convention had discussed separation openly, so secrecy invited the worst available reading. The moderates' control of the outcome was invisible; the secrecy was not. (b) The emissaries reached Washington in mid-February 1815, simultaneously with the news of New Orleans and the Treaty of Ghent. Demands drafted as leverage against a failing war arrived in a capital that believed the war triumphantly won; leverage became, overnight, evidence of disloyalty at the moment of national vindication. The same text, arriving in November 1814, would have read as hard bargaining. (c) The report's text establishes what the convention formally proposed: amendments, not secession. It cannot by itself establish what the delegates wanted, feared, or would have done had the war continued, and it could not control what contemporaries believed: a document's political meaning is fixed jointly by its text and by the information environment it lands in. The Federalist Party was killed less by what the report said than by when it arrived.
</Solution>

Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating causes of the June 1812 declaration of war, and defend the ranking in a short paragraph: (a) impressment and the seizure of American ships; (b) the land conflict in the Ohio Valley and the British–confederacy connection; (c) the Republican supermajority in the Twelfth Congress and the War Hawk leadership.

<Exercise>
In <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">the previous lesson</Ref>, no court ever ruled on the Alien and Sedition Acts: they were defeated by expiry, pardon, and the election of 1800, and constitutional limits were enforced, when at all, by elections. This lesson establishes judicial review in 1803. Explain why Marbury changed nothing, for half a century, about how statutes like the Sedition Act would meet their end — and what, on the record of 1798–1805, actually protected constitutional limits in the early republic. Use at least three specific facts from the two lessons.

<Solution>
Marbury established the power's existence, not its use: the Court did not void another act of Congress until Dred Scott in 1857, so for fifty-four years a Sedition-Act-style statute faced no realistic judicial threat; and the Sedition Act itself had been enforced without embarrassment by federal judges, showing the bench of that generation was not waiting for permission to strike partisan laws it approved of. Marshall's design compounded the dormancy: the power was asserted precisely in a case where using it required no confrontation, and the Court avoided tests it could lose. It was a check calibrated to survive, not to govern. What actually protected limits, on the record: the electorate (the transfer of 1800 retired the party of the Sedition Act, and Jefferson pardoned its convicts); statutory sunsets (the act expired March 3, 1801 by its own terms); and the impeachment boundary fixed by the Chase acquittal of 1805, which protected judicial independence without making judges active guardians. The early republic's working constitution was enforced by elections and political settlements, with judicial review held in reserve: a reserve first drawn on, catastrophically, in 1857.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
A claim: "The Louisiana Purchase proves that constitutional limits bind only until they become inconvenient." Name two facts from this chapter the claim fails to account for, state what would have to be true for the claim to survive, and write the defensible version.

<Solution>
Two facts. First, the scruple was real and on the record: Jefferson drafted a constitutional amendment to authorize the acquisition, and privately admitted the act was "beyond the Constitution": the limit was acknowledged, argued over inside the administration, and overridden openly, not ignored; and the override was then ratified through the Constitution's own procedures (Senate approval 24–7, House appropriation, and elections that endorsed the result). Second, a colorable constitutional argument existed: most Republicans held that the treaty power and the power to admit new states covered acquisition, so the episode is partly a dispute between two readings, not a naked breach; Jefferson's own strict reading was the minority position even in his party. For the claim to survive, the record would need to show concealment or the bypassing of ratifying institutions; it shows the opposite. Defensible version: the purchase shows that a written limit is enforced only when some empowered actor has both standing and motive to enforce it. When a measure benefits every section, every party, and every institution that could object, no such actor exists, so the limit yields. That is a claim about enforcement structure rather than cynicism, and it is the same lesson Marbury teaches from the other side: constitutional limits are real exactly where a motivated enforcer is.
</Solution>

Between 1815 and 1848 canals, steamboats, and railroads cut the cost of moving goods by an order of magnitude and pulled ordinary households out of subsistence and into markets.

The course's argument stands at the start of its eighth move. The constitutional machine survived its stress tests, and <Ref lesson="jeffersonian-america">the previous lesson</Ref> closed on the constraint that survived with it: a peace treaty that needed 49 days to cross the Atlantic, and a battle fought two weeks into a peace nobody present had heard of. Distance was still the republic's governing condition, and the continental land base bought in 1803 was economically inert so long as nothing heavy could afford to cross it. This lesson establishes, in order: what distance cost in 1815 and why that price sealed the interior; how the Erie Canal knocked an order of magnitude off it and what the canal's own freight ledgers show being built; how information broke free of freight entirely, from the post office to the 1844 telegraph; what the new market did to households, to the young women who staffed the first factories, and to the credit system that financed all of it, twice to destruction. <Ref lesson="jacksonian-democracy">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the mass politics this market society produced.

## Distance priced: the world of 1815

The before-picture is quantitative. A Senate committee reported in 1816 that \$9 would move a ton of goods across the entire Atlantic Ocean, or exactly 30 miles overland: the ocean and 30 miles of American road cost the same. At the wagon rate that figure implies, roughly 30 cents to move a ton one mile, bulk produce could not travel. A ton of wheat was worth about \$33; hauling it 150 miles cost more than the wheat. Farmers beyond a river or a day's ride from a port therefore grew food to eat it, made cloth to wear it, and traded surpluses locally, in kind, in account books kept with neighbors. The interior's one outlet was the river system that drains to New Orleans, which is why the city mattered enough to fight for in 1815, and why the battle's news took weeks to travel.

Historians call what replaced this world the **market revolution**, a name fixed by Charles Sellers's *The Market Revolution: Jacksonian America, 1815–1846* (1991). The term is a historian's periodization, not a phrase contemporaries used, and its two halves are both contested. Sellers read the change as capitalism imposed on a resisting agrarian majority; Daniel Walker Howe, whose *What Hath God Wrought* covers the same years, rejects the metaphor, arguing that most Americans wanted market access and that the era's real engine was a *communications* revolution: cheap print, the postal network, and finally the telegraph overcoming what he calls the tyranny of distance. This lesson uses the standard name and carries both theses to the evidence.

<Remark title="markets were not new — the scale and the share were">
The claim to reject at the outset: that Americans before 1815 lived outside markets and that factories then pulled them in. Colonial farmers had sold tobacco, wheat, and salt fish across the Atlantic for two centuries; <Ref lesson="colonial-societies">the colonial societies lesson</Ref> measured free colonists among the best-paid ordinary people in the world, which only trade can produce. What changed after 1815 was scale, speed, and the share of a household's output that was sold rather than eaten. Nor did the change arrive mainly through factories: *commercialization* (selling what you grow, buying what you use) reached most American households a generation before *industrialization* (working in one) reached any but a few. The market revolution was, for the typical family, a change in what the farm did, not where anyone worked.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
In 1816, \$9 moved a ton of goods across the Atlantic or 30 miles inland: at that price, grain consumed its own value in freight within about 150 miles, and the interior was priced out of every distant market.
</KeyFact>

## The Erie Canal: an order of magnitude off the price

The number above is the problem the canal attacked. Between 1817 and 1825 the state of New York dug a channel 363 miles long from the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo, through the one low gap in the Appalachian chain. It cost \$7.1 million of state money, the largest engineering work yet attempted in North America, and it was finished on October 26, 1825. The economics were immediate: shipping costs on the corridor fell about 90 percent against wagon carriage, and the barrel of flour that had cost \$3 to move from Rochester to Albany now moved for 75 cents. Tolls repaid the construction debt by 1837.

<Example title="pricing the canal">
**The rate.** A barrel of flour holds 196 pounds, just under a tenth of a ton, and Rochester lies about 230 canal miles from Albany. The before-price of \$3 works out to $3 / (0.098 \times 230) \approx 13$ cents per ton-mile by road; the after-price of 75 cents works out to about 3 cents per ton-mile by canal. Canal rates kept falling as tolls were cut and boats grew, toward a cent; wagon rates had nowhere to fall, because horses eat.

**What the rate decides.** Wheat sold for about \$1 a bushel, 60 pounds, so a ton of wheat (about 33 bushels) was worth about \$33. At the 1816 wagon rate of 30 cents per ton-mile, moving that ton 150 miles cost \$45: freight 136 percent of value, so the crop stayed home. At 2 cents on the canal, the same 150 miles cost \$3: freight 9 percent of value, so the crop became cargo. The calculation is the historical argument: a price change of one order of magnitude converted the interior's produce from unsaleable to saleable, and every other change in this lesson follows from it.

**The uncertainty.** Rates varied by year, direction, and cargo, and the surviving quotations mix tolls, freight, and insurance. None of that matters to the conclusion, which needs only the order of magnitude: 30 cents against 1–3.
</Example>

### The ledger: a hinterland becoming a market

The canal's own records show what the price change built. New York's canal officials counted the freight arriving at Hudson River tidewater each year and recorded where it came from. In 1836, 13 percent of it originated in the western states; in 1847 western freight passed New York's own for the first time; by 1860 the flow was 2.28 million tons, 83 percent of it western. In 1849, the year the [American Panorama canals atlas](https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/canals/) uses as its benchmark, the Erie carried 1,622,444 tons of freight in all. The total is impressive; the changing origin is the finding. The canal did not merely serve an existing trade. It created its own hinterland, teaching Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois farmers to grow wheat for New York harbor, and it rotated the interior's commerce ninety degrees: produce that had drifted south to New Orleans now ran east to the Atlantic.

## The network fills in: steamboats, the canal boom, railroads

New York's success set every rival port digging, and the map filled in over three decades in three layers. Steamboats came first: Robert Fulton's Hudson River service dated to 1807, and the machine mattered most on the western rivers, where "downstream" had been the only direction that paid. More than two hundred steamboats worked the Mississippi system by 1830, moving cargo upstream against the current and cutting upstream rates hardest. The canal boom came second: Pennsylvania answered the Erie with its Main Line to Pittsburgh, Ohio cut two canals linking Lake Erie to the Ohio River by 1840, and total canal mileage passed three thousand. Railroads came third and outgrew everything: the Baltimore & Ohio began building in 1827, the country had 9,000 miles of track by 1850 and more than 30,000 by 1860, roughly two-thirds of it in the free states.

The unevenness in the last frame is structural, not accidental. The South's capital was invested in land and enslaved people, its cotton moved cheaply downriver to ports as it always had, and its planters saw little reason to tax themselves for canals; the paying case for iron and locks was weakest exactly where the plantation already had its river. The canals and railroads therefore bound the Northwest to the Northeast, not to the South, and the "North" that fought the sectional battles of the 1850s as one bloc was in part their creation. <Ref lesson="cotton-kingdom">The Cotton Kingdom lesson</Ref> prices the economy that chose the river.

## Information outran freight

Freight got cheap; information got fast, and faster than freight, which is Howe's central observation about the era. The first carrier was the state itself. The Post Office was the federal government's largest civilian presence by a wide margin: about three-quarters of all federal civilian employees worked for it, its payroll quadrupled in the generation after 1815, and its thousands of local offices reached villages no other federal institution touched. Under the postal law of 1792 newspapers traveled at a token rate, a deliberate subsidy to circulation, and print grew on it: the country's newspaper titles multiplied more than tenfold between 1790 and 1835, and the copies mailed each year more than twentyfold. In 1833 the *New York Sun* cut the price of a daily paper from six cents to one, and the penny press made the news a mass commodity. Speed rose with volume: an express relay carried the text of President Jackson's 1831 annual message from Washington to New York in 15.5 hours, against the week that news of Washington's death had needed to travel a shorter distance in 1799.

Culture traveled the same channels and sold the same way. Charles Willson Peale's Philadelphia museum, opened in the 1780s and installed after 1802 in the Long Room of the old Pennsylvania State House, charged 25 cents at the door and sold natural history to a paying public; the mastodon skeleton Peale excavated in 1801 cost 50 cents extra. His 1822 self-portrait shows the proprietor lifting a curtain on the ranked cases like a showman, which is what a museum on ticket revenue was. The formula scaled and coarsened: the collection was sold to P. T. Barnum in 1849. A mass audience that paid a penny for news and a quarter for a mastodon was a market product in its own right, and it is the audience the mass politics of <Ref lesson="jacksonian-democracy">the next lesson</Ref> was built to reach.

### 1844: the message leaves the mail

Every improvement so far moved information by moving paper faster. The telegraph ended the coupling. Congress paid Samuel Morse \$30,000 in 1843 to string an experimental line the forty miles from Washington to Baltimore along the B&O right of way, and on May 24, 1844, from the old Supreme Court chamber in the Capitol, Morse tapped a test message to his partner at Baltimore's Mount Clare depot: "What hath God wrought." The receiver embossed the dots and dashes on a paper strip, Morse annotated it, and the strip survives in the [Library of Congress's Morse papers](https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-f-b-morse-papers/). Within four years telegraph wires were carrying Mexican War battle reports to eastern newspapers in days, and the era that had opened with an army fighting two weeks after its war ended closed with information moving at the speed of electricity.

The era is bracketed by its own communications revolution: in 1815 the peace of Ghent took 49 days to reach America and a battle was fought inside the gap; on May 24, 1844 a message left Washington and arrived in Baltimore in the same instant.

## The household turned inside out

Cheap distance reached into the kitchen. The subsistence household of 1800 was a small factory: women spun and wove its cloth, sewed its clothing, made its candles, soap, butter, and cheese, and the account book with the neighbors settled in kind what little was traded. Factory cloth unravelled that first. Once mill yardage undersold homespun, the spinning wheel stopped paying, and the family bought cloth with cash raised by selling whatever its farm did best; the diary of the Maine midwife Martha Ballard, dense with spinning and weaving entries from 1785 to 1812, records the world that stopped. Buying required cash, cash required a sale crop, and a sale crop tied the farm to prices set in markets no one in town controlled. The country store extended credit against the harvest, banks stood behind the store, and the *cash nexus* had replaced the neighborhood ledger.

Time changed with the goods. Farm work is task time, set by daylight, weather, and the animals; mill and shop work is clock time, set by a bell that rings whether the task is done or not, and the clock on the mantel was itself typically the household's first factory-made purchase. The reorganization sorted people as well as hours. In families that could live on one income, paid work moved out of the house with the husband while the wife's unpaid work stayed in it, and the arrangement hardened into the ideology of separate spheres: a public male world of production and a domestic female one, the mark of the new middle class. Poorer households could not afford the ideal, which is precisely what made it a class marker, and their daughters went where the cash was: the mills.

## Lowell: the model factory put to the test

Industrialization proper began as espionage. Samuel Slater built America's first water-powered spinning mill at Pawtucket, Rhode Island in 1790 from machinery he had memorized as an English apprentice, and in 1813 Francis Cabot Lowell came home from a tour of Manchester's mills carrying the power loom in his head. The system his partners built at the town they named for him, incorporated in the 1820s on the Merrimack River falls north of Boston, integrated every step from bale to bolt under one roof. It also integrated the workforce's whole life, and that design answered a real anxiety: everyone knew Manchester, England as a slum of permanent, degraded operatives, and republican theory doubted that a factory class and self-government could coexist. Lowell was built to be the refutation: hire respectable New England farm daughters for a few years each, house them in corporation boardinghouses with matrons, curfews, and compulsory church, pay cash wages, and return them home unharmed. This is *wage labor*, work sold by the hour for cash, offered in its best possible dress.

By 1840 the Lowell mills employed more than 8,000 workers, nearly three-quarters of them women, most between fifteen and thirty. Wages ran \$3 to \$4 a week with about \$1.25 deducted for board, for roughly 73 hours at the machines. The women wrote and edited their own magazine, the *Lowell Offering* (1840–1845), and visitors from two continents came to see the factory that had allegedly solved the factory problem. That is the claim to test, and the workforce's own records supply the evidence for both sides.

<Example title="testing the Lowell claim">
**The claim.** The Waltham–Lowell system was an egalitarian alternative to English factory labor: temporary, respectable, and beneficial to the women who passed through it.

**Evidence for.** The wages were real and the highest cash earnings available to New England women in any occupation; operatives banked savings, paid off family mortgages, and financed their own or their brothers' schooling. The boardinghouse community was dense with lending libraries, evening classes, and the *Offering* itself, written by workers. Turnover supports "temporary": most women stayed a few years and left, usually to marry, rather than forming a hereditary mill class. Against Manchester's child labor and slum mortality, the contrast was not propaganda.

**Evidence against.** The system's economics tightened as competition rose, and each squeeze landed on the workers. In February 1834 the corporations announced a 12.5 percent wage cut; about 800 women "turned out" in protest, and the strike failed within days. In October 1836 the corporations raised the board charge, an effective pay cut of about a dollar a week; this time 1,500 or more turned out, and the corporations backed down. Through the 1840s came the speed-up and the stretch-out (faster machines, more machines per operative) without matching pay, and the workers' response outgrew strikes: the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, founded by twelve operatives under Sarah Bagley in January 1845, grew to five hundred members in six months, published the *Voice of Industry*, and sent ten-hour petitions with thousands of signatures to the legislature, which received them and did nothing. After 1845 the corporations found a workforce that could not go home to the farm: Irish famine immigrants, hired at lower wages, without the boardinghouse apparatus. By 1860 the Yankee mill girl was a minority in the mills and the "experiment" had quietly ended.

**Verdict.** The claim is true, temporarily and by design's accident rather than design. Lowell was better than Manchester so long as the corporations needed to attract respectable farm daughters who had somewhere else to go; the moral machinery was a recruiting cost, and when a cheaper workforce appeared the machinery was dropped. The women's own testimony cuts both ways and should: the *Offering* is evidence of literacy and pride, the turn-outs are evidence of grievance, and both were written by the same population.
</Example>

One participant's voice, recorded long after: Harriet Hanson Robinson, a child worker in the mill in 1836, [remembered leading her room out](https://www.americanyawp.com/reader/the-market-revolution/harriet-h-robinson-describes-a-mill-workers-strike-1863/) in that October's turn-out: "I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether any one else does or not." She also judged that "so far as results were concerned this strike did no good," the corporations going on cutting wages afterward. Memoir is evidence of a particular kind, written six decades later by a suffragist making an argument about women's capacity, and one of this lesson's exercises reads it with exactly that caution. What no source disputes: the women in the model factory concluded, within a decade of its founding, that their interests and the corporations' were not the same, which is the fact the model was built to prevent. Whether wage labor itself was freedom or a new dependency became a standing American argument from this decade forward; the free-labor ideology that answers it arrives with <Ref lesson="sectional-crisis">the Republicans</Ref>.

</KeyFact>

## Credit: the fuel, and its two collapses

Every canal boat, mill, and stocked country store in this lesson ran on borrowed money, and the borrowing ran through a banking system growing faster than anyone could supervise it. The United States had 1 state-chartered bank in 1783, 266 by 1820, 702 by 1840, and 1,371 by 1860. Each issued its own paper notes, redeemable in gold or silver coin (*specie*) on demand; the notes circulated as the working currency, at discounts that grew with distance from the issuer, alongside a swarm of counterfeits. Credit chained the system together: the farmer owed the storekeeper, the storekeeper owed the city merchant, the merchant owed the bank, and the bank had printed several notes for every specie dollar in its vault. The chain financed expansion nobody could have saved for; it also meant that one hard pull at any link ran the length of it.

It failed twice in a generation, and the first failure taught the pattern. In 1819, a postwar boom in land and cotton, pumped by easy bank credit, collapsed when cotton prices broke and the Second Bank of the United States abruptly tightened. The Second Bank, chartered in 1816 as a larger successor to <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">Hamilton's national bank of 1791</Ref>, held the federal government's deposits, and because state banks' notes flowed into it with the government's revenue it could discipline their issue by returning the notes for specie; in 1818–19, protecting its own reserves, it did so all at once. Land values fell, note-holders demanded specie that did not exist, and the country got its first modern peacetime depression, with foreclosures across the West and relief laws fought through the state legislatures. Recovery bred a bigger boom in the 1830s, this one in western land, canals, cotton, and enslaved people, and it broke the same way but harder. Cotton fell 25 percent in February and March 1837; on May 10, 1837 New York's banks stopped redeeming notes in specie, and every bank in the country followed. Of about 850 American banks, 343 closed outright and 62 partially failed. Several states defaulted on canal-built debts, and the depression that followed lasted into the mid-1840s.

The panic's politics belong to <Ref lesson="jacksonian-democracy">the next lesson</Ref>, because the credit system's fate had already become the central question of mass politics: Andrew Jackson's war on the Second Bank ended federal supervision of the note-issuing banks in 1836, one year before they all stopped paying. What belongs here is the structural point: the market revolution was not a machine with a crash as an occasional malfunction. Boom, overextension, and panic were the machine, and Americans of 1819, 1837, and 1857 learned it each time by losing farms bought at boom prices.

<Aside title="what financed the growth: North's cotton engine">
Douglass North's *The Economic Growth of the United States, 1790–1860* (1961), the classic quantitative model of this era, ties the whole structure to one export. In North's account, cotton earned the foreign exchange, cotton profits and their London credit financed the imports and much of the banking, and the plantation South bought western food and northeastern services, so that the three regions grew as one interlocked system with the Erie Canal and its kin as the connectors. The model explains, among other things, why the panics of 1819 and 1837 both begin with the price of cotton. Later scholarship has cut the cotton multiplier down: the West fed itself and eastern cities more than it fed the plantations, and internal Northern trade was larger than North's model assumed. The dependence runs in both directions and the next module weighs it from the Southern side; the version to retain is the weaker, well-supported one: the antebellum boom was export-led, and the export was cotton.
</Aside>

What is now established: the tyranny of distance is broken, by water for freight and by wire for words; the ledger shows a hinterland converted into a market; the household, the workshop, and the mill are reorganized around cash, clock, and credit; and the credit is periodically lethal. Thesis element four, technology, has done its first full turn: cheap land became one market because a literate commercial population built the machinery to connect it. Two consequences are already visible and belong to the next lessons. The same market society produced a mass electorate and the machinery to mobilize it, and the same cotton engine that financed the boom bound slavery to it. <Ref lesson="jacksonian-democracy">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the first: the politics of the white male mass franchise, the Bank War, and Indian removal, advancing together.

(b) The Erie tidewater series: the western share and tonnage in 1836 and in 1860, and the crossover year. (c) The two dates that bracket the era's communications revolution, and what each stands for.

<Solution>
(a) \$9 moved a ton of goods across the Atlantic or 30 miles overland — roughly 30 cents per ton-mile by wagon — so hauling a ton of wheat (worth about \$33) 150 miles cost more than the wheat; grain could not travel by road. (b) 1836: 54,000 tons from the western states, 13 percent of tidewater arrivals; 1847: western freight passes New York's own; 1860: 1.9 million western tons, 83 percent of a 2.28-million-ton total. The changing origin, not the total, is the point: the canal built its own hinterland. (c) 1815: the Battle of New Orleans, fought two weeks into a peace whose news was still at sea (49 days for the treaty to cross); May 24, 1844: "What hath God wrought" travels Washington to Baltimore instantly. The era opens with information trapped in the mail and closes with it on the wire.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Repeat the worked freight computation for the trunk route. Before the canal, freight from Buffalo to New York City went overland, roughly 360 miles at the 1816 wagon rate of about 30 cents per ton-mile, taking about 20 days. After 1825 it went by canal to Albany and down the Hudson at a through rate of roughly 2 to 3 cents per ton-mile. Compute the before and after cost of moving one ton, compare your result with the figures tradition quotes for this route, and state in one sentence what the change did to the map — then state the main uncertainty in the computation.

<Solution>
Before: $360 \times \$0.30 \approx \$108$ per ton. After, at 2 to 3 cents per ton-mile: between $360 \times \$0.02 \approx \$7$ and $360 \times \$0.03 \approx \$11$ per ton. Tradition quotes "\$100 a ton before, \$10 after, twenty days down to about eight," and the computation reproduces it from the ton-mile rates: the round numbers are the same order-of-magnitude fact. What it did to the map: Buffalo, and behind it the entire Great Lakes basin, moved into New York City's economic hinterland, which is why the tidewater ledger fills with western freight after 1836. The main uncertainty: the rates are averages that mix tolls, freight charges, insurance, and year-to-year variation, and the wagon figure is an 1816 benchmark rather than a constant — so the answer is trustworthy to its order of magnitude, not its dollars, and the argument needs nothing more.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing drill to Harriet Hanson Robinson's account of the 1836 turn-out. Metadata: published 1898 in her memoir *Loom and Spindle*; the author was a child worker in the mill during the October 1836 turn-out — the corporation agent's complaint to her mother, in her telling, was that "your daughter is a child, and her you could control"; by 1898 she was a veteran suffrage campaigner writing to show what the early mill girls had been and become. Predict from the metadata what the account will emphasize and omit; then state what it records, what it argues, and what it can and cannot be used to establish about the 1836 strike.

<Solution>
Prediction: a suffragist's memoir written six decades later will cast the turn-out as an early proof of women's political capacity, foreground her own agency, and blur operational details (dates, numbers, demands) that memory does not keep; expect vividness about the moment of decision and vagueness about the aftermath. Records: that a child worker perceived the strike as a moral event; the internal scene of hesitation in her room and her leading it out ("I don't care what you do, I am going to turn out"); a participant's estimate that "twelve or fifteen hundred girls turned out"; her verdict that "so far as results were concerned this strike did no good." Argues: that mill women were self-respecting actors rather than victims — the memoir's purpose is to enlist the mill girls' history in the case for women's citizenship. Can establish: that the turn-out happened, was large, was led and experienced by the women themselves, and was remembered as formative; her numbers usefully corroborate contemporary reports of 1,500+. Cannot establish, alone: the strike's precise course or outcome (her "did no good" folds the strike into the wages' later slide, while the payroll-era evidence shows the corporations backed down on the board charge), or typical operative attitudes, since the author is by construction the exceptional case who stayed articulate and public. Use it for the texture and the fact of agency; use the 1836 sources for the outcome.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
A second claim, in the pattern of the worked Lowell test: "The *Lowell Offering* proves that the mill workers were contented and that the turn-outs were the work of a disaffected few." Name the strongest evidence for the claim, at least two facts it fails to account for, and write the defensible version of what the *Offering* establishes.

<Solution>
Strongest evidence for: the *Offering* existed at all — five years (1840–1845) of poems, essays, and fiction written and edited by operatives is real evidence of literacy, leisure enough to write, and pride in the work; no Manchester mill produced its equivalent. What the claim fails to account for: first, the participation numbers run the wrong way — the turn-outs mobilized 800 (1834) and 1,500+ (1836) operatives, and the ten-hour petitions carried thousands of signatures, while the *Offering*'s contributors numbered a few dozen, so if either source represents "a few," it is the magazine; second, the same population produced both bodies of evidence, sometimes the same individuals, and the operatives' reform paper (the *Voice of Industry*, under Sarah Bagley, herself a former *Offering* contributor) attacked the *Offering* precisely for its gentleness toward the corporations, which shows contemporaries already contesting its representativeness; third, the magazine's run ended in 1845, exactly when the LFLRA organized, so its silence about grievance is bounded in time. Defensible version: the *Offering* establishes that Lowell's workforce included educated, articulate women who took pride in their independence — which is evidence for the "egalitarian alternative" claim's *for* column, not proof of contentment; on the question of grievance it is one voice in an argument the workers were having among themselves, and the strike and petition numbers say which side of that argument was larger.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating causes of the antebellum North's market integration, and defend the ranking in a short paragraph: (a) the collapse of transport costs after 1825; (b) the expansion of state bank credit; (c) Southern cotton exports as the economy's earner of foreign exchange (North's thesis).

<Solution>
One defensible ranking. Necessary: (a). At 30 cents per ton-mile no bulk commodity could travel, whatever the credit or export conditions; the freight computation shows wheat consuming its own value in 150 road miles. Remove cheap carriage and there is no interregional market to integrate — every other factor operates through this one. Enabling: (b). The canals, mills, stocked stores, and westward farm-making all ran on borrowed money that note-issuing banks multiplied (1 bank in 1783, 702 by 1840); credit set the speed of integration and its instability, but credit without cheap freight had nothing to finance, as the pre-1815 interior shows. Precipitating is the wrong category for (c), which is better classed as enabling at the system level: in North's model cotton earned the exchange and anchored the credit that (b) distributed, and the panics of 1819 and 1837 both start with the cotton price, which is strong evidence of the linkage; but later scholarship shows the West's food went more to eastern cities than to plantations, so cotton was one engine among several rather than the load-bearing one. The ranking is arguable between (b) and (c) — a defender of North would promote cotton to co-necessary — but (a) is not arguable: the price of distance is the constraint everything else waited on.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In <Ref lesson="jeffersonian-america">the previous lesson</Ref>, Jefferson's *empire of liberty* justified continental expansion as the multiplication of independent citizen-farmers — the republic's load-bearing material. Using at least three facts from this lesson, explain how the market revolution both fulfilled and undermined that program.

<Solution>
Fulfilled: the market revolution is what made the empire of liberty's land economically real. The Louisiana-scale interior was worthless to a citizen-farmer who could not sell a crop; the Erie Canal and its successors converted that land into viable farms (western tidewater freight growing from 54,000 tons in 1836 to 1.9 million in 1860 is the process measured), and the farmers who filled Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois were recognizably Jefferson's multiplied freeholders, selling wheat instead of subsisting. Undermined, in two ways. First, the independent household itself dissolved into dependence on markets: the farm family of 1840 bought its cloth, borrowed at the store against the harvest, and lived at the mercy of prices, credit, and panics set far away — the panics of 1819 and 1837 foreclosed exactly the freeholders the theory prized. Second, the same process created the class the theory had no place for: permanent wage laborers. Lowell's 8,000 operatives, and the Irish workforce that replaced them after 1845, sold their hours for cash with no farm to return to, and republican theory from Jefferson forward had held that such dependence was incompatible with citizenship. The market revolution therefore delivered the empire of liberty's geography while dissolving its sociology — a tension the free-labor ideology of the 1850s was invented to resolve.
</Solution>

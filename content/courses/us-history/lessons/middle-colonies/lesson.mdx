By 1750 Philadelphia was on its way to being the second city of the British
Empire, and its colony had never had an established church — an
eighteenth-century anomaly that looks, in hindsight, like a prototype.

The course's engine argument now has two cases on the table: the previous
lesson set a tobacco-and-servants Chesapeake beside a family-and-church New
England, both self-governing, both prosperous, one committed by statute to
hereditary slavery. A third English society sits between them, and it fits
neither column. This lesson supplies that case: New York, a conquered Dutch
port that kept its merchants and its mixture, and Pennsylvania, a Quaker
proprietary whose founding documents made liberty of conscience a statute.
The claim to establish is that pluralism functioned as an institution here,
not an accident of neglect, and that it paid: the colonies that admitted
everyone grew fastest, and the configuration they reached by 1750 is the one
the national settlement of 1787–91 later generalized.

## New Netherland's inheritance: a port before a polity

The Dutch entrepôt model from <Ref lesson="european-empires">the three-empires
lesson</Ref> planted a trading post, not a society, and the trading post set
the region's character before any English constitution touched it. New
Amsterdam was a company town: the Dutch West India Company wanted cargo
turnover, and it recruited whoever would come. The French Jesuit Isaac
Jogues, passing through in 1643, was told by the director-general that the
roughly five hundred men at Manhattan's tip spoke "eighteen different
languages." The port held Walloons, Germans, Scandinavians, Sephardic Jews,
English dissenters, and, by 1660, the **largest urban enslaved population on
the continent**, working the company's docks and walls.

The company's tolerance was commercial policy, not conviction, and
Director-General Peter Stuyvesant tested it. In 1656–57 he ordered fines and
banishment for anyone harboring Quakers. In December 1657 the English
townsmen of Flushing, on Long Island, answered with a signed petition now
called the [Flushing Remonstrance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flushing_Remonstrance):
thirty signers, none of them Quakers, refusing to enforce the order. The law
of "love, peace and liberty," they wrote, extended even "to Jews, Turks and
Egyptians, as they are considered sonnes of Adam," and as for the Quakers,
"wee cannot in conscience lay violent hands upon them."

A principle asserted this plainly would be expected to carry the argument
that followed; the record runs the other way. Stuyvesant jailed the town
clerk who drafted the petition and removed the sheriff who endorsed it, and
the Remonstrance changed no policy. What changed policy, six years later,
was arithmetic.

The winning appeal came from a farmer. John Bowne, banished in 1662 for
hosting Quaker meetings in his house, sailed to Amsterdam and put his case
to the company's directors, and they overruled their own director-general:
toleration was to be restored, because the colony's growth could not spare
dissenters. Bowne came home in 1664. Conscience won in New Netherland as a
**business ruling**, where the petition arguing from Scripture had failed,
and that is precisely why the ruling survived what came next.

### 1664: conquest by fleet, continuity by charter

Conquest is where an inherited toleration should have died: a new sovereign,
a proprietor who governed without any elected assembly, a state church at
home. The record shows the opposite: the module's cleanest case of
institutional continuity. In August 1664 four English frigates anchored off
New Amsterdam and demanded surrender; the town's merchants declined to fight
for the company, and Stuyvesant capitulated without a shot. The Articles of
Capitulation changed the flag and almost nothing else: the Dutch kept their
property and inheritance customs and "the liberty of their consciences in
religion." The colony became New York, after its new proprietor the Duke of
York, and the port kept its languages, its Dutch merchant families, and its
toleration in practice.

Practice is the operative word. New York never wrote a Pennsylvania-style
guarantee: the assembly's 1683 Charter of Liberties protected only those who
"professe ffaith in God by Jesus Christ," and the Ministry Act of 1693 taxed
four counties around the port for a Protestant ministry that royal governors
treated as an Anglican establishment. And the port's pluralism stopped at
the slave market. Enslaved people were about one in five of New York City's
population, and the line was held by exemplary violence: an armed rising in
1712 and an alleged conspiracy in 1741 each ended in mass executions, the
second on trial evidence historians still dispute.

<KeyFact>
New York inherited its pluralism rather than legislating it: a company
recruiting policy, confirmed by conquest terms in 1664, made the port the
most heterogeneous in the empire, while an establishment stood on paper and
one person in five in the city was enslaved.
</KeyFact>

## Penn's constitution: conscience as statute

New York shows toleration as inherited practice; Pennsylvania is the
deliberate version, and it was written by a man the English state had
repeatedly jailed. William Penn, son of an admiral, converted young to the
Society of Friends and spent time in the Tower of London and Newgate prison
for preaching and publishing. At his 1670 trial for street preaching the jury
refused to convict, was locked up without food for its verdict, and was
vindicated on appeal: a landmark for jury independence that returns below in
the Zenger case.

  src="./figures/middle-colonies-penn-portrait.png"
  float="right"
  width={380}
  alt="Engraved half-length portrait of William Penn in a broad tricorne hat, long curled wig, white cravat and a plain dark coat, set inside a double-ruled oval frame."
  caption="William Penn, engraved in the eighteenth century. The man who wrote Pennsylvania's constitution had been jailed in England for his preaching, and had watched a London jury refuse to convict him for it."

The colony began as a debt settlement. The Crown owed Penn's late father
£16,000 it was in no position to pay in cash, and the charter of 1681
discharged the debt with about 45,000 square miles west of the Delaware, an
area not far short of England itself. Penn called the venture a "holy
experiment" and constituted it on paper before most colonists sailed: a
Frame of Government in 1682, twice revised within fifteen years, and
alongside it an
[Act for Freedom of Conscience](https://oll.libertyfund.org/title/lutz-colonial-origins-of-the-american-constitution)
(December 1682) protecting everyone who "shall confess and acknowledge one
almighty God" from being "molested or prejudiced for his or her
conscientious persuasion or practice."

### The Charter of Privileges, read closely

The constitution that lasted came in 1701. The
[Charter of Privileges](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/pa07.asp),
granted in October 1701, replaced the Frames and governed Pennsylvania
until 1776: seventy-five years, one of the longest service lives of any
written constitution then in existence. Its first article is the operative
text, and it repays exact reading.

<Task>
Before the close reading, predict from the author's position. Penn in 1701
was a proprietor recruiting settlers to an underpopulated province, and a
convict of England's conscience laws. Write down what you expect his
toleration article to grant, and where you expect it to draw its boundary;
then read Article I below and check both predictions.
</Task>

<Example title="reading a toleration statute's boundary">
Article I's operative sentence grants, in sequence: no person "who shall
confess and acknowledge one almighty God … shall be in any case molested or
prejudiced in his or their person or estate because of his or their
conscientious persuasion or practice, nor be compelled to frequent or
maintain any religious worship, place, or ministry."

Mark what that sentence does. **The grant:** immunity of person and property
for religious practice, and no compelled attendance or church tax: the
absence of establishment, as law. **The boundary:** the grant's subject is
anyone who acknowledges "one almighty God." A Jew, a Muslim, or a Deist is
inside; an atheist is not.

The article's second clause draws a narrower circle for power: "all persons
who also profess to believe in Jesus Christ … shall be capable … to serve
this government." Office, unlike worship, is restricted to Christians.

One more clause completes the design. The charter can be amended only by the
governor with "six parts of seven of the Assembly"; Article I is
exempted even from that, to "remain without any alteration, inviolably
forever." A toleration that earlier depended on a company's balance sheet or
a proprietor's temper is here entrenched above ordinary law.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/charter-dissected.svg"
  alt="The Charter of Privileges' Article I with grant phrases (no molestation, no compelled worship) and boundary phrases (acknowledge one almighty God; office for professing Christians) labeled, an entrenchment clause, and a duration bar from 1701 to 1776"
  caption="One sentence, dissected: the grant, its two boundaries, and the entrenchment that kept it in force for 75 years."
/>

The boundaries matter because a documented misconception sits here. What the
middle colonies pioneered was toleration as enforceable, entrenched law
inside a stated circle. The popular account, that colonial America was
founded on religious freedom in the modern sense, does not survive the
documents, and it fails for a specific reason: it reads a period legal
instrument as a timeless principle. Every toleration text of the era draws a
boundary and most keep a sanction; what varies, and what the close reading
above measures, is who stands inside the grant, who may hold power, and
whether anything stands above ordinary law. On those three questions
Pennsylvania's article is radical for 1701 in its mechanics (no church tax
ever, an unamendable core) while still fencing office with a creed. The
Maryland statute in the exercises, a "toleration act" that made denying the
Trinity a capital crime, is the discriminating case.

The rest of the charter matches the article's temper. It created the only
**unicameral assembly** in British America, elected yearly, with the
privileges of the Commons; it accepted affirmations in place of oaths, which
opened juries, courts, and offices to Quakers and Mennonites; and it
established no militia, in a colony run by pacifists. Fischer's *Albion's
Seed* reads this as the third of his British folkways: over twenty thousand
Friends from the North Midlands of England and from Wales carrying a
"reciprocal liberty" grounded in the golden rule. The synthesis is contested
in its parts, but the migration and the documents it produced are on the
record.

<KeyFact>
Pennsylvania made liberty of conscience a statute in 1682 and an entrenched
constitutional article in 1701, and the 1701 charter ran unamended to 1776:
a working written constitution one long lifetime before the federal one.
</KeyFact>

## Pluralism as economics: toleration recruited

The charter was also an advertisement, and it was marketed as one. Penn's
promotional tract *Some Account of the Province of Pennsilvania* (1681) was
printed in German and Dutch translations in its first year; agents sold land
across the Rhine valley; and in 1683 thirteen families from Krefeld, a
weaving town on the lower Rhine, founded Germantown just outside
Philadelphia under Francis Daniel Pastorius, the first German settlement in
English America. The instruments of recruitment (conscience by statute,
affirmation in place of oaths, cheap land, easy naturalization) were all in
place before the population curve turned.

<Figure
  src="./figures/pluralism-recruits.svg"
  alt="Pennsylvania's population curve from about 700 in 1680 to about 327,000 in 1780, with a box listing the recruitment instruments in place from 1681 to 1683: the German-printed promotional tract, the conscience statute, Germantown's founding, affirmations, and easy naturalization"
  caption="Recruitment as policy: the instruments date from 1681–83; the curve is what they bought. Census-derived estimates."
/>

The curve is the payoff. A colony of about 700 Europeans in 1680 passed
18,000 by 1700 and reached roughly a third of a million by 1780: the
**fastest-growing colony** in British America, from the latest start.
German-speaking migrants (the "Pennsylvania Dutch," who were Germans, from
*Deutsch*) filled the interior counties; Scots-Irish migrants from Ulster,
Ireland's northern province, landed at Philadelphia by the tens of thousands
from the late 1710s and pushed to the frontier. This is
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">settler selection</Ref> operating by a
different filter: not a covenant screening for the godly, but an open door
selecting for whoever found every other door closed.

### Wheat, flour, and the port that shipped them

The migrants' farms made the economics circular. Southeastern Pennsylvania's
limestone soils grew wheat, wheat became flour in the mills around
Philadelphia, and flour fed the Caribbean sugar islands and southern Europe
through the port. The region earned its contemporary name, "the best poor
man's country": the eighteenth-century wage measurement of
<Ref lesson="colonial-societies">the previous lesson</Ref> applied at full
strength in a colony where land was cheap, labor dear, and no tithe or
establishment taxed either.

<Figure
  src="./figures/philadelphia-passes-boston.svg"
  alt="Two population lines from 1700 to 1775: Boston rising to about 16,000 by 1743 and then flat, Philadelphia rising from about 2,500 to about 32,000 and crossing Boston around 1755"
  caption="The commercial payoff in one chart: Boston stalls at about 16,000 after 1743; Philadelphia, seventy years younger, passes it in the 1750s."

Philadelphia compounded with its colony. Boston, the largest town in English
America for a century, stalled near 16,000 people in the 1740s; Philadelphia
passed it in the 1750s and reached roughly 32,000 with its suburbs by 1775,
the largest city in British America and second in the empire after London.
On top of the commerce grew a civic layer this course meets again in
Franklin: within one generation the city organized itself a subscription
library, a fire company, a learned society, a college, and a hospital:
voluntary associations doing work that towns did by covenant in New England
and vestries did by law in Virginia.

<Aside>
The association ledger, dated: the Junto club (1727), the Library Company
(1731), the Union Fire Company (1736), the American Philosophical Society
(1743), the Academy (1749) that became the University of Pennsylvania, and
Pennsylvania Hospital (1751). Benjamin Franklin, printer, had a founding
hand in every one of them.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Toleration recruited: with conscience by statute, affirmations, and cheap
land, Pennsylvania grew from about 700 people to a third of a million in a
century and built the largest city in British America; growth the
homogeneous colonies did not match.
</KeyFact>

## Quaker distinctives: meetings, women, and the first antislavery petition

Two Quaker institutions carried consequences beyond the Society of Friends,
and both are documented in the period's own records rather than in later
memory.

### Women's meetings with jurisdiction

The first is the structure of the meeting itself. Friends governed
by monthly, quarterly, and yearly meetings, and from the 1680s Pennsylvania
Quakers ran parallel **women's meetings** with real jurisdiction: approving
or refusing proposed marriages, disciplining members, administering poor
relief. Women also preached. The Society recognized traveling women
ministers, and Quaker women spoke in worship and crossed the Atlantic on
ministry at a time when no other English denomination licensed a woman's
public voice.

### The Germantown Protest, 1688

The second is the earliest recorded antislavery protest in English America,
and its reception breaks the natural expectation: from the Society whose
name later attached to abolitionism, one would expect adoption; the record
shows the petition tabled for decades.

On February 18, 1688, four Germantown Friends signed one page against "the
traffick of mens-body": Francis Daniel Pastorius, Garret Hendericks, and
the brothers Derick and Abraham op den Graeff. The
[argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1688_Germantown_Quaker_Petition_Against_Slavery)
is the golden rule applied without exception: there is no liberty of
conscience worth the name for those "who steal or robb men," and what
Christians would not have done to themselves they may not do to others,
whatever their "descent or colour." The monthly meeting found the matter too
weighty and passed it up; the quarterly meeting passed it up again; the
yearly meeting tabled it. The page then sat in meeting records until 1844.

Both halves of that record are facts to keep together. The petition is the
first of its kind in English America, and it was **tabled for decades**:
Penn himself held enslaved people at Pennsbury, his manor up the Delaware,
slave labor worked Philadelphia's docks, and the Society of Friends moved
against slaveholding only in the 1750s–70s, disciplining traders from 1758
and barring slaveholders in 1776. Pennsylvania's gradual abolition act of
1780, the first of its kind, descends from that late turn, not from an
early consensus.

<KeyFact>
The Germantown Protest of 1688 is the first recorded antislavery petition in
English America, and the meeting that received it tabled it; Quaker
antislavery won inside the Society only in the 1750s–70s. Both dates belong
in the same sentence.
</KeyFact>

## Women's legal standing: three regimes

The women's meeting is one of three legal environments colonial women
actually lived under in the middle colonies, and setting them side by side
completes the module's social picture. The comparison is legal-historical:
statutes, wills, and cases, not themes.

**The baseline is coverture.** At English common law a married woman was a
*feme covert*: in the English jurist William Blackstone's later formulation
(1765), "the very being or legal existence of the woman is suspended during
the marriage." She could not own property apart from her husband, contract,
sue, or make a will without his consent; her protection was dower, a life
interest in one third of his real estate at widowhood. Every English colony,
Pennsylvania included, imported this doctrine.

**Dutch law ran on a different principle.** New Netherland followed
Roman-Dutch practice: most couples married in community of goods, spouses
commonly wrote *mutual wills* naming each other as equal heirs, and a
married woman could register as a trader and do business in her own name.
The standing case is Margaret Hardenbroeck, a New Amsterdam merchant who
collected debts, owned ships, and crossed the Atlantic as supercargo through
two marriages. After 1664 the English courts phased her rights out: under
common law she could no longer trade on her own authority, and her profits
belonged to her husband. The transition is measurable, and the historian
Linda Biemer's count of New York records 1643–1727 measures it: married
women's wills, joint property arrangements, and women in trade all decline
as English law displaces Dutch.

<Figure
  src="./figures/womens-standing.svg"
  alt="Three-column table comparing English coverture, Dutch practice, and the Quaker meeting on property in marriage, will-making, and voice in governance, with an arrow marking the post-1664 erosion of the Dutch regime"
  caption="Three regimes, three answers. The Dutch column is the one that erodes: after 1664, English law phases out mutual wills and women traders."

**The Quaker meeting was a governance exception, not a property one.**
Pennsylvania law held Quaker wives to coverture like everyone else; what the
Society gave women was standing inside its own institutions: a meeting with
jurisdiction, a recognized ministry, a public voice. The three regimes thus
answer differently on different rows: the Dutch on property, the Quakers on
governance, the common law on neither. Two of the three answers were
narrowing over the eighteenth century as English law consolidated.

## The template by 1750

### The Zenger verdict: a practical precedent

Each element this lesson has established was, somewhere in the region,
already tested in public by mid-century, and the last element arrived
through the courts. In 1734 New York's governor William Cosby jailed the
printer John Peter Zenger for seditious libel over opposition essays in the
*New-York Weekly Journal*. At trial in 1735, Andrew Hamilton of
Philadelphia conceded publication and argued what the law did not yet allow:
that **truth is a defense**, and that a jury of neighbors, not a royal
judge, should say whether criticism of a governor is a crime. The jury
acquitted in about ten minutes.

The verdict set no legal rule: truth became a statutory defense in New York
only in 1805–06, and governors kept prosecuting printers. What it set was a
practical one. The trial report, *A Brief Narrative of the Case and Tryal of
John Peter Zenger* (1736), written largely by Zenger's editor James
Alexander, went through edition after edition in the colonies and London,
and no colonial jury afterward could be relied on to convict a printer for
criticizing a government. It is the Penn jury of 1670 operating a
generation later, in the same middle-colony courts.

### The outlier column

The region's configuration by 1750 can now be stated as one row of facts:
religious pluralism as working order in New York and as entrenched statute
in Pennsylvania; ethnic pluralism at demographic scale; a free-press
precedent made by a jury; and commercial primacy in the empire's
second city. Set against the module's other polities, the middle colonies
are the outlier on every institutional row, and the outlier is what the
United States later looks like.

What is now established: the settler engine's third case ran on selected,

rather than a covenant or a headright, and it compounded into pluralism,
commerce, and a written constitution that served seventy-five years. The
national settlement of 1787–91 (no religious establishment, no test oath
for federal office, a free press) generalized this column, as

will show in the documents. First, the three separate societies of this

religion," and separately made denying the Trinity or the divinity of Christ
punishable by death and forfeiture. State the grant, the boundary of the
grant, and the enforcement clause, and compare each with Pennsylvania's
Article I of 1701.

<Solution>
**Grant:** immunity from being "troubled, Molested or discountenanced" for
religion: protection of worship and person, like Penn's grant of
non-molestation.

**Boundary:** the protected class is persons "professing to believe in Jesus
Christ": Trinitarian and non-conforming Christians are inside; Jews, Deists,
and non-Christians are outside. Pennsylvania's 1701 boundary is wider (the
grant runs to anyone acknowledging "one almighty God"), with the Christian
test moved to office-holding rather than protection.

**Enforcement clause:** Maryland pairs its toleration with a capital
blasphemy law: denying the Trinity or Christ's divinity costs life and
estate. Pennsylvania's 1682 act punished profanity with five-shilling fines
and jail days, not death.

The comparison in one sentence: Maryland protects a narrower circle and
kills outside it; Pennsylvania protects a wider circle, fines at its edges,
and reserves only office for Christians.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the full source pass on the Flushing Remonstrance, using the
<Ref lesson="indigenous-america">records / argues / omits</Ref> method with
two additions. (a) Metadata: authors, date, addressee, and the signers' own
religious position. (b) Prediction: from the metadata alone, what would you
expect the document to appeal to: Dutch statute, the town's own founding
documents, or Scripture? (c) One thing the document records, one it argues,
one it omits. (d) Use: state what the Remonstrance can establish about
toleration in New Netherland and what it cannot, and how the fate of its
signers changes that use.

<Solution>
(a) Thirty English townsmen of Flushing, none of them Quakers, December
1657, addressed to Director-General Stuyvesant, refusing to execute his
anti-Quaker order.

(b) English settlers holding a Dutch town patent and risking punishment for
refusal would be expected to appeal to the strongest authorities available
to them: their town's chartered terms and religious duty, not Dutch statute,
which stood against them. The document does both: it invokes "the patent
and charter of our Towne," whose 1645 terms had promised liberty of
conscience after the custom of Holland, and it argues from Scripture ("the
law of love, peace and liberty," "sonnes of Adam").

(c) Records: that Stuyvesant's order existed and that thirty named
non-Quakers refused to enforce it. Argues: that conscience lies beyond the
magistrate's reach, for "Jews, Turks and Egyptians" as much as for Quakers.
Omits: any defense of Quaker doctrine (the signers defend hosting, not
belief) and any economic argument: the argument that ultimately won, in
Bowne's appeal, is exactly the one the Remonstrance does not make.

(d) It can establish that toleration as a principle was being asserted from
below, by ordinary townsmen, forty-four years before Penn entrenched it, and
that the principle's circle was drawn wide. It cannot establish that
toleration was New Netherland's policy or practice: it is a petition, not a
law, and it granted nothing, bound no court, and protected no one. The
signers' fate (clerk jailed, sheriff removed) converts the document into
evidence of the opposite condition: a government actively suppressing what
the petition asserted. Its provenance, a failed petition preserved in the
council's own records, makes it usable as evidence of assertion and of
repression, not of liberty enjoyed.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Two claims a survey might make: "Pennsylvania practiced religious liberty,"
citing the Charter of Privileges, and "the colonies had a free press,"
citing the Zenger verdict. For each artifact, state (a) what its genre and
provenance let it establish, (b) the part of the claim it cannot establish
on its own, and (c) one class of record that would close the gap. End with
one sentence on how the two artifacts fail in opposite directions.

<Solution>
**Charter of Privileges.** (a) An enacted constitutional text: it
establishes what the law promised, where the promise's boundaries ran, and
that the promise was entrenched above ordinary legislation. (b) It cannot
establish practice: whether a Catholic mass went unmolested or an
affirmation was actually accepted in court; even its seventy-five unamended
years are weak evidence, since a dead-letter text can also sit unamended.
(c) Corroboration comes from records of conduct: court dockets showing
affirmations accepted and conscience prosecutions absent, the roll of
denominations building churches in Philadelphia, naturalization records of
non-English migrants.

**Zenger verdict.** (a) A jury verdict plus a widely reprinted trial report:
it establishes that one 1735 jury refused to convict and that the example
circulated. (b) It cannot establish a right: seditious libel remained a
crime, truth remained no defense in law until 1805–06, and a governor could
still jail a printer awaiting trial, as Cosby held Zenger for eight months.
(c) Corroboration comes from the prosecution record after 1735: how many
seditious-libel prosecutions were brought and how they ended.

The failure directions are mirror images: the charter is text without proof
of practice, the verdict is practice without change in the text, which is
why this lesson reads each against the other kind of evidence.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reconstruct the Germantown Protest's argument as a syllogism, using the two
phrases quoted in this lesson, and then state, from the fate of the
document, what the argument's reception shows about the distance between a
principle's statement and its adoption.

<Solution>
The syllogism: (1) The rule Friends claim for themselves is that conscience
and person are inviolable, and its ground is the golden rule: do to others
what you would have done to you, making no difference of descent or colour.
(2) Slavery is "the traffick of mens-body": doing to others (seizure, sale,
lifetime bondage) what no one would have done to himself; those who buy
stolen men stand with those "who steal or robb men." (3) Therefore Friends
cannot hold slaves while claiming liberty of conscience.

The reception: the argument was never refuted, only deferred, monthly to
quarterly to yearly meeting, each finding it too weighty, and the page then
lay in the records until 1844. Adoption came seventy to ninety years later
(discipline for traders 1758, disownment of holders 1776, gradual abolition
1780), driven by a later generation. The distance between statement and
adoption was two to three generations, inside the very community whose own
stated principle the argument used.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two readings of middle-colony toleration compete. Reading A: toleration was
principle, Quaker "reciprocal liberty" enacted into law. Reading B:
toleration was recruitment economics, an immigration subsidy that any
labor-hungry colony would buy. (a) Using the census-derived estimates
(Pennsylvania: about 18,000 in 1700 and 327,000 in 1780; New York: about
19,100 in 1700 and 210,500 in 1780), compute the two growth multiples and
state what the comparison can and cannot establish, including its
uncertainty. (b) Name two documented facts from this lesson supporting each
reading. (c) State which facts decide, and what would have to be true of the
record for each reading to survive alone.

<Solution>
(a) Pennsylvania: $327{,}000 / 18{,}000 \approx 18$-fold. New York:
$210{,}500 / 19{,}100 \approx 11$-fold. From nearly identical 1700 bases,
the codified-toleration colony grew about two-thirds faster over the same
eighty years. The comparison establishes divergence, not cause: the figures
are census-derived reconstructions, and New York's great Hudson Valley land
patents suppressed migration independently of toleration, so the gap is
consistent with Reading B but cannot prove it: land policy and toleration
policy were both institutional choices, and the multiple cannot separate
them.

(b) For Reading A (principle): the Flushing signers and the Germantown
petitioners argued from Scripture, at personal cost, with nothing to sell
and nothing to gain; and Penn entrenched Article I "inviolably forever":
a recruiting device does not need to be unamendable, and the pacifist,
affirmation-run government cost Penn real proprietary power. For Reading B
(economics): the company restored Bowne's toleration explicitly because
growth "could not spare dissenters"; and Penn's tract was in German print
within a year, the toleration texts functioning as recruitment instruments
in the emigration market.

(c) The costly, non-recruiting cases decide against B standing alone: a
pure-economics reading cannot account for Flushing (principle asserted by
men with nothing to gain, and punished) or for entrenchment beyond what any
advertisement required. New Netherland's record decides against A standing
alone: there, toleration appeared exactly where and because it paid, and
vanished from law (the 1683 Christian boundary, the Ministry Act) when it
stopped being anyone's conviction. For B to survive alone, Flushing and
Germantown would have to be explainable as interest; for A to survive alone,
toleration would have to appear where it did not pay. The record supports a
split verdict: economics as the operating mechanism in New York, principle
entrenched and then marketed in Pennsylvania.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the change-and-continuity stem to the 1664 conquest. For each of four
groups (the port's merchants, its enslaved workers, its religious
dissenters, and its married women) state what changed, what persisted, and
on what timescale, using the Articles of Capitulation and Margaret
Hardenbroeck's career as evidence. End with one sentence on why the conquest
date is the wrong unit for measuring the change.

<Solution>
**Merchants:** sovereignty changed in a week; everything they cared about
(property, inheritance customs, trade) persisted by the Articles' text, and
Dutch merchant families remained prominent in the port for a century.

**Enslaved workers:** continuity of bondage across the conquest, then change
for the worse over decades: under English rule the trade grew, the city's
enslaved share reached about one in five, and the legal regime hardened:
the risings of 1712 and 1741 were answered with mass executions under
English law.

**Religious dissenters:** continuity first: the Articles confirmed "the
liberty of their consciences," preserving the company's business ruling.
Partial reversal came on paper a generation later, when the 1683 charter
drew a Christian boundary and the 1693 Ministry Act taxed four counties for
an establishment; practice stayed broader than law throughout.

**Married women:** the deepest change on the slowest clock. Nothing in the
Articles mentioned them, yet coverture displaced Roman-Dutch community
property case by case over some sixty years: Biemer's 1643–1727 series
shows mutual wills, joint property, and women traders declining as English
law consolidated. Hardenbroeck lived the transition: a registered trader in
her own name before 1664, in law her husband's instrument after.

The closing sentence: the conquest transferred sovereignty on a single day,
but each institution changed at its own rate, immediately (the flag),
never (property titles), or over two generations (women's legal standing),
so the date measures the change of sovereign, not the change of society.
</Solution>

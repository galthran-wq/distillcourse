The New England that shared the 1621 harvest feast with the Wampanoag fought the Pequot within sixteen years and the Wampanoag's own grandchildren within fifty-five; the alliance and the wars are one story, and each side's choices were rational at every step.

The course's argument stands inside the settler-engine module. <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">The covenant lesson</Ref> measured the engine itself: selected, literate family migrants doubling every generation on covenant institutions. What it left unexplained is the room the engine compounded into, because southern New England was Native country in 1620 and English country by 1680, and the change has a mechanism with dates. This lesson supplies it in two parts: a trade equilibrium that held for a generation because it paid both sides, and the two wars that ended it. The route: the trade system drawn, the Pequot War counted in the colonial record's own words, the four datable mechanisms that broke the alliance, King Philip's War measured against every later American war, Virginia's parallel, and the two archives the wars left behind.

## The trade equilibrium: wampum for furs, furs for cloth and metal

Sixteen years separate the harvest feast from the first war, and the natural reading of that interval is a slide from friendship into hostility. The record shows neither friendship nor a slide: the coexistence of the 1620s and 1630s was a working economy, entered deliberately on both sides, and its currency was made of shell.

*Wampum* was beads ground and drilled from whelk and quahog shells on the coasts of Long Island Sound, strung and woven into strings and belts. Among the nations of the interior it carried ceremonial and diplomatic value; a belt of wampum sealed a treaty or condoled a death. Dutch traders at [New Netherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) were the first Europeans to see what that meant commercially: they carried wampum along Native trade routes, exchanged it for beaver pelts, and turned it into everyday money, good for a loaf of bread or a plot of land.

  caption="The currency itself: quahog purple and whelk white, drilled, strung, and woven. A seventeenth-century Algonquian and Iroquois treaty belt, with a loose string of white beads beside it (Peabody Museum, Harvard)."

The circuit had four stations. The coastal nations (Wampanoag, Narragansett, Pequot) made wampum and grew corn; European metal drills multiplied their output. Interior nations trapped beaver, whose felt European hatters paid for. English and Dutch traders supplied the manufactured goods both wanted: cloth, kettles, hoes, knives, and, despite every colony's ban, guns.

The circuit also cleared colonial books. Plymouth serviced its London debts with beaver; Massachusetts made wampum legal tender for small debts from 1637 to 1661. Each party held one corner of a system none could run alone.

  src="./figures/trade-equilibrium.svg"

  caption="The system that worked: four stations, two currencies, and the rivalries (dashed) that ran through it."

The rivalries drawn dashed in the figure are the second half of the equilibrium. Native nations were not a bloc facing the English; they were polities with their own conflicts, and they used the newcomers in them. Massasoit's 1621 treaty, established in <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>, added English muskets to the light side of his balance against the Narragansett. Uncas broke his Mohegan followers away from the Pequot and attached them to the English. The Narragansett weighed the Pequot's offer of a joint war against the English in 1636 and chose the English side instead: Bradford records that the memory of Pequot wrongs made "revenge so sweete" that it "prevailed above all the rest."

<KeyFact>
For two decades Native&ndash;English coexistence was an equilibrium, not a prelude: wampum, furs, corn, cloth, and metal moved through a four-station circuit that paid every party, and Native nations used English trade and alliance as instruments in their own rivalries.
</KeyFact>

## The Pequot War, 1636&ndash;1638: the first breakdown, counted

The first war grew directly out of the trade the previous section drew, in the one place where the circuit's lines crossed: the lower Connecticut valley, where the Pequot controlled the river trade and the tributary wampum coast, and where Dutch and English posts pressed in on the same corridor after 1633.

### The escalation: two dead traders, a punitive raid, a siege

The escalation ran on killings and reprisals, each answering the last. In 1634 Western Niantic tributaries of the Pequot killed John Stone, an English smuggler who had kidnapped Native people on the river; in 1636 traders' rivals killed John Oldham off Block Island. Massachusetts answered with John Endecott's punitive expedition, which burned villages and crops on Block Island and the Pequot coast.

The Pequot answered the raid by besieging Fort Saybrook through the winter and, in April 1637, striking Wethersfield upriver; the raid's nine dead turned reprisal into declared war. In May 1637 Connecticut's General Court declared it, and the coalition of the trade map assembled: about 90 Englishmen under John Mason and John Underhill, marching with roughly three times their number of Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic fighters.

### Mystic, May 26, 1637: about an hour

Before dawn the force surrounded the fortified Pequot town at Mystic, one of the nation's two principal forts, while most of its fighting men were away at the other. The attackers blocked both gates, entered, met hard resistance, and fired the wigwams; the wind did the rest. Pequot who climbed the palisade were shot; those who got over it met the ring of allied fighters outside. The commanders' own counts are the record: Mason reckoned 400 people in the fort of whom "not above five" escaped, Underhill counted six or seven hundred dead. Most were women, children, and old men. English losses were two dead.

William Bradford, writing from the reports that reached Plymouth, set down the colonial record's own words:

> It was a fearfull sight to see them thus frying in the fyer, and the streams of blood quenching the same, and horrible was the stinck & sente ther of; but the victory seemed a sweete sacrifice, and they gave the prays therof to God, who had wrought so wonderfuly for them, thus to inclose their enimise in their hands, and give them so speedy a victory over so proud & insulting an enimie.

Underhill's published account, [*Newes from America*](https://archive.org/details/newesfromamerica00unde) (London, 1638), records both the objection and his answer. The Narragansett allies, he writes, protested the manner of the killing: "mach it, mach it," which he glosses "it is naught, it is naught, because it is too furious, and slays too many men." His own defense: "sometimes the Scripture declareth women and children must perish with their parents"; "we had sufficient light from the word of God for our proceedings."

### Hartford, 1638: a nation outlawed by name

The remainder of the war was pursuit. English and Mohegan forces hunted the scattered Pequot bands through the summer; a final fight in a swamp near present-day Fairfield broke the last large group in July 1637. The chief sachem Sassacus fled west to the Mohawk, who killed him and sent his scalp to Hartford.

The colonists' running boast was that the Pequot "were drove out of their country, and slain by the sword, to the number of fifteen hundred." Hundreds of captives were sold into slavery in the West Indies; the few hundred survivors were divided as tributaries between the Mohegan and the Narragansett.

The Treaty of Hartford (September 1638) then did something rarer than a land cession: it outlawed the Pequot name and language, dissolving the polity on paper as the fire had dissolved its principal town.

<KeyFact>
The Pequot War's ledger, from the victors' own records: 400&ndash;700 killed at Mystic in about an hour, most of them women and children, against two English dead; the survivors enslaved to the Caribbean or distributed as tributaries; and a 1638 treaty that made the nation's own name illegal.
</KeyFact>

## Why the alliance broke: land, demography, sovereignty, succession

From the Mystic fire one would expect the Plymouth&ndash;Wampanoag treaty to be the next casualty; instead it ran another four decades, because the Wampanoag were the Pequot coalition's beneficiaries, not its targets. The mechanisms that eventually broke the alliance are separable, and each one can be dated.

### Land: livestock and deeds

The most litigated grievance was animals. English law let cattle and hogs range free, and English courts put the burden of fencing on whoever wanted crops protected. Native fields, unfenced through centuries of practice, were ruined season after season: a pattern Virginia DeJohn Anderson's study ["King Philip's Herds"](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2947436) traces through the county court records.

The same courts read land tenure one way: land not fenced, plowed, or built on was unowned and available. Debt and pressure turned that reading into deeds, sale by sale through the 1660s, and in 1667 Plymouth planted the town of Swansea beside Sowams, the home territory of Massasoit's own people.

The Narragansett sachem Miantonomo had named the mechanism as early as 1642, in a speech the English officer Lion Gardener wrote down: "these English having gotten our land, they with scythes cut down the grass, and with axes fell the trees; their cows and horses eat the grass, and their hogs spoil our clam banks, and we shall all be starved."

### Demography: the numbers inverted

The second mechanism needs no documents beyond the counts already established. In 1621 about fifty colonists treated with a diminished but populous Wampanoag confederation. By 1675 the English of New England numbered roughly 50,000 to 60,000, doubling every generation per <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref>, while repeated epidemics (the coastal plague of 1616&ndash;19 and the smallpox of 1633&ndash;34 among them) held the Native population of southern New England near 20,000.

The <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">epidemic collapse</Ref> ran continuously under the whole period: every generation the alliance's demographic terms were renegotiated by disease and births before any diplomat spoke.

### Sovereignty: the covenants read two ways

The third mechanism was jurisdictional. Wampanoag sachems, like most in the region, had signed covenants of "submission" with the colonies, and the two parties never read the word the same way: the sachems took the covenants as pacts of protection and mutual obligation; the colonies read them as subjection to English law.

The readings collided on datable occasions. In 1662 Plymouth had Massasoit's heir Wamsutta marched in at gunpoint for questioning about land sales; he sickened and died within days, and his brother Metacom, whom the English called King Philip, succeeded believing him poisoned. In 1671 Plymouth summoned Metacom to Taunton and ordered the surrender of Wampanoag arms; months later it fined him and extracted a written acknowledgment of subjection.

In June 1675 a Plymouth jury of twelve Englishmen, with six Christian Indians assenting, convicted three of Metacom's counselors of murdering John Sassamon: a Harvard-educated Christian Wampanoag found dead under pond ice the previous winter, after warning the English that Metacom was preparing for war. All three were hanged on June 8, two weeks before the war's first shots. An English court had tried Wampanoag men for the death of a Wampanoag man: jurisdiction asserted over the alliance's other party.

### Succession: an alliance that no longer paid

The fourth mechanism is the sum of the first three, seen from the Wampanoag council. For Massasoit in 1621 the alliance bought protection from the Narragansett and a trade partner with goods no one else supplied. By Metacom's accession the beaver were trapped out of southern New England, wampum had inflated into an ordinary commodity and lost its legal-tender status (1661), land was the only asset the English still wanted, and the ally had become a court that summoned, fined, disarmed, and hanged. Both leaders reasoned from the same treaty; the numbers under it had changed.

## King Philip's War, 1675&ndash;1676: the measured case

The war that began at Swansea in the last week of June 1675 is the module's hardest number, and this section counts it.

### Fourteen months, three phases

Metacom's coalition was assembled less by his diplomacy than by English escalation. Through the summer of 1675 his Wampanoag bands struck Plymouth towns and eluded pursuit; colonial demands that neutral nations surrender their weapons pushed the Nipmuc and most of the upper Connecticut valley nations into the war by autumn, and towns at the region's edge burned: Deerfield, Hadley, Brookfield.

In December 1675 the United Colonies preempted the still-neutral Narragansett: militia stormed and torched the fortified Narragansett refuge in the Great Swamp Fight, a second Mystic that killed as many as 1,000 men, women, and children and added the survivors to the enemy. Between February and April 1676 Native forces devastated towns within striking distance of Boston, Lancaster and Medfield among them.

The tide turned in spring: the colonies finally took Native allies and Native tactics seriously, food ran out in a countryside no one could plant, and fighters surrendered, fled, or changed sides through the summer. In August 1676 a Native soldier serving with Benjamin Church's mixed company shot Metacom at Mount Hope. His head stood on a pole over Plymouth for two decades.

### The praying towns: loyal, used, and interned anyway

One population belonged to both sides of the war's ledger. By the missionary administrator Daniel Gookin's 1674 census, about 1,100 Christian Indians lived in fourteen "praying towns" under Massachusetts jurisdiction. Praying Indians fought on both sides; those who served the English, as scouts, soldiers, and the spies whose intelligence shaped the spring campaign, were indispensable to the victory. Indispensable service ordinarily buys protection; Massachusetts interned its allies anyway: in October 1675 the colony ordered the praying towns emptied and some 500 people confined to Deer Island in Boston harbor, without adequate food or shelter, through the winter. A large fraction, by most accounts about half, died of cold and starvation before the survivors were released in 1676.

### The count

The figure holds the itemized tallies, from the syntheses named in the references; what they mean is two different kinds of catastrophe. On the English side: 600 to 1,000 dead of roughly 55,000 colonists, more than half of the region's about 90 towns attacked, and a line of settlement pushed back toward the coast that took a generation to recover. On the Native side the same fourteen months were not a disaster but an ending: in Nathaniel Philbrick's accounting, southern New England's Native population fell by 60 to 80 percent, to battle, disease, famine, enslavement, and flight.

The enslavement was commerce, entered in account books. The enslaved included Metacom's nine-year-old son, shipped to the West Indies after Boston's ministers debated executing him; shiploads of other captives followed, and the Barbados assembly refused further imports for fear they would foment rebellion.

Jill Lepore's one-sentence verdict in [*The Name of War*](https://archive.org/details/nameofwarkingphi0000lepo) states the proportional fact: the war was, "in proportion to population, the bloodiest in American history."

The result closes the mechanism this lesson set out to supply, and one distinction from <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the first lesson</Ref> must survive the closing. Disease did most of the demographic work of emptying the land, and disease was not policy. The wars were policy: declared, financed, fought, and concluded by treaty, with the enslavement of captives entered in the account books. After 1676 the two mechanisms had converged on one outcome: no Native polity could contest southern New England, and the settler engine's demographic runway ran clear.

## Virginia ran the same sequence

New England's arc was not regional peculiarity; the other English society ran the same sequence on its own dates.

<Figure
  src="./figures/parallel-sequence.svg"

Virginia's equilibrium was the Powhatan trade that kept Jamestown alive, sealed by the Rolfe-Pocahontas marriage peace of 1614. Its breakdown came in two blows from the other direction. In March 1622 Opechancanough's coordinated attack killed 347 colonists, a third of the colony, in a single day. His second strike in 1644 killed as many again, but against a colony grown many times larger the proportional blow was a fraction of 1622's: the demographic inversion New England measured, running on Virginia's clock.

The English answer after 1622 was announced as policy, in Governor Francis Wyatt's phrase, "expulsion of the savages to gain the free range of the country," and the treaty of 1646 reduced the Powhatan remnant to tributary status behind a fixed boundary. Thirty years later, frontier killings and the colonists' demand for a general Indian war set off <Ref lesson="colonial-societies">Bacon's Rebellion</Ref>. The pattern was continental: equilibrium, escalation, war, and a settlement dictated by the demographic winner.

## Two archives: what each preserves, what each cannot

The wars are knowable because they left records, and the records come in two unequal bodies. This section reads one document from the larger archive with the course's standing method, then states what the smaller archive holds.

### The colonial archive's best-seller: Rowlandson, 1682

Mary Rowlandson, a minister's wife, was taken when Native forces burned Lancaster in February 1676, in the winter campaign counted above, and held for eleven weeks, moving with her captors through twenty "removes" before her ransom that May. Her account, [*The Sovereignty and Goodness of God*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/851) (Cambridge, 1682), went through four printings in its first year, in Boston and London, and is commonly called the first American best-seller. It did not reach print raw: it appeared with an anonymous preface signed "Per Amicum," attributed to Increase Mather, and the Mather circle's framing, captivity as God's affliction and deliverance of a covenant people, governs how the book asks to be read. Two passages carry the method.

On food, in the third week of captivity, marching with a wartime economy that was itself starving:

> The first week of my being among them I hardly ate any thing; the second week I found my stomach grow very faint for want of something; and yet it was very hard to get down their filthy trash; but the third week &hellip; they were sweet and savory to my taste.

On her ransom, before the assembled sachems:

> Then they bade me stand up, and said they were the General Court. They bid me speak what I thought he would give. &hellip; Yet at a venture I said "Twenty pounds," yet desired them to take less.

Read it with the records / argues / omits discipline the course has applied since <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the first lesson</Ref>. The narrative **records** the famine that was defeating both sides, and the structure of Native politics seen from inside: her mistress Weetamoo, the female sachem of Pocasset, "a severe and proud dame" whose daily dress and wampum-work Rowlandson describes as rank, deliberately performed. It also records a working captive economy, in which Rowlandson knitted and sewed for pay, and a ransom negotiation conducted by a council that named itself with the English colonies' own institutional vocabulary.

It **argues** providence: every event is God's instruction to one soul and one people, and the enemy appears as God's scourge rather than as a polity with reasons.

It **omits** the war's causes (the ladder of the previous section entirely) and her captors' own losses; their hunger appears as evidence of God's power, not as suffering. The £20 that freed her was raised by public subscription among the women of Boston.

### The Native record, and one man in both archives

The other archive exists, in forms the colonial one does not use. Oral tradition preserved the war's causes and courses in Wampanoag, Narragansett, and Nipmuc communities that survived it. The material record, wampum belts, the archaeology of burned towns and the Great Swamp, holds what no one wrote. And a thin but direct documentary trail survives inside colonial custody: petitions from the praying towns, testimony of Native veterans and defendants filtered through English courts, and wartime letters in Native hands. What this archive cannot preserve is what the war destroyed: its keepers were killed, enslaved to the Caribbean, or dispersed, and its written fraction survives mostly on colonial terms, as pleas and depositions.

One documented life joins the two bodies. James Printer, a Nipmuc from the praying town of Hassanamesit, apprenticed at the Cambridge press and helped set the type of Eliot's Algonquian Bible; during the war he was with Metacom's forces and drafted their ransom letters in the Rowlandson negotiation; he returned under a 1676 amnesty; and back at the Cambridge press he set type for the 1682 edition of Rowlandson's narrative. The first American best-seller about the war passed through the hands of a man who had spent the war on the other side of it.

What is now established: the alliance of 1621 and the war of 1675 were one story of rational choices under shifting power; the Pequot War and King Philip's War, counted from both records, ended Native political control of southern New England at a proportional cost no later American war has matched; and the region the settler engine would fill was cleared by policy as well as by disease. <Ref lesson="colonial-societies">The next lesson</Ref> sets the other English society, the tobacco Chesapeake, beside New England and traces the legal turn to hereditary slavery: the same engine, on different terms.

(a) Coastal epidemic 1616&ndash;19; Mayflower Compact 1620; Plymouth&ndash;Wampanoag treaty 1621; Mystic massacre 1637; Treaty of Hartford 1638; Wamsutta's death 1662; Taunton arms surrender 1671; Sassamon hangings June 1675; Great Swamp Fight December 1675; Metacom killed August 1676. Durations: the trade equilibrium held about fifteen years, from the 1621 treaty to the escalation of 1636; the alliance itself ran fifty-four years, 1621 to the attack on Swansea in June 1675.

(b) (i) Both. The Great Swamp assault repeated Mystic's method: surround a palisaded refuge, fire it, kill those who fled the flames; the cue that separates them is only date and nation (Pequot 1637, Narragansett 1675). (ii) The 1621 treaty only: three of its six clauses bind both sides in the same words, and reciprocity is written only when neither party can dictate. (iii) Hartford only: it outlaws the Pequot name and language and distributes the survivors as tributaries, and no clause binds the English to anything; an instrument of that shape is itself evidence of the balance of power at signing, independent of any casualty count. (iv) Both. At Mystic the English ring was inside a larger ring of Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic fighters; in 1676 Native scouts, soldiers, and spies carried the spring campaign, and a Native soldier killed Metacom. The cue is coalition rosters, not racial lines: both wars were wars between coalitions.

<Exercise>
Apply the records / argues / omits method to Bradford's Mystic passage as quoted in this lesson, treating it as a primary source for the event and for its author's community.

<Solution>
Records: the mechanics and scale of the killing: the fort fired, death mostly by burning rather than the sword, "about 400" destroyed in Bradford's count, and the sensory particulars ("the streams of blood quenching the same," the stench) that establish the writer knew what a mass killing by fire was. Also the immediate response of the English force: a victory celebration, not an apology.

Argues: providence: "the victory seemed a sweete sacrifice, and they gave the prays therof to God." The passage argues that the outcome was God's work against "so proud & insulting an enimie," which converts the massacre into evidence of divine favor and forecloses the question of proportionality that Underhill's own account records the Narragansett raising ("it is too furious, and slays too many men").

Omits: the composition of the dead: Bradford does not state that the fort held mostly women, children, and old men, which the commanders' accounts and the location of the fighting men at the second fort establish. It also omits the allies' objection, the war's origins in the trade system, and any Pequot perspective at all. The omissions are as informative as the record: a community that needed no justification recorded none.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Run the sourcing drill on *The Sovereignty and Goodness of God* metadata-first. The metadata: written by a returned captive, a minister's wife; printed in 1682, six years after the war, at Cambridge, the colony's own press; prefaced anonymously by a leading Boston minister; addressed "especially to her dear Children and Relations" and "for the benefit of the Afflicted."

(a) Prediction, from the metadata alone: name two things a source of this provenance should record reliably and two it should omit or reframe, each with the reason drawn from the metadata, not from the text. (b) Check the predictions against the lesson's records / argues / omits reading. (c) Use: state one question about the war the book is strong evidence for and one it cannot answer, and how two further provenance facts &mdash; the preface attributed to Increase Mather, and the 1682 edition typeset by James Printer, a Nipmuc veteran of the Wampanoag side &mdash; change what the book can be used to establish.

(a) Model prediction. Reliable: the physical and economic texture of captivity (food, work, payment, the route of the marches), because a survivor's memory of daily survival carries no doctrinal load and her named audience of relations could check its outline; and the visible structure of her captors' society (who commanded, who paid, who negotiated), because a captive's safety depended on reading it correctly. Omitted or reframed: the war's causes, because a colonial press printing a minister's wife six years after the war has no interest in rehearsing colonial provocations; and her captors' suffering as suffering, because the imprint, the ministerial preface, and the address "to the Afflicted" all predict a providential frame, affliction and deliverance, before a page is read.

(b) The predictions match the lesson's reading: the book records famine, the captive economy, and Weetamoo's performed rank; argues providence; omits the causes entirely. One prediction fails instructively: the narrative does record the captors' hunger, but converted into evidence of God's power to sustain His scourge; recorded and reframed at once, which is subtler than omission.

(c) Use. Strong evidence for: the famine that was defeating both sides in the winter of 1676, the working economy inside a wartime camp, and a Native council conducting a ransom negotiation with the colonies' own institutional vocabulary, facts the book preserves against or beside its own argument. Cannot answer: why the war came or what the Wampanoag council intended; those silences are structural, so they can never be cited as evidence of absence. The Mather preface means the providential argument must be weighed as the Boston clerical establishment's publication project, not the captive's spontaneous reading: the "argues" layer has at least two authors, while the recorded texture, carrying no doctrinal load, is the layer least likely to be shaped. The Printer fact means the two archives are physically entangled: the colonial best-seller was set in type by a Native veteran who had drafted the captors' ransom letters that appear inside the story, which documents Native literacy and agency inside colonial print and warns against treating the archives as sealed containers. Verdict: read it as a co-produced object of the postwar decade; trust its texture, interrogate its argument, and never treat its silences as facts about the war.

<Exercise>
Four questions a historian of the war might ask: (i) which towns were attacked, and when; (ii) what Metacom's council believed the war was for; (iii) what people in a Native encampment ate in the starving winter of 1676; (iv) how many Narragansett died at the Great Swamp. For each, state which archive can establish it (the colonial record, the Native record, both, or neither fully), with what confidence, and what corroboration between the two would look like.

<Solution>
(i) The colonial record, with high confidence: dated town, militia, and narrative records; Foster's 1677 map already prints the attacked towns as a keyed list. Oral tradition can corroborate the sequence, and it adds what the colonial ledger omits: the attacks on Native communities that the same months contained.

(ii) The Native record, and only partially. No minutes of Metacom's council exist; the causes survive in oral tradition and, filtered, in what Native negotiators and defendants said to English interlocutors. Miantonomo's 1642 speech shows the filter's shape: the grievance survives because an English officer wrote it down, selected and translated by the adversary. Corroboration: the same grievances surfacing independently in deeds disputes, court cases, and negotiation records that were kept for other purposes.

(iii) Both, from opposite directions, and with high confidence precisely because the fact carries no argumentative load for either archive: Rowlandson records the diet in detail because she ate it, one of the rare places the colonial archive documents Native wartime experience from inside, and the archaeology of camp sites corroborates independently.

(iv) Neither fully. The only counts are the attackers', made at night, in a burning fort, by the side with reasons to magnify the victory; the "as many as 1,000" is a claim, not an enumeration. Archaeology can bound the settlement's size but cannot count its dead. The general rule the four cases teach: each archive is strongest where it is least interested, and a number recorded only by the party that produced the dead is an estimate carrying that provenance forever.

<Exercise>
Run the worked example's computation for the Native side alone: about 20,000 people in southern New England in 1675; about 2,000 killed in the fighting and 3,000 dead of disease and starvation; about 1,000 enslaved and shipped out and 2,000 fled. Compute the share killed, the total share lost to the region, and check the result against the 60&ndash;80 percent decline the syntheses report. Then state at least three reasons the Native figures carry wider uncertainty than the English ones.

<Solution>
Share killed: $(2{,}000 + 3{,}000) / 20{,}000 = 25\%$ dead in fourteen months, against roughly $1.5\%$ of the English population ($800/55{,}000$).

Total loss to the region: $(5{,}000 + 1{,}000 + 2{,}000) / 20{,}000 = 40\%$ directly counted. The reported 60&ndash;80 percent decline is larger than the counted 40 percent, which signals that the enumerated categories are incomplete: continued flight and deaths after 1676, uncounted famine and disease mortality, and the base population's own uncertainty absorb the difference. If the true base were 15,000 rather than 20,000, the counted losses alone reach 53 percent.

Why the uncertainty is wider: (1) no censuses: the English population is reconstructed from town, militia, and tax records; Native population estimates rest on colonial guesses and back-projection. (2) The records of Native losses were kept by the enemy, and mostly for its own purposes (captives sold, bounties paid); deaths from starvation and disease in dispersed communities were never recorded by anyone. (3) The categories blur: "fled," "died," and "enslaved" were often indistinguishable to the record keepers, and people counted as fled may have died uncounted. (4) The base year matters and is itself uncertain, since epidemics were still cutting the population in the decades before 1675. The direction of the bias is consistent: every gap hides Native losses, so the true toll is at least the counted one.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Rank the four mechanisms of the alliance's breakdown (land, demography, sovereignty, succession) as necessary, enabling, or precipitating causes of the war that began in June 1675. Defend each assignment with one dated fact from the lesson, name the single event that best qualifies as the precipitant, and state what the ranking implies about whether abler diplomacy on either side could have prevented the war.

<Solution>
Demography is the necessary cause: without the inversion to roughly 3-to-1 by 1675 (50,000&ndash;60,000 English against about 20,000 Native people), the English could neither absorb land deed by deed nor enforce jurisdiction, and every other mechanism presupposes that weight. Land is enabling: the livestock cases and deed sales of the 1660s, and Swansea planted beside Sowams in 1667, converted the demographic weight into a daily, litigated grievance, but land loss by itself set no date. Sovereignty is enabling shading into precipitating: the dated series (Wamsutta 1662, Taunton 1671) established that the English would read the alliance as subjection, and its final step supplied the trigger. Succession is an amplifier rather than an independent cause: Wamsutta's death in English custody (1662) attached a grievance to the office Metacom inherited, changing who decided and with what memory, not the balance the decision weighed.

Two rungs resist single labels, which is evidence the mechanisms are separable in analysis but not in the record: Wamsutta 1662 is sovereignty in form (a sachem compelled at gunpoint) and succession in consequence, and the livestock cases are demography expressed as county-court paperwork, since doubling households meant doubling free-ranging animals.

The precipitant is the Sassamon verdict and the hangings of June 8, 1675, two weeks before Swansea: it is the one event whose removal plausibly moves the war's date, which is the working test of a precipitating cause. The implication: diplomacy could have shifted the timing (a different verdict, no Taunton humiliation), but the necessary and enabling causes were compounding every year, so each side's position at the negotiating table was worse each season. That is why the lesson can call Massasoit's alliance and Metacom's war equally rational, and why prevention would have required changing the trend, not the trigger.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
The 1621 treaty established in <Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the covenant lesson</Ref> contained six clauses, including mutual restitution (clause 3) and mutual defense (clause 4). Using the four mechanisms of this lesson, explain why the same treaty was a rational instrument for Massasoit in 1621 and no longer one for Metacom in 1675. Anchor each step in a number or a dated event.

<Solution>
1621: the epidemics of 1616&ndash;19 had devastated the coastal Wampanoag while sparing the inland Narragansett, who pressed on Massasoit; about fifty armed English with muskets and cannon were a real increment to the weaker side of that balance (the demographic and rivalry facts of the covenant lesson). Clause 4's mutual defense was therefore worth its price, and the trade circuit supplied goods (metal, cloth) obtainable nowhere else. The treaty bought protection and trade at the cost of coexistence with a tiny neighbor.

1675: every term of that computation had reversed. Demography: the English numbered 50,000&ndash;60,000 against roughly 20,000 Native people in southern New England, so the "protector" was now the region's dominant power and the Narragansett threat was obsolete. Land: the deeds and livestock cases of the 1660s, and Swansea (1667) beside Sowams, converted the coexistence cost from tolerable to existential. Sovereignty: Taunton (1671) and the Sassamon trial (June 1675) showed that the English read the alliance as subjection, so the treaty no longer secured Wampanoag autonomy; it dissolved it. Succession: Wamsutta's death in English custody (1662) attached a personal grievance to the office Metacom held. The alliance's benefits (protection, trade) had gone to zero while its costs (land, jurisdiction) compounded; the same reasoning that signed the treaty in 1621 counseled war in 1675.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Construct the two-step argument that the praying towns falsify both simple stories of the war ("faithful allies rewarded" and "race war of all Natives against all English"), using the documented facts of this lesson: Gookin's 1674 census, service on both sides, the October 1675 Deer Island order, and the deaths there.

<Solution>
Against "race war": the war's lines were political, not racial. About 1,100 Christian Indians lived in fourteen praying towns in 1674, and praying Indians fought on both sides; Native scouts and soldiers serving the English were decisive in the spring 1676 campaign, and Metacom was killed by a Native soldier in Church's company. Uncas's Mohegan and the Pequot remnant also fought for the colonies. A war in which Native fighters are indispensable to both armies is a war between coalitions, not races.

Against "faithful allies rewarded": the English treated the praying Indians racially even while depending on them. In October 1675 Massachusetts emptied the praying towns and interned some 500 people on Deer Island through the winter without adequate food or shelter, and about half died: a punishment inflicted on the colonies' own allies and converts for being Native. The praying towns thus break both narratives at once: their service breaks the race-war story, and their internment breaks the loyalty-rewarded story. What the documents support instead is the lesson's frame: polities and individuals choosing under pressure, and a colonial society that, at maximum fear, sorted by ancestry rather than allegiance.
</Solution>

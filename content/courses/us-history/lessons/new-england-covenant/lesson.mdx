In November 1620, 41 men aboard a merchant ship off Cape Cod signed a one-paragraph agreement to 'covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil body politic' — the first instrument of self-government written in English America, and the template the region then reproduced at every scale.

The course's argument stands at its hinge. <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">The contact lesson</Ref> established that epidemics emptied the land; <Ref lesson="european-empires">the empires lesson</Ref> established that Spain's rule, France's exchange, and the Dutch company's commerce each failed by design to produce a self-reproducing settler society, leaving the English colonies' compounding population as the anomaly to explain. This lesson supplies the anomaly's engine in its founding case: a selected, literate, family-based migration that wrote self-government into its founding documents. The route: the Compact read as text, the Wampanoag treaty of 1621 read from both sides, covenant as an institutional technology reproduced from congregation to colony, the demography measured, and the literacy statutes that made the region's near-universal male reading a matter of law.

## The Compact: consent creating authority, dated November 11, 1620

The document in the opening sentence was not a theory. It was a practical fix for a legal emergency, and its date, author-count, and occasion are all on record.

### Why it was written: a ship outside its patent

The *Mayflower* carried 102 passengers under a patent to settle "the Northern parts of Virginia," near the mouth of the Hudson. Storms and shoals put the ship instead off Cape Cod, outside any government's jurisdiction. About half the passengers were religious Separatists from the Leiden exile congregation; the rest, whom the Separatists called "strangers," were recruited laborers and craftsmen with no stake in the church.

William Bradford, later the colony's governor and historian, records what happened next: the combination was "occasioned partly by the discontented & mutinous speeches that some of the strangers amongst them had let fall from them in the ship — That when they came a shore they would use their owne libertie; for none had power to command them, the patente they had being for Virginia, and not for New-england." The argument was legally sound. The colony's leaders answered it not by force but by a signing: an agreement that, Bradford writes, "might be as firme as any patent, and in some respects more sure."

### The text, whole

The agreement fits in one paragraph. This is the earliest surviving text, from Nathaniel Morton's 1669 reprint; the original manuscript disappeared in the seventeenth century.

> IN the Name of God, Amen. We whose Names are under-written, the Loyal Subjects of our dread Soveraign Lord King James … Having undertaken for the glory of God, and advancement of the Christian Faith, and the Honour of our King and Countrey, a Voyage to plant the first Colony in the Northern parts of Virginia; Do by these Presents, solemnly and mutually, in the presence of God and one another, Covenant and Combine our selves together into a Civil Body Politick, for our better ordering and preservation, and furtherance of the ends aforesaid: and by virtue hereof do enact, constitute, and frame, such just and equal Laws, Ordinances, Acts, Constitutions and Officers, from time to time, as shall be thought most meet and convenient for the general good of the Colony; unto which we promise all due submission and obedience. In witness whereof we have hereunto subscribed our Names at Cape Cod, the eleventh of November … Anno Dom. 1620.

Three phrases carry the whole instrument. **Who acts:** the signers themselves, "solemnly and mutually, in the presence of God and one another": each man binding himself to every other, with no authority above them invoked except as witness. **What is created:** a "Civil Body Politick," a government, brought into being by the agreement itself. **What it may do:** "enact, constitute, and frame such just and equal Laws … unto which we promise all due submission and obedience": a legislature and, in the same breath, the promised obedience that makes law law.

### What it is and is not

41 of the 65 men and boys aboard signed: the free adult men, Separatist and stranger alike. The document contains no institutions, no rights, no procedures; the signers elected John Carver governor immediately after, but nothing in the text requires a governor at all. It is not a constitution, and it did not found American democracy. The claim this course makes for it is narrower and stronger: it is the first rung of a ladder, and the ladder is the argument of this lesson.

The signers did not call it the Mayflower Compact. Plymouth's records call it "the combination," and the editor Donald Lutz notes that the name "Mayflower Compact" first appears in 1793, in a New York reprint. What contemporaries saw was a familiar form put to a new use: a *covenant*, the mutual promise by which dissenting Protestants formed churches, here forming a government. The reason, in Lutz's analysis: New England's settlers were "middling sort" people without the ready-made gentry that gave Virginia its natural governing class, and formal agreement signed by all was the legitimacy they could manufacture on the spot.

<KeyFact>
The Compact is a dated, practical answer to a concrete problem: with the patent void and the "strangers" claiming no government could bind them, 41 signers created authority by mutual consent on November 11, 1620: not a theory but a fix.
</KeyFact>

The first test of the new body politic was mortality. Within three months, Bradford records, "halfe of their company dyed," of scurvy and exposure in the winter of 1620–21. The colony that met the Wampanoag in spring numbered about fifty.

## The alliance of 1621: a treaty, a feast, and a holiday

The Compact ordered relations among the colonists; the colony's survival depended on relations with the nations around it. What followed in March 1621 was diplomacy between two parties who each had exact reasons to want it.

### Massasoit's position: the epidemic arithmetic

Plymouth stood on the cleared fields of Patuxet, a town emptied by the epidemics of 1616–1619: the same <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">epidemic collapse</Ref> the course has already measured, here at village scale. The coastal outbreaks had devastated the Wampanoag confederation while largely sparing their inland rivals the Narragansett, who now pressed on a weakened Massasoit, the Wampanoag's principal sachem. An alliance with the armed newcomers was rational power politics: their muskets and mounted cannon added weight to the lighter side of a regional balance. The treaty that resulted was not a gift to starving colonists; it was a calculated instrument on both sides.

The interpreter who made it possible has a documented and astonishing biography that fits in three sentences. Tisquantum, called Squanto, was a Patuxet man kidnapped in 1614 by an English captain, Thomas Hunt, who "thought to sell them for slaves in Spaine," in Bradford's words. He escaped to London, lived in the household of a merchant, John Slany, sailed to Newfoundland in English employ, and reached home with the explorer Thomas Dermer in 1619 to find Patuxet dead to the last inhabitant. He attached himself to Plymouth as translator and agent, pursued his own standing between the two sides until his death in 1622, and Bradford, who distrusted his maneuvering, still called him "a spetiall instrument sent of God for their good beyond their expectation."

### The six terms, read for reciprocity

On March 22, 1621, after an English-speaking Abenaki named Samoset made the introduction, Massasoit appeared above Plymouth with a train of sixty men and concluded an agreement. Bradford, writing in the 1640s, sets out its terms as a numbered list and notes it "hath now continued this 24. years."

### The feast on the record

The treaty's first autumn produced the event every American schoolchild inherits, and its entire contemporary documentation is one paragraph. Edward Winslow, in a letter of December 11, 1621, printed in London in 1622 in [*Mourt's Relation*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66359):

> Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling, that so we might after a special manner rejoice together after we had gathered the fruit of our labors … At which time, amongst other recreations, we exercised our arms, many of the Indians coming amongst us, and among the rest their greatest king Massasoit, with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted, and they went out and killed five deer, which they brought to the plantation and bestowed on our governor, and upon the captain and others.

That is the whole record: about fifty colonists, some ninety of Massasoit's men, three days, five deer, fowl, and a militia drill. Winslow does not call it a thanksgiving; Bradford's history mentions the season's "great store of wild Turkies" and plenty but not the gathering. Three facts with three dates, kept separate: a treaty (March 1621), a harvest feast (autumn 1621), and a national holiday, proclaimed by Abraham Lincoln in 1863, in the middle of the war a later lesson reaches. Nothing in the 1621 record is a pageant, and nothing in it needs debunking; the feast was the alliance working, one autumn into a peace that held.

### The 54-year arithmetic

The peace outlasted every man who made it. Carver died in 1621, Squanto in 1622, Bradford in 1657, Massasoit around 1661; the treaty held through them all, until Massasoit's son Metacom, whom the English called King Philip, went to war in June 1675, 54 years after the signing. A treaty kept for two generations, and a war that then killed a larger share of the population than any later American war, are one story, and <Ref lesson="natives-and-english">the next lesson</Ref> tells it from both sides.

<KeyFact>
The 1621 Plymouth–Wampanoag agreement was a real diplomatic instrument (six clauses, mutual defense, mutual restitution), rational for both parties after the epidemics of 1616–19, and it held some 54 years, longer than any of its signers lived.
</KeyFact>

## Covenant as an institutional technology

The Compact was not an isolated improvisation. It applied a form its authors already knew, the church covenant, and the decade after 1620 shows the same form founding congregations, towns, and colonies. This section follows the mechanism up the scale.

### A church made by agreement

A dissenting Protestant congregation was created not by a bishop's license but by explicit mutual promise. The whole founding document of the Salem church (1629), perhaps the briefest covenant in American history, reads:

> We Covenant with the Lord and one with an other; and doe bynd our selves in the presence of God, to walke together in all his waies, according as he is pleased to reveale himselfe unto us in his Blessed word of truth.

One sentence creates an institution: named parties, a mutual bond, witnessed, with a rule of conduct. The Charlestown-Boston church covenanted the same way in July 1630, within weeks of its members' landing. Set the Salem sentence beside the Compact and the shared skeleton is visible: the Compact is a church covenant with "Civil Body Politick" in the place of "church." The settlers carried, in their religious practice, a portable technology for founding institutions by consent, and they used it for everything.

### The Great Migration and Winthrop's conditional

The form scaled with the migration. Between 1630 and 1642 some 21,000 English settlers crossed to Massachusetts Bay and its offshoots: the *Great Migration*, led by John Winthrop, a Suffolk lawyer elected governor before the fleet sailed. Aboard the *Arbella* in 1630 Winthrop delivered the lay sermon ["A Model of Christian Charity"](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/winthmod.html), whose most quoted sentence is usually cut off half way. In period spelling, with the sentence that follows it:

> For wee must consider that wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us. Soe that if wee shall deale falsely with our God in this worke wee haue undertaken, and soe cause him to withdrawe his present help from us, wee shall be made a story and a by-word through the world.

Read whole, the passage is not a boast but a conditional warning inside covenant logic: Winthrop has just told the settlers "We are entered into Covenant with Him for this worke," and a covenant has penalty clauses. Visibility is stated as exposure, not glory: if the colony fails its terms, the watching world sees the failure. The sermon is the covenant form applied to an entire society, penalties included.

<Aside>
The settlers read events as covenant enforcement, and their readings are primary evidence of their self-understanding. Winthrop's own pen, in a 1634 letter to Sir Nathaniel Rich, drew the conclusion from the epidemics: "For the natives, they are near all dead of the smallpox, so the Lord hath cleared our title to what we possess." The sentence does double duty for this course: it corroborates the demographic fact established in <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the contact lesson</Ref>, and it documents how the beneficiaries explained that fact to themselves: as providence, and as title.
</Aside>

### The town: land to a congregation, rule by meeting

The unit that reproduced the covenant across the landscape was the town. Massachusetts did not sell land to individuals at the frontier; the General Court granted a township to a group of families, often a congregation with its minister, who covenanted with each other, laid out a meetinghouse, home lots, and common fields, admitted or refused new members, and governed themselves by general meeting of the householders. Dedham's covenant of 1636 binds its signers to "one truth," mutual arbitration of disputes, and payment of "his share in all such … charges," with every future inhabitant required to sign; Exercise 1 puts its full text under the same reading as the Compact.

</KeyFact>

## From town to colony: the ladder and its boundaries

Above the towns stood colony governments, and the documentary record shows the lower rungs generating the upper ones. This section states the compounding claim and, in the same breath, the exclusions that bounded it.

### The General Court and the documents that cite each other

Massachusetts Bay arrived with a corporate charter that happened to travel with the colonists, which let a trading company's general court of shareholders become a legislature. In 1631 the General Court restricted freemanship, the franchise, to church members; in 1634 the towns began sending elected deputies, turning the body representative; in 1641 it adopted the Body of Liberties, a hundred-article code of rights proposed by Nathaniel Ward. Lutz's edition counts what that document seeded: of the 26 specific rights in the later federal Bill of Rights, at most seven trace to Magna Carta and the English acts, while seven others originate in the Massachusetts Body of Liberties: a colonial statute of 1641.

The rungs cite one another explicitly. Plymouth's Pilgrim Code of Law (1636), a candidate for the first true written constitution of the modern world, opens by reciting its own legal foundations: the committee met "having read the combination made at Cape Cod the 11th of November 1620 … as also our letters patents." A functioning legal code, sixteen years on, names the shipboard signing as its constitutional basis. In 1639 the river towns of Windsor, Hartford, and Wethersfield, each already covenanted internally, covenanted with each other in the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, creating a two-level government of towns and General Court: the compounding move again, one level up.

The ladder is this course's spine figure, and the claim it draws is specific: not that the Compact caused the Constitution, but that a century and a half of practice in founding governments by written mutual consent ran continuously from these documents to the state constitutions of 1776 and the convention of 1787. Lutz's summary of the timing: the foundations were operative before Locke published, and "in temporal terms, it makes more sense to call Locke an American than it does to call America Lockean." When the settlers of the Piscataqua wrote their combination in 1641, Locke was nine years old. Two later lessons re-certify the ladder from outside: <Ref lesson="tocqueville-democracy">Tocqueville</Ref>, observing in 1831, wrote that "In New England townships were completely and definitively constituted as early as 1650," and <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the founding module</Ref> redraws this figure with the top rungs filled in.

### The boundaries: whom the covenant excluded

The same records that show the institutions show their limits, and the two belong in one section. The franchise followed church membership, not residence: Massachusetts was a covenant community, not a democracy, and the excluded included most adult men in some towns. Dissent from the covenant's terms was answered by expulsion. Roger Williams, banished by the General Court in 1635 for denying its authority over conscience, founded Providence in 1636 on a compact that confined government "only in civil things." Anne Hutchinson, tried in 1637 for holding unauthorized religious meetings and outarguing her judges from scripture, was banished in 1638. Between 1659 and 1661 Massachusetts hanged four Quakers, Mary Dyer among them, for returning from banishment. And the covenant's reach stopped entirely at the town line: Native people were parties to treaties, like Massasoit's, but never to covenants, a boundary whose consequences <Ref lesson="natives-and-english">the next lesson</Ref> counts in war deaths.

<KeyFact>
Self-government compounded from the bottom up, compact to congregation to town to General Court with each document citing the last, and it compounded inside hard boundaries: a church-member franchise, banishment for dissenters, execution for returning Quakers, and no covenant with Native nations at all. The thesis is selection and compounding, not virtue.
</KeyFact>

## The demography, measured

The institutions were run by a population unlike any other colonial society's, and the difference is measurable. The measurements below are this lesson's to establish; <Ref lesson="colonial-societies">a later lesson</Ref> deploys them against the Chesapeake's.

### Families, longevity, doubling

Three numbers define the migration. **Who came:** the Great Migration moved households, not single laborers: men outnumbered women only about three to two, against roughly six to one among migrants to 1630s Virginia, and ships' lists show artisans, farmers, and their children crossing together. **How long they lived:** the cold, dispersed, well-fed settlements escaped both the Chesapeake's malaria and Europe's crowd diseases; in Philip Greven's reconstruction of Andover from parish and probate records, first-generation men died at an average age of 71; a man of 20 could expect to see 70. **How many children survived:** six to eight per completed family, in a world where much of Europe buried half.

Compounding did the rest. The migration itself stopped in 1642, when England's civil war gave Puritans reasons to stay home; net migration to New England was near zero for the following two generations. Population growth after 1642 was therefore almost purely births over deaths, and the totals are on record: about 21,000 immigrants had arrived by 1642, and the census-era reconstructions count about 91,000 New England colonists by 1700.

<Example title="the doubling time of New England">
Compute the growth rate implied by the two endpoints, with immigration shut.

Growth factor, 1642 to 1700: $91{,}000 / 21{,}000 \approx 4.33$.

Number of doublings: $\log_2 4.33 = \ln 4.33 / \ln 2 = 1.466 / 0.693 \approx 2.1$.

Doubling time: $58 \text{ years} / 2.1 \approx 27$ years: one human generation.

Every generation, twice the people: twice the households, twice the town covenants, twice the men signing and taxing and meeting. This is the same compounding <Ref lesson="european-empires">Franklin priced in 1751</Ref> (his band was 20 to 25 years, for the colonies as a whole, immigration included); New England reached it first, and reached it on births alone.
</Example>

Fischer's larger claim, that this founding population fixed a durable regional culture of "ordered liberty," is a synthesis historians dispute in its strong form; the migration figures and the family composition are the parts the county studies corroborate, and they are all this lesson's argument needs.
</Aside>

About 21,000 selected, family-based migrants became about 91,000 colonists by 1700 with immigration shut after 1642: a population doubling every generation on births alone, and with it every covenant institution the previous sections built.
</KeyFact>

## Literacy engineered: two statutes, a college, a press

Selection and compounding explain the population's size; its most consequential trait was deliberately manufactured. A society organized around reading scripture legislated reading itself, and the statutes survive with their operative clauses.

### The laws of 1642 and 1647

The 1642 Massachusetts law made literacy a household duty enforceable by the selectmen: parents and masters were required to ensure that children could "read & understand the principles of religion and the capital laws of this country," on pain of fines and, in the extreme, removal of the child. The 1647 law, known from its preamble as the [Old Deluder Satan Act](https://archive.org/details/recordsgovernor00courgoog/page/203), shifted the duty from households to towns, with thresholds and a penalty:

> It being one chief project of that old deluder, Satan, to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures … It is therefore ordered, that every township in this jurisdiction, after the Lord hath increased them to the number of fifty householders, shall then forthwith appoint one within their town to teach all such children as shall resort to him to write and read … and it is further ordered, that where any town shall increase to the number of one hundred families or householders, they shall set up a grammar school … every such town shall pay 5 pounds to the next school till they shall perform this order.

The design is worth stating as a mechanism: the school requirement was indexed to exactly the growth the previous section measured. A population doubling each generation kept pushing towns across the 50-household and 100-family thresholds, so the statute converted demographic compounding into school-founding automatically. Contrast the contemporary Chesapeake: Virginia's governor William Berkeley reported to London in 1671, "I thank God, there are no free schools nor printing, and I hope we shall not have these hundred years."

<Example title="what the thresholds meant in children">
Take a town at the 100-family line, with the completed-family sizes of the previous section (six to eight surviving children, so roughly two to three of school age at any moment per family).

School-age children at the grammar-school threshold: $100 \times 2.5 \approx 250$, across the writing school and the grammar school.

A town crossing 50 households carried roughly $50 \times 2.5 \approx 125$ children for its one required schoolmaster: a real school, not a statutory fiction, with the £5 annual penalty running until the town complied. Dedham, whose covenant Exercise 1 reads, voted a tax-supported school in 1644, three years before the law required one.
</Example>

The output of the statutes is measurable because colonial documents demanded signatures. Kenneth Lockridge's *Literacy in Colonial New England* (1974) counted signatures against marks on thousands of wills: about **60 percent** of New England men could sign in 1650–1670, about 85 percent by 1758–1762, and about 90 percent by 1787–1795: near-universal male literacy on the eve of the Republic, roughly twice contemporary English rates at the start of the series, and the highest of any large population then measured. Women's signature rates ran far lower, about 31 percent rising to 48 percent.

State the method with its limits, because the proxy is imperfect in a known direction. Signing is a writing skill, and colonial schooling taught reading before writing, to girls often reading only, so signature counts undercount readers, and undercount women most. Wills also skew toward the propertied and the old. The corrections widen rather than narrow the finding: measured conservatively, New England reading approached universality among men and was substantially underestimated among women.

Around the schools grew the rest of a print economy, each piece dated. Harvard College was voted by the General Court in 1636, six years after the colony's founding, while the settlements were still palisaded. A printing press reached Cambridge in 1638, the first in English America; its first surviving book, the *Bay Psalm Book* (1640), was the first book printed in the English colonies. The first colonial newspaper, *Publick Occurrences* (Boston, 1690), was suppressed after a single issue; the *Boston News-Letter* (1704) became the first to last, and by the mid-1760s the thirteen colonies supported some two dozen weekly newspapers for the literate public this section counted.

This is the settler-selection measurement the course's thesis runs on, and it is now on the record: a migration filtered for the literate and dissenting, a legal system that manufactured literacy by statute, and the institutional ladder of the previous sections staffed by people who could read the documents they were signing. What stands established is the anomaly's engine, measured: selected family migration compounding at a 27-year doubling time, self-government reproduced by covenant at every scale, and near-universal male literacy engineered by law. What the engine did to the nations around it is the next question: <Ref lesson="natives-and-english">the next lesson</Ref> takes up the alliance of 1621 and follows it to the war of 1675.

<KeyFact>
New England literacy was engineered, not inherited: a 1642 household mandate, a 1647 town mandate indexed to population growth, a college by 1636, a press by 1638, and a measured male signature-literacy series running 60 percent (1650–70) to 90 percent (1787–95), the highest in the Atlantic world.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Apply the Compact's three-move reading (who acts, what is created, what it may do) to the Dedham town covenant of 1636, quoted here in Lockridge's modernized text:

> One: We whose names are here unto subscribed do, in the fear and reverence of our Almighty God, mutually and severally promise amongst ourselves and each other to profess and practice one truth …
>
> Two: That we shall by all means labor to keep off from us all such as are contrary minded, and receive only such unto us as may be probably of one heart with us …
>
> Three: That if at any time differences shall rise between parties of our said town, that then such party or parties shall presently refer all such differences unto some one, two, or three others of our said society to be fully accorded and determined …
>
> Four: That every man that … shall have lots in our said town shall pay his share in all such … charges as shall be imposed on him …, as also become freely subject unto all such orders and constitutions as shall be … made now or at any time hereafter …
>
> Five: … every man so received into the town is to subscribe hereunto his name, thereby obliging both himself and his successors after him forever …

Identify the three moves, then state two operative powers this covenant contains that the Mayflower Compact does not.

<Solution>
Who acts: the subscribed householders, "mutually and severally … amongst ourselves and each other": the same structure as the Compact's "solemnly and mutually," each signer binding himself to every other, God invoked as witness ("in the fear and reverence of our Almighty God") rather than as a chartering authority.

What is created: a town, a bounded community with membership ("received into the town"), which is the Compact's "civil body politic" one scale down.

What it may do: impose "charges" (taxation, section four) and make "orders and constitutions … now or at any time hereafter" (ongoing legislation, section four), with the same promised subjection that makes the Compact's laws binding.

Two powers beyond the Compact: (1) admission control: section two commits the town to screen and refuse "such as are contrary minded," a membership boundary the Compact, signed by whoever was aboard, never draws; (2) compulsory dispute arbitration: section three requires members to submit differences to internal arbitrators, a judicial mechanism the Compact lacks entirely. The town covenant also binds "successors … forever" (section five), extending consent to future members by requiring each to sign on admission: the covenant's answer to the problem of binding people not yet arrived.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Bradford, writing his history in the 1640s, records clause 6 of the 1621 treaty as: "That when ther men came to them, they should leave their bows & arrows behind them." *Mourt's Relation*, printed in London in 1622, records the same clause as: "That when their men came to us, they should leave their bows and arrows behind them, as we should do our pieces when we came to them." State which record is closer in time to the event, what the discrepancy consists of, and what a historian should conclude, about the clause and about using either source alone.

<Solution>
Proximity: *Mourt's Relation* was written within months of the treaty (its material dates from 1620–1621) and printed in 1622; Bradford wrote his account some twenty years later, from memory and papers.

The discrepancy: Mourt's records a symmetric disarmament clause: Wampanoag men leave bows, English men leave guns, at each other's settlements. Bradford's later version keeps the Wampanoag obligation and drops the English one, turning a mutual clause into a one-sided one.

Conclusion about the clause: the earlier, closer record should be preferred; the 1621 agreement almost certainly bound both sides to disarm when visiting, which fits the treaty's other reciprocal clauses (3 and 4).

Conclusion about sources: recollection drifts in the rememberer's favor: two decades on, with relations deteriorating, the English side's obligations had faded from the colony's memory of its own treaty. Neither source is dispensable: Bradford supplies the numbered list and the duration ("continued this 24. years"); Mourt's supplies the contemporary text. Reading them against each other is the method, the same records/argues/omits discipline the course has applied since <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the first lesson</Ref>, here catching a primary source revising another.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
New England's population grew from about 21,000 in 1642 to about 91,000 in 1700 with net migration near zero. Benjamin Franklin's 1751 estimate, established in <Ref lesson="european-empires">the empires lesson</Ref>, put colonial doubling at 20 to 25 years with immigration included. Compute New England's doubling time from the endpoints, state whether it falls inside Franklin's band, and explain why a birth-driven doubling time is the stronger evidence for the course's compounding argument.

<Solution>
Growth factor: $91{,}000 / 21{,}000 \approx 4.33$. Doublings: $\log_2 4.33 \approx 2.1$. Elapsed time: $1700 - 1642 = 58$ years. Doubling time: $58 / 2.1 \approx 27$ years, just outside Franklin's 20-to-25-year band, close to its slow edge.

Why it is stronger evidence: Franklin's band describes the colonies as a whole, where immigration and natural increase are mixed and cannot be separated from the totals. New England after 1642 is a natural experiment with the immigration term removed: the doubling is attributable to births over deaths alone. A population that doubles by reproduction needs no continuing inflow, no recruiting, and no company subsidy: it compounds wherever it stands, which is exactly the property the rival colonization models of <Ref lesson="european-empires">the empires lesson</Ref> lacked. The 27-year figure is the anomaly's engine running with every other input shut off.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A Massachusetts town grows along the trajectory of this lesson: 30 households at founding, doubling every 27 years. Using the 1647 law's thresholds, compute approximately when the town becomes legally obliged (a) to appoint a schoolmaster and (b) to keep a grammar school, and state what this implies about the statute's relationship to the demography.

<Solution>
Households over time: $30 \to 60$ after 27 years $\to 120$ after 54 years.

(a) The 50-household threshold falls between year 0 and year 27: $30 \times 2^{t/27} = 50$ gives $t = 27 \log_2(50/30) = 27 \times 0.737 \approx 20$ years. About two decades after founding, the town must appoint a teacher of reading and writing.

(b) The 100-family threshold: $t = 27 \log_2(100/30) = 27 \times 1.737 \approx 47$ years. Within the founders' children's lifetimes, the town must maintain a grammar school (the Latin school preparing boys for Harvard), or pay £5 a year until it does.

Implication: the statute is indexed to the demography. Because the thresholds are population counts and the population doubles each generation, every successful town is pulled across both lines on a predictable clock: the law converts compounding households into compounding schools without any further legislative act. Literacy was not left to prosperity; it was coupled to it by statute.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The literacy series in this lesson rests on counting signatures versus marks on wills. State the method's central assumption, then give two specific biases, one direction each, and what each implies about the true reading rate relative to the measured 60-to-90-percent series.

<Solution>
Central assumption: the ability to sign one's name is a usable proxy for literacy: that people who could sign could read, and people who made a mark could not.

Bias 1 (measured rate understates reading): colonial pedagogy taught reading first and writing second, and many people, girls especially in schools that taught them reading only, learned to read without ever learning to write. Such readers appear as marks. The true reading rate therefore sits above the signature rate, and the gap is largest for women, whose measured 31-to-48-percent series most underestimates actual reading.

Bias 2 (sample is unrepresentative): wills are made disproportionately by the propertied, the old, and by men. Since literacy correlated with property, will-makers likely over-represent the literate relative to the full adult population, pushing in the opposite direction from bias 1 for the male series.

Net: the male series is an approximation bracketed by two offsetting biases; the female series is a floor. The lesson's claim survives both: even read conservatively, New England male literacy approached universality by the Revolution, and the engineered mechanism (the 1642 and 1647 statutes) is documented independently of the measurement.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Winthrop's "citty upon a hill" sentence is quoted in this lesson together with the sentence that follows it. Apply the records / argues / omits method to the passage as a primary source for 1630, and state in one sentence why quoting the famous sentence alone reverses the passage's meaning.

<Solution>
Possible answers.

Records: the expectations of the migration's leadership at the moment of crossing: that the colony was understood as a covenant with God, publicly visible, whose success was conditional on conduct; evidence of the settlers' self-understanding, not of what the colony became.

Argues: that the colony's visibility is a liability under covenant logic: "the eies of all people are uppon us" is offered as a reason for discipline, because failure will be seen and will become "a story and a by-word through the world." The sermon argues for mutual obligation ("wee are entered into Covenant with Him"), not for exceptionalism.

Omits: everyone outside the covenant: the Wampanoag on whose emptied coast the fleet was landing, the non-Puritan English, and any account of what failure would cost people other than the covenanters; also any doubt that the enterprise's terms were in fact God's.

One sentence: cut off after "uppon us," the passage reads as a boast about being watched in admiration, when as written it is a warning about being watched for failure; the conditional clause is the argument, and quoting the antecedent without the consequent converts a penalty clause into a compliment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Pilgrim Code of Law (1636) opens with its drafters "having read the combination made at Cape Cod the 11th of November 1620 … as also our letters patents." Using this clause and the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut (1639), construct the two-step argument that New England self-government *compounded*, that is, that later foundations were built from earlier ones rather than improvised independently, and state one observation from this lesson that limits how far the compounding claim can be pushed.

<Solution>
Step 1 (vertical compounding): the Pilgrim Code of Law is a working legal code that names, as its own source of authority, a prior founding document, the 1620 combination, alongside the royal patent. This is a paper trail, not an inference: sixteen years after the signing, Plymouth's legislators treated the shipboard covenant as constitutional bedrock and legislated on top of it. Later documents inherit authority from earlier ones, which is what compounding means.

Step 2 (horizontal compounding): the Fundamental Orders show the same operation joining communities instead of years. Windsor, Hartford, and Wethersfield each already existed under its own covenant; in 1639 the three towns covenanted with each other, creating a common General Court above intact town governments. The unit of the earlier foundations (the covenanted town) becomes the building block of the later one (the covenanted colony): a two-level structure built from the same form.

The limit: the ladder's form spread further than any single text's influence. Lutz notes that the Piscataqua settlers of 1641 had almost certainly never seen the Mayflower Compact yet produced a document of the same shape, because both descend from the shared church-covenant form. Compounding therefore describes a replicating institutional technology, not a chain of citations only; the Pilgrim Code's explicit citation is the strongest single link, but the mechanism was a common template, independently reapplied, which is both weaker (no single founding text) and stronger (no single point of failure) than a citation chain.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

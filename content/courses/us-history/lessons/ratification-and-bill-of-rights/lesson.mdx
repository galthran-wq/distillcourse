The Constitution took effect only after a nine-month public argument fought in newspapers and state conventions, and the Bill of Rights is the price its opponents extracted.

The course's argument has established a selected, self-governing population, a war for independence won at measurable odds, and, across the last two lessons, the design: classical and English materials machined into a seven-article compound republic, signed by 39 of 55 delegates, with George Mason's refusal on the record ("There is no Declaration of Rights"). What stands open is whether the states would accept it. This lesson carries the design through the fight Article VII prescribed. It establishes, in order: the rules of the contest and why they favored the document's friends; the opposition's case, stated on its own terms; Publius's two answers; the thirteen convention verdicts, with the three margins that decided everything; and the twelve amendment articles the fight produced.

## Article VII: the rules were written by one side

The contest's rules came from the document being contested. <Ref lesson="constitutional-convention">The previous lesson</Ref> established Article VII's two choices: ratification by nine states rather than all thirteen, and by elected conventions rather than sitting legislatures. Both choices did work in the fight that followed.

The nine-state rule repaired the defect the framers knew best. Under the Articles of Confederation, amendment required unanimity, and the rule had bitten twice: the impost, the league's only revenue plan, was approved by twelve states and killed by one. Unanimity gives the last holdout a veto to sell. Nine-of-thirteen inverts the position: once the threshold is met, a holdout is not blocking a proposal but standing outside a functioning union, cut off from its trade, its protection, and its decisions. The last states to decide faced exclusion, not leverage, and the two that tested the proposition (below) folded under exactly that pressure.

Conventions did different work. Ratification by legislatures would have put the decision to the bodies the Constitution demoted, and would have made the document legally their act, a treaty of governments. Elected conventions continued the relocation of sovereignty <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the course has traced</Ref>: the people, not their governments, ordain the fundamental law. The Confederation Congress, asked in late September 1787 to forward the text, did so without endorsing it, after Richard Henry Lee's motion to attach amendments was turned back; the document's friends counted the silence as a win and pressed every state for a quick convention.

<KeyFact>
Article VII inverted the holdout's leverage before the argument began: under the Articles one state could veto any change, while under the nine-state rule the last states risked exclusion from a union that would leave without them. The ratification game was designed by the side playing offense.
</KeyFact>

One decision at Philadelphia ran the other way, and it set the agenda for the entire fight. On September 12, 1787, five days before the signing, Mason moved that a bill of rights be prepared, arguing it could be drafted in a few hours from the state declarations; the motion lost with every state delegation voting no. The convention's reasoning, that a government of enumerated powers needed no rights fence, is examined below. As campaign strategy the vote was a blunder: it handed the opposition its single most resonant charge, and repairing it would eventually cost a constitutional amendment in ten parts.

A note on names, because the record's vocabulary was itself a campaign move. The document's supporters took the name *Federalist* before their opponents could, though the opponents were the ones defending the existing federation; the opposition was left with the negative label. "Anti-Federalist" also implies a party, and there was none: the opposition ran from moderates who wanted amendments before ratification to men who wanted the document dead, and the two wings never coordinated a program. This lesson uses the record's term for the winners' opponents while flagging it as the winners' term.

## The case against: scale, the purse, the sword, and the missing bill

The opposition's argument deserves to be stated first and fairly, because it was the constitutional orthodoxy of its age. The first systematic statement appeared in the *New-York Journal* on October 18, 1787, signed [Brutus](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/brutus-i/), an anonymous New Yorker whose identity is still argued. Brutus opened where <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the design lesson</Ref>'s authorities pointed: Montesquieu, quoted directly, "It is natural to a republic to have only a small territory, otherwise it cannot long subsist." A republic works because representatives know their constituents and citizens trust laws they can see being made. Stretch it over a continent and the connection snaps: "History furnishes no example of a free republic, any thing like the extent of the United States."

The second count was powers. The new government would hold taxation, and "the authority to lay and collect taxes is the most important of any power that can be granted; it connects with it almost all other powers." It would also raise armies in peacetime, and standing armies, Brutus wrote, "have always proved the destruction of liberty." Purse and sword in one distant government, forever, with a supremacy clause over every state law: the objection was not to any clause's wording but to the concentration itself.

The third count was Mason's. Most of the revolutionary state constitutions carried declarations of rights, Virginia's of 1776 first among them; the federal document had none. James Wilson, the Scottish-trained lawyer who had signed both the Declaration and the Constitution, gave the standard Federalist answer in Philadelphia's State House Yard that October (the federal government held only enumerated powers, so a rights list was unnecessary) and added the sharper point in the state convention: no list could be complete, and a list that missed a right would imply the right did not exist. The Anti-Federalist reply was practical: the taxing power alone could reach anything, a press taxed to death needs no censorship statute, and the Constitution already protected some rights (jury trial in criminal cases, habeas corpus), which on Wilson's own logic endangered the rest.

<KeyFact>
The Anti-Federalist case was the era's textbook constitutionalism: republics must be small, and purse-and-sword powers must be fenced by declared rights. Half of that program became the Bill of Rights; the other half, structural limits on the taxing and army powers, was refused and never enacted.
</KeyFact>

## Publius's answers: the axioms inverted

The defense that became canon was written as campaign copy. Between October 1787 and May 1788, Hamilton, Madison, and Jay published 85 essays over the signature *Publius* (<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the Roman pseudonym</Ref> was itself a move in the argument) in New York City newspapers, aimed at New York's hostile electorate. [Federalist No. 1](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed01.asp) stated the stakes as the course's spine states them: whether societies of men can establish good government "from reflection and choice," or are "forever destined to depend for their political constitutions on accident and force."

<Remark title="what the Federalist Papers did and did not do">
A common account makes the essays the instrument that won ratification. The record does not support it, and the correct claim matters. The essays circulated thinly outside New York; the press that carried them was one in which barely a paper in eight printed substantial opposition material anyway; and the fight's decisive device, the Massachusetts Compromise, owed them nothing: Massachusetts voted on February 6, 1788, the day No. 51 first appeared in a New York paper. In New York itself, where the essays saturated the press, the convention was elected two-to-one against the Constitution and ratified by three votes, moved by news of other states' votes rather than by argument. The essays' canonical standing came afterward, as courts and historians mined them for the framers' reasoning. Read them as this course reads Las Casas or the Declaration: as the strongest surviving statement of one side's case, not as the cause of the outcome.
</Remark>

### No. 10: faction cured by scale

Madison's answer to Brutus took the small-republic axiom head on. The concrete problem was the one the course met in the 1780s record: state majorities, debtors most visibly, had used seven legislatures to print money and suspend debts, faithfully serving the interest that elected them (<Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the "vices"</Ref>). [No. 10](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed10.asp) names the disease: a *faction* is any group, "whether amounting to a majority or a minority of the whole," united by an interest "adversed to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community."

The essay then closes the cure space with an engineer's elimination. Two ways to remove faction's causes: destroy liberty, which is "worse than the disease," or give every citizen the same opinions, which is impossible, since "the latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man." So causes stay; only effects can be controlled. A minority faction is handled by ordinary voting. The hard case is a majority faction, and here Madison inverts the axiom he was taught: "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens."

  caption="No. 10's elimination argument: both cause-removing cures rejected, leaving control of effects — and the extended republic as the control."

The *extended republic* is the claim that scale itself is the control: in a small republic one interest can be a standing majority and rule; in a large one, a majority must be a coalition of many interests, and coalitions must bargain. Brutus's small-republic rule descends from Montesquieu and, behind him, from the classical theory of politics <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the course traced</Ref>; No. 10 is the argument Gordon Wood's *Creation of the American Republic* marks as the end of that theory: a republic stabilized by multiplied interests rather than by virtue or smallness. The classical premise was retired in a newspaper column.

### No. 39 and No. 51: the compound republic, named and armed

To the charge of "consolidation," Madison answered with a description, not a denial. [No. 39](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed39.asp) walks the document's features and concludes it is "in strictness, neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a composition of both": federal in ratification and the Senate, national in the House and in operating directly on citizens. The previous lesson used the sentence to name the structure; in 1788 it was a rebuttal, conceding half of Brutus's description and denying the half that predicted the states' destruction.

[No. 51](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed51.asp) supplies the mechanics in the aphorisms the course has already met once: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition"; "If men were angels, no government would be necessary"; and the designer's statement of the whole problem, "you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself." The essay's addition for the ratification argument is the compound structure as a rights guarantee: power "is first divided between two distinct governments, and then the portion allotted to each subdivided among distinct and separate departments. Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people." Structure, Publius argued, protects rights better than parchment declarations, which is the same premise that lost Mason's motion.

### An argument under tilt

The presses were not neutral ground. More than ninety newspapers and magazines circulated in 1787–88; twelve of them published any substantial Anti-Federalist material. Publishers sat in coastal cities where Federalism was strongest, and the few editors who tried to print both sides faced subscriber and advertiser boycotts; over ninety percent of the population lived in the countryside the papers barely reached. The debate "the people" conducted ran through a channel one side owned.

Pennsylvania showed how far the document's friends would push. In September 1787 the state assembly's Federalist majority, needing a quorum to call a quick convention before opposition could organize, had two boycotting members dragged from their lodgings and held in their seats. The convention ratified on December 12, 46–23, the first large state; Federalist printers then published convention reports scrubbed of the minority's speeches. The hardball backfired: the minority's printed *Dissent* circulated west and armed the opposition everywhere.

The same Pennsylvania record preserves what the conventions actually were. When Wilson and Chief Justice Thomas McKean lectured the convention on the history of juries, citing Sweden's lapsed jury trials, William Findley, a self-taught Scots-Irish weaver turned backcountry assemblyman, challenged their learning on the floor, returned days later with the supporting citation from Blackstone, and observed that a son of his six months into legal study would deserve a whipping for not knowing the passage. Wilson threw back the old courtroom adage: "[I have] forgotten more law than ever you learned." Pandemonium, by the reporter's account; also real argument, between a signer of the Constitution and a weaver, on the record, about a clause. The conventions were rushed and tilted, and they were still deliberative bodies.

New England ran the contest through the institutions this course has tracked since the covenant: towns met, debated, and elected instructed delegates. Connecticut's convention ratified 128–40 on January 9, 1788, after town meetings across the state had chosen their men; Doolittle's cartoon at right, engraved in New Haven that year, drew the state as a debt-mired wagon hauled opposite ways by the two factions, which is a fair one-image summary of the electorate the delegates answered to.

### Massachusetts: the compromise that became the template

<Example title="the Massachusetts Compromise, worked">
**The problem.** Massachusetts was the first state where ratification was genuinely in doubt, and the biggest yet to vote. Its convention of 355-plus delegates, the largest in any state, was heavy with delegates from the western towns that had marched with Shays twelve months earlier (<Ref lesson="constitutional-convention">the Springfield confrontation</Ref>); Federalist head-counts through January 1788 projected a losing vote.

**The device.** Instead of fighting amendments, Federalist managers changed what a yes vote meant: ratify *unconditionally*, and with the ratification *recommend* nine amendments, which the state's future congressmen would be instructed to pursue "agreeably to the Fifth article." An Anti-Federalist delegate could now vote yes without endorsing the document as it stood; the union got its ratification immediately, and the objections were routed into the Constitution's own repair machinery: Article V, used as a political instrument before the government it belonged to existed.

**The broker and his price.** The proposal needed a name no one could call a Federalist tool. Governor John Hancock, the convention's president, had stayed home with gout while the outcome was uncertain. Federalist leaders offered him the motion and — the terms are reported at second hand — their support for his reelection, with talk of the vice presidency or, should Virginia stay out and leave Washington ineligible, the presidency itself. Hancock appeared, presented the amendments as his own "Conciliatory Proposition," and Samuel Adams seconded. The [form of ratification](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/ratma.asp) carries the design in one clause: the amendments "would remove the fears and quiet the apprehensions of many of the good people of this Commonwealth."

**The result.** February 6, 1788: 187–168. Ten switched votes lose it. The losers mostly accepted the outcome as fairly reached, which mattered as much as the number: no second-convention movement came out of Massachusetts.
</Example>

Every state that ratified after Massachusetts attached its own recommended amendments, and the live question everywhere became *prior versus subsequent*: amendments as a condition of ratifying, the Anti-Federalist fallback, or as a recommendation after it, the Massachusetts form. That distinction, procedural on its face, decided whether the Constitution took effect; the razor-thin states below all came down to it.

### South Carolina, and the ninth state

South Carolina ratified on May 23, 1788, 149–73, a comfortable-looking margin produced by an uncomfortable map: the coastal parishes, with fewer than 29,000 people, held 143 convention seats; the backcountry, nearly four times as populous and far more skeptical, held 93. The convention also met in Charleston, where planters and merchants kept open houses for wavering delegates. The vote records the state's elite consensus, not its population.

New Hampshire supplied the threshold. Its convention met in February 1788 with enough delegates bound by hostile town instructions to defeat ratification, so Federalist managers adjourned it before a vote could be taken, four months of time in which several towns reconsidered. On June 21, 1788, New Hampshire ratified 57–47, the ninth state: Article VII was satisfied and the Constitution would take effect for the ratifiers. On paper the contest was over. In fact the new union was a shell without Virginia and New York, the largest state and the harbor that split New England from the rest; both were in convention that week, and the news was days away from either.

The Richmond convention, June 2–25, 1788, was the fight at full strength: the largest state, half the signers' prestige, and the Revolution's most famous orator against the Constitution's principal architect. Patrick Henry, whose defiance of the Stamp Act had opened <Ref lesson="american-revolution">the imperial crisis</Ref>, held the floor for as much as a quarter of the proceedings. His opening struck the document's first three words: "What right had they to say, We, the people?... Who authorized them to speak the language of, We, the people, instead of, We, the states?" On the checks Publius advertised: "There will be no checks, no real balances, in this government. What can avail your specious, imaginary balances, your rope-dancing, chain-rattling, ridiculous ideal checks and contrivances?" On the presidency: the plan "has an awful squinting; it squints towards monarchy."

Madison answered the way he had argued at Philadelphia: quietly, clause by clause, in a voice contemporaries strained to hear, with Governor Edmund Randolph, who had refused to sign in September, now converted by the fear of disunion and arguing for ratification. The Federalists' winning move was the Massachusetts form. On June 25 the convention rejected prior amendments 88–80, then ratified 89–79, appending a twenty-article declaration of rights and twenty structural amendments as recommendations. The margin was ten delegates of 168. Richmond voted believing Virginia might be the ninth state, the one whose vote launched the government; the news from New Hampshire, four days old, had not arrived.

### New York: express riders and a secession threat, 30 to 27

<Figure
  src="./figures/hamilton-trumbull.jpg"
  alt="Half-length oil portrait of Alexander Hamilton in a dark coat and white cravat, head turned to his left, against a plain dark brown ground"
  caption="Alexander Hamilton, painted by John Trumbull in 1806, two years after his death. New York's convention met at Poughkeepsie, eighty miles up the Hudson, on June 17, elected roughly two-to-one against the Constitution under Governor George Clinton, who presided for the opposition. Hamilton's campaign was logistics as much as argument: he paid from his own pocket for relays of express riders from the New Hampshire and Virginia conventions, and stalled his own convention until they arrived. The New Hampshire rider came in on June 24, Virginia's on July 2, and together they changed the question: not whether the Constitution would exist, but whether New York would be inside it. New York City's Federalists supplied pressure that may have been decisive: an open threat that the city would secede from the state and ratify alone, taking the harbor's customs revenue with it.

Melancton Smith, the opposition's ablest floor leader, moved the resolution that ended it: ratification not "upon condition" but "[in full confidence](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/ratny.asp)" that amendments would follow, the Massachusetts device stretched to cover a state that had wanted conditions. On July 26, 1788, New York ratified 30–27, three votes of 57, with some fifty recommended amendments attached. The price of even that margin was a unanimous *circular letter* to the state legislatures urging a second general convention to consider amendments, a device aimed at reopening the whole settlement. Madison, watching from New York, wrote Washington on August 11 that the letter "has a most pestilent tendency." Exercise 4 takes the letter apart.

### Out past the inauguration

Two states stayed out. North Carolina's Hillsborough convention voted 184–84 on August 2, 1788, to neither ratify nor reject until a declaration of rights and amendments existed, the only state to make amendments a formal precondition. Rhode Island never called a convention at all in 1788; it put the Constitution to a popular referendum in March, which Federalists boycotted and the Constitution lost 2,708–237. Both states were foreign territory on April 30, 1789, when Washington took the oath in New York. North Carolina ratified 194–77 in November 1789, after Congress had sent the amendment articles north of it; Rhode Island held out until May 29, 1790, ratifying 34–32 with the Senate debating a bill to cut the state off from American commerce. Article VII's exclusion logic, not persuasion, finished the map.

## The price: from two hundred demands to ten amendments

The compromise that carried the conventions left a debt on the table. Seven ratifying states plus North Carolina had filed recommended amendments, Massachusetts nine, Virginia forty, New York some fifty, and the lists sorted into the two categories the opposition's case had always contained: declarations of rights (press, religion, jury, due process, arms) and structural limits on the new government (federal direct taxes restricted, standing armies curbed, federal powers confined to those "expressly" delegated, supermajorities for trade laws). The second category was the Anti-Federalist program proper: it would have re-fought Philadelphia by amendment.

Whether the debt would be paid was an open question, because the men now running the government had argued rights declarations were useless or worse. Hamilton had made the case at full strength in [No. 84](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fed84.asp): bills of rights "are not only unnecessary in the proposed Constitution, but would even be dangerous. For why declare that things shall not be done which there is no power to do?"; the Constitution, "in every rational sense, and to every useful purpose," was itself a bill of rights. Madison's private view was cooler but parallel: state declarations had been "parchment barriers" overrun by "overbearing majorities in every State," he wrote Jefferson in October 1788, and he had "never thought the omission a material defect." Jefferson, from Paris, had pushed the other way for a year: "a bill of rights is what the people are entitled to against every government on earth, general or particular, and what no just government should refuse, or rest on inference."

### Madison's conversion, engineered by his enemies

What converted Madison was the ratification bargain plus Patrick Henry. Henry's majority in the Virginia legislature kept Madison out of the new Senate, electing two Anti-Federalists instead, and drew his House district to favor James Monroe; the campaign's dominant issue was whether Madison would honor the promised amendments. In open letters to his constituents Madison pledged to pursue a bill of rights in the First Congress, and won narrowly. The pledge, made to survive a gerrymander, became federal policy: Madison drafted the call for rights amendments in Washington's inaugural address, then rose in the House on June 8, 1789, to force the question on colleagues who thought revenue and the judiciary more urgent.

His selection was the fight's last filter, and the figure below is the lesson's summary of it. Madison took the states' lists and kept, almost exclusively, the rights category; every structural demand was dropped as unable to clear two-thirds of a Federalist Congress, and, in Madison's design, as unwise. Anti-Federalists saw the maneuver plainly: Aedanus Burke of South Carolina called the amendments "a tub thrown out to a whale," a decoy to distract from the structural program. The one structural survivor proves the rule: what became the Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated powers to the states, but Madison struck the word "expressly" that the state lists had demanded, leaving the reservation without teeth against implied powers.

The chain ran: Madison's proposals in June, seventeen articles from the House in August, twelve from the Senate in September. Congress gave the twelve articles final approval on September 25, 1789 — the amendments' official proposal date — and Washington transmitted them to the states on October 2. Ratification took two years and ended where the ratification fight had been sharpest: Virginia's assent on December 15, 1791, made ten of the twelve articles part of the Constitution.

### Reading the parchment

The engrossed parchment is worth reading closely, because it does not say what a modern reader expects. It lists twelve articles, not ten, and the numbering never matches the amendments Americans cite. "Article the first," a formula for enlarging the House, was never ratified; sent without a deadline, it is technically still before the states. "Article the second," barring a sitting Congress from raising its own pay before an election intervened, fell short in 1791 and then sat, ratifiable, until a state-by-state campaign completed it in May 1992 as the Twenty-seventh Amendment, 202 years after Congress proposed it. The First Amendment is the parchment's "Article the third."

<Figure
  src="./figures/twelve-articles-fates.svg"
  alt="Mapping of the twelve 1789 articles to their fates: Article the first, on House size, never ratified; Article the second, on congressional pay, ratified in May 1992 as the 27th Amendment, 202 years later; Articles the third through the twelfth became Amendments 1 through 10, ratified December 15, 1791, with Article the third becoming the First Amendment"
  caption="The parchment's own numbering against the amendments' — the off-by-two left by Articles the first and second failing to ratify in 1791."
/>

Three period facts keep the story straight. First, no ratifying state had made amendments a legal condition; only North Carolina, which had refused to ratify, held its vote until the articles existed. Second, contemporaries did not call the ten articles "the Bill of Rights" and did not treat them as a founding monument; they were the closing entry of a political bargain, and their veneration is a later construction, much as the Federalist's is. Third, the amendments bound only the federal government: "Congress shall make no law" restrained Congress, not Virginia or Massachusetts, and states kept established churches and unfenced police powers for decades. Enforcing these guarantees against the states required the Fourteenth Amendment (<Ref lesson="reconstruction">the second founding</Ref>) and a twentieth-century Supreme Court, a thread the course picks up with <Ref lesson="jim-crow">Jim Crow</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
Congress proposed twelve articles on September 25, 1789 — Washington transmitted them to the states on October 2 — and ten were ratified on December 15, 1791. The rights half of the Anti-Federalist program became constitutional text; the structural half was filtered out in Madison's hands, and "Article the second" waited 202 years to become the Twenty-seventh Amendment.
</KeyFact>

What is now established: Article VII set a nine-state convention game that inverted holdout leverage; the opposition argued the era's orthodoxy, scale and rights, against the design; Publius answered by inverting the small-republic axiom and naming the compound; the conventions ratified by margins of 19, 10, and 3 in the three states the union could not lose, carried by the Massachusetts device of unconditional ratification plus recommended amendments; and the First Congress paid the bargain's price in ten rights amendments while burying the structural program. The Constitution of 1787–91 was now law, with a government to run. <Ref lesson="early-republic-politics">The next lesson</Ref> follows that government's first decade: the founders who dreaded faction building two organized parties, and the election of 1800 testing whether the machine could transfer power between enemies.

<Exercise>
Use the vote table. (a) Compute, for Massachusetts, Virginia, and New York separately, the minimum number of delegates who had to switch sides to produce a recorded majority against ratification — a tied vote is not a majority against, and your solution should treat the tie case explicitly — and the total. (b) State precisely what that total establishes about ratification, and (c) one reason the switch-count model understates the contingency and one reason it overstates it.

<Solution>
(a) A switch moves one vote across the aisle, so a margin $m$ becomes a majority against when switches $s$ satisfy $2s > m$. Massachusetts, margin 19: $s = 10$ (177–178). Virginia, margin 10, is the tie case: five switches produce only 84–84, and while a tied convention defeats the motion to ratify, a tie is not a recorded majority against, so the stem's definition requires $s = 6$ (83–85). New York, margin 3: $s = 2$ (28–29). Total: $18$ delegates of the $580$ voting in the three conventions (17 if a Virginia tie were accepted as reversal). (b) It establishes contingency in the course's sense: the constitutional order was not structurally inevitable; eighteen individual decisions in three rooms, about three percent of the three conventions' members, separate the outcome from a rejected Constitution. (c) Understates: the three votes were sequential, not independent; a Massachusetts defeat in February would likely have flipped later conventions wholesale, so fewer than eighteen switches (ten, in Boston) might have sufficed to lose far more than three states. Overstates: the same sequencing means late no-votes were partly free (some New York delegates could vote no precisely because ten states had already ratified), so the recorded margins are not pure measures of opinion, and a genuinely decisive vote might have gone differently. The number frames the contingency; the narrative supplies its direction.

<Exercise>
The chapter worked the Massachusetts Compromise as a device: unconditional ratification plus recommended amendments, routing objections into Article V. Analyze New York's circular letter as the same device pointed the other way. What does the letter record, what does it argue, what did each side intend it to do, and why did Madison tell Washington it had "a most pestilent tendency" while accepting recommended amendments calmly?

<Solution>
Records: that New York's convention, unanimously (Federalists included, as the price of the 30–27 ratification), asked the state legislatures to procure a second general convention to consider amendments. Argues: that the ratification settlement was provisional and the document's defects general, requiring a body with power over the whole text, not piecemeal congressional amendment. Intent: for Anti-Federalists, to reopen at a second convention the structural questions (taxes, army, "expressly" delegated powers) that recommended amendments would never deliver through a Federalist Congress; for New York Federalists, a face-saving concession that made the three-vote ratification possible. The asymmetry Madison saw: recommended amendments flow through Article V's congressional route, where two-thirds of a friendly Congress filters them; the Massachusetts device kept control with the Constitution's supporters. A second convention would sit outside that filter with an open mandate, able to redraft anything, elected amid organized opposition: the same constituent device that had produced the Constitution, aimed back at it. Same mechanism, opposite control of the funnel: that is why the letter was dangerous and the recommendations were not. Only Virginia endorsed the call, and the Bill of Rights drained the movement.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
A textbook sentence claims: "The Federalist Papers persuaded the states to ratify the Constitution." Name two facts from this chapter the claim fails to account for, state what would have to be true for it to survive, and write the defensible version.

<Solution>
Two facts. First, reach and timing: the essays ran in New York City papers for a New York audience and circulated thinly elsewhere; Massachusetts, the pivotal convention, voted the day No. 51 first appeared in New York, so the decisive compromise there cannot descend from essays not yet written or distributed. Second, the outcome where the essays saturated: New York elected its convention roughly two-to-one against the Constitution after months of Publius, and ratified 30–27 only after news of the ninth and tenth states and a city secession threat changed the stakes; the swing followed events, not argument. For the claim to survive, the record would need wide contemporary reprinting in the doubtful states and testimony from swing delegates crediting the essays; it shows neither. Defensible version: the Federalist Papers are the fullest surviving statement of the case for ratification, written as campaign literature for one state, and their canonical, quasi-authoritative standing was built afterward by courts and historians mining them for the framers' reasoning. Persuasion state by state was done by conventions, compromises, and Article VII's arithmetic.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Brutus rested the opposition's case on Montesquieu's small-republic doctrine. Drawing on the roman-blueprint lesson (the citation count that put Montesquieu first among named authorities, and Wood's "end of classical politics"), explain in a short paragraph why both sides had to argue through Montesquieu, and state exactly what Madison's extended-republic argument retired and what it kept.

<Solution>
Montesquieu led the founders' named citations (8.3 percent, ahead of Blackstone and far ahead of Locke), so his doctrines functioned as shared premises: an argument that ignored him conceded authority to the side that quoted him. Brutus therefore opened with Montesquieu's small-territory rule as settled science, and Madison could not dismiss the authority (Federalist 47 had just called him "the oracle" on separated powers), so No. 10, with No. 9's "great improvement" claim, had to beat the doctrine on its own ground, arguing that representation and scale changed the analysis rather than that the authority erred. What the extended-republic argument retired: the classical premise that a republic survives on smallness and civic virtue, the 1776 ideology Wood's account tracks to its 1780s falsification, replaced by stability from multiplied, mutually checking interests; this is why Wood marks No. 10 as the end of classical politics. What it kept: Montesquieu's mechanics of separated powers, mixed checking, and the fear of concentrated purse and sword, now redeployed inside a large republic. The same split the course found at Philadelphia: classical machinery retained, classical sociology discarded.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Klarman's review of Maier gives one section the title "Was It a Fair Fight?". Answer it: using only evidence from this chapter, write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph, stating your criterion of fairness explicitly. Your evidence must include at least one item from each side of the ledger.

<Solution>
One defensible answer. Criterion: a fair contest requires roughly equal access to the deciding electorate and decision rules not engineered for one outcome. Claim: the fight was genuinely deliberative but structurally tilted toward ratification. Evidence for tilt: twelve of ninety-plus papers printed substantial opposition material, before an electorate over ninety percent rural; South Carolina's convention gave 143 seats to coastal parishes with under 29,000 people against 93 for a backcountry four times larger; Pennsylvania's quorum was made by physical force and its published debates scrubbed; conventions met in Federalist cities; Article VII itself converted late opposition into a choice between joining and exclusion. Evidence against: the conventions were elected, argued clause by clause for weeks, and are preserved in real debate (Findley forcing Wilson to the floor over a Blackstone citation); the losing side extracted a real price, the recommended amendments that became the Bill of Rights; Massachusetts's losers judged themselves fairly treated; and the three decisive margins show no vote was foreordained. Reasoning: by the stated criterion the process fails the equal-access test while passing the deliberation test; the honest verdict is a contested, genuinely close argument conducted on a field one side had built, which is what the vote table's razors and the opposition's half-paid program both record.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The states recommended both rights amendments and structural amendments; only the rights survived. Explain why, using at least three causes from this chapter, and use the fate of the word "expressly" as your test case.

<Solution>
Three causes. Control of the funnel: Article V routes amendments through Congress, and the First Congress was overwhelmingly Federalist; Madison, holding the drafting pen, selected rights provisions and dropped every structural demand as unable to clear two-thirds; the filter sat with the Constitution's friends. Asymmetric cost: rights declarations subtracted no power the Federalists valued (Hamilton's No. 84 called them unnecessary precisely because the powers didn't reach those objects), while tax limits, army limits, and supermajority trade rules would have re-created the Confederation's impotence: one concession was cheap, the other was the whole fight again. Collapsed leverage: after eleven ratifications the Anti-Federalists had no threat left; the second-convention movement died with only Virginia backing New York's circular letter, so promises could be paid in the cheapest coin. Test case: the state lists demanded that federal powers be confined to those "expressly" delegated, the Articles' own language and a genuine structural fence. Madison kept the reservation of powers (the Tenth Amendment) but struck "expressly," leaving implied powers intact; the one structural amendment that survived did so by being defanged, which is the funnel's whole logic in a single word.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

In December 1776 the Continental Army was down to a few thousand effectives
with most enlistments expiring on the 31st; ten days and two small battles
later the Revolution was a going concern again, and that margin is how the
whole war worked.

The course's argument has reached the war its previous step required.
<Ref lesson="american-revolution">The last lesson</Ref> closed with
independence enacted by resolution on July 2, 1776 and justified as a legal
argument; what stands open is whether a declaration by thirteen colonies
without a navy, a treasury, or a professional army could survive contact
with the empire that owned all three. This lesson states the odds first and
then explains the outcome, in order: the mismatch on paper; Boston, won by
engineering; the near-death of 1776 and the ten days that reversed it;
Saratoga and the French alliance that made the rebellion a world war; the
war's reality as a civil war among colonists, Native nations, and the
enslaved; and Yorktown, where seven weeks of French sea power ended it.

The
lesson also introduces a standing analytic category of this course,
contingency, which later lessons reuse for the ratification margins, the
election of 1800, and Atlanta in 1864. The constitutional question that
victory created, whether self-government could scale from town to
continent, passes to <Ref lesson="roman-blueprint">the next lesson</Ref>.

## The odds on paper: everything but distance

A declaration is not a war, and the war's terms are best stated as a table.
Britain in 1776 fielded the largest navy on earth; an army that averaged
some 35,000 regulars in North America over the war, peaking near 40,000 in
1781; roughly 30,000 professional soldiers hired from Hesse-Kassel and its
neighbors (the *Hessians*) over the war's whole course, about 18,000 of
them in America by the end of 1776; and the taxing and borrowing power of
the empire whose fiscal reach
<Ref lesson="american-revolution">started the crisis</Ref>.

The colonies had no navy, a Congress that could requisition money from the
states but not tax, a paper currency that depreciated through the war, and
a Continental Army that rarely counted 20,000 effectives and was rebuilt
almost annually as enlistments expired.

The population arithmetic ran the same way: about eight million Britons
against about 2.5 million colonists, one in five of them enslaved. George
III, who had told Parliament in October 1775 that the rebellion aimed at an
independent empire (<Ref lesson="american-revolution">the speech that
closed reconciliation</Ref>), was not being irrational when he expected a
short police action.

### Space: the one line that favored the rebels

The colonies held one strategic asset, and it was not military: **space**.
Every British soldier, cannon, and barrel of beef crossed 3,000 miles of
ocean, six to eight weeks under sail; the theater was the size of Western
Europe; and the rebellion had no capital whose capture would end it.

Britain took Boston, New York, and Philadelphia in succession, and the war
continued each time. The two sides therefore fought different wars: Britain
had to reconquer a continent, while Washington had only to keep an army in
existence until Britain's costs exceeded its patience. That asymmetry
defines what "winning" meant in every section below.

<KeyFact>
Britain held the largest navy on earth, kept an average of some 35,000
regulars in North America, and hired roughly 30,000 German soldiers over
the war's course, against a Continental Army that rarely counted 20,000
effectives at any one time; the rebellion's one compensating asset was
space: 3,000 miles of ocean, a continental theater, and no capital whose
fall could end the war.
</KeyFact>

## Boston: the first victory was an engineering project

The war's opening year tested whether the rebel asset could be converted
into results, and the answer came at Boston without a battle.

### An improvised army

The army besieging the town in 1775 (the militia force adopted by Congress
after Lexington and Concord) was an improvisation: some 16,000 New England
militiamen, chronically short of gunpowder, commanded from July by
Washington and led in part by men promoted straight from civilian life.
Nathanael Greene, a Rhode Island Quaker ironmaster who had taught himself
war from books, became its best general; Henry Knox, a 25-year-old Boston
bookseller, its chief of artillery.

### The noble train and one night's work

Knox's first assignment was a logistics feat. The siege lacked heavy guns;
Fort Ticonderoga, captured in May 1775 at the foot of Lake Champlain in
northern New York, had them. In the dead of winter Knox's party moved **59
cannon and mortars** some **300 miles**
by boat, sledge, and oxen over frozen rivers and the Berkshire hills,
leaving the fort in early December and reaching the camps outside Boston on
January 25, 1776.

The guns were used the way an engineer would use them. The ground on
Dorchester Heights, the hills commanding Boston harbor from the south, was
frozen too hard to entrench, so the fortifications were prefabricated:
timber frames and fascines built out of sight, then hauled up and assembled
in a single night, March 4–5, 1776, with the cannon behind them.

General
William Howe, commanding the garrison, judged an assault too costly and on
March 17 evacuated Boston entirely, taking about 9,000 troops and 1,100
Loyalist civilians to Halifax.

<KeyFact>
Boston was won without a battle: 59 cannon hauled 300 miles in winter and
one night of prefabricated fortification made the position untenable, and
the British army evacuated its only base in the colonies.
</KeyFact>

## 1776: the army nearly dies twice

Boston set an expectation the rest of 1776 demolished. From the first
campaign one would expect a rebellion that could out-engineer the empire to
hold its own; the record of August through December is instead a chain of
defeats that brought the Continental Army within days of dissolution.

### Long Island and the East River

The British response to Boston was the largest expeditionary force Britain
had ever sent abroad: through the summer of 1776 some 32,000 troops,
Hessians included, assembled at New York on more than 400 ships. Washington
moved his army there to contest the harbor. On August 27, on Long Island,
Howe turned the American flank through an unguarded pass and routed the
forward divisions; the survivors fell back to fortifications on Brooklyn
Heights with the East River at their backs and the Royal Navy expected in
it.

The navy never arrived. Contrary winds kept the frigates out of the river,
and on the night of August 29–30 the regiment of John Glover, fishermen
from Marblehead, Massachusetts, ferried about 9,000 men, with guns and horses,
across a mile of water in silence; a fog that settled at dawn covered the
last boats. Washington crossed with the rear guard. The army had escaped
annihilation by one night's wind and one morning's weather.

This course uses *contingency* for outcomes decided by narrow, datable
events (a wind, a fog, a vote margin, a week of sea power) rather than by
structural balance alone. The category is analytic, not romantic: every
contingent escape in this lesson sits inside structural conditions (space,
alliance politics, British overreach) that made escape usable. Later
lessons apply the same category to the ratification votes, the election of
1800, and Atlanta in 1864.
</Remark>

### The retreat and the enlistment clock

What followed the escape was worse. New York City fell on September 15.
On November 16 Fort Washington, the last American position on Manhattan,
surrendered with nearly 3,000 men: the war's largest American loss to
that date. The remainder retreated across New Jersey into Pennsylvania,
shedding deserters, while General Charles Cornwallis followed.

By early December about **3,000 effectives** remained with the main army —
the number the strength curve below bottoms out at — and the remnants of
two other commands that reached camp before Christmas roughly doubled the
force without changing its deadline: most enlistments, written for one
year, expired on December 31, after which the Continental Army would
largely cease to exist as a matter of contract.

<Aside>
The enlistment cliff was a cultural institution, not a failure of nerve.
Fred Anderson's *A People's Army* (1984), a study of Massachusetts soldiers
in the Seven Years' War, shows that New England soldiers treated enlistment
as a covenant: a contract for a stated term, stated pay, and a stated
commander, binding on both sides and void when breached or expired. Men who
marched home on the day their term ended were enforcing an agreement, in
the same covenant logic that built
<Ref lesson="new-england-covenant">the region's towns and churches</Ref>.
Washington's problem in December 1776 was that his army's legal culture was
working exactly as designed.
</Aside>

On December 19 a Philadelphia newspaper printed the first number of Thomas
Paine's [*The American Crisis*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3741),
written on the retreat; its opening line, "These are the times that try
men's souls," was read in the camps along the Delaware within the week.
The period's own framing of the moment survives in that sentence: the
question in late December was not whether the army could win but whether it
would exist in January.

## Ten days: Trenton, Assunpink, Princeton

Washington's answer was to spend the army before it expired. Across the
river from his camps lay the outposts of the British winter line in New
Jersey; the nearest, at Trenton, held a Hessian brigade of about 1,500
under Colonel Johann Rall.

### The crossing

The plan for Christmas night committed
everything: three columns crossing the Delaware at three points, with the
watchword "Victory or Death."

Only one column made it. Ice and a rising nor'easter of snow and sleet
stopped the two supporting crossings, cutting the attack by some 3,000 men;
the main force of **2,400**, with 18 guns, crossed in Durham boats, the
40- to 60-foot flat-bottomed cargo vessels of the iron trade, and completed
the landing around 3 a.m., three hours behind schedule.

The planned pre-dawn
surprise was gone; the columns marched nine miles and attacked Trenton in
daylight, in the storm, at about eight in the morning of December 26.

### Two hours in the streets

The garrison fought in the streets and lost in under two hours. Knox's guns
commanded the two main avenues; the Hessians who formed in the open were
broken by volleys from houses and fences. The judgment the ledger below
supports: a 1,500-man garrison effectively destroyed for **two American
dead**, both of exposure on the night march — enemy fire killed no one.

### The myth against the record

A popular legend holds that the garrison was helpless with Christmas drink.
It was not, and the evidence is an eyewitness on the American side: John
Greenwood, a fifer who guarded the prisoners, wrote that "not a drop of
liquor was drunk during the whole night."

The garrison collapsed for
documented military reasons: surprise in a storm no one expected an army to
move through, three prior days of American raids that had kept the Hessians
under arms and exhausted, and Rall's refusal to fortify the town. The
drunk-Hessian story survives because it flatters everyone, the victors'
daring and the losers' excuse alike, which is precisely why it needs the
eyewitness against it.

One detail of the legend is real: a Loyalist
messenger reached Rall's Christmas supper with a written warning that
American columns were on the road, and the note was found, unread, in
Rall's pocket after he fell. The story rests on postwar testimony, carried
by the campaign's historians as a corroborated anecdote.

<Example title="pricing the Trenton exchange">
The exchange ratio is computable from the ledger. Hessian losses in
killed, wounded, and captured: roughly $22 + 83 + 900 \approx 1{,}000$,
the effective destruction of a 1,500-man garrison. American deaths: 2.
As a ratio of men lost, the action ran about 500 to 1 in the attackers'
favor. The ratio is still the wrong measure of what was won.

Militarily the haul was small: Britain could replace 900 hired soldiers,
and did. The decisive values were political, and each can be named. First,
existence: the victory, followed by a ten-dollar bounty and a personal
appeal from Washington, persuaded enough veterans to re-enlist past
December 31 that the army legally survived. Second, momentum: New Jersey
militia turned out through January to harry British outposts, and the
British winter line contracted 60 miles. Third, credibility: the news
reached European courts weighing whether the rebellion was a going
concern, the same calculation Saratoga would settle ten months later.
The worked rule, reused in the exercises: a battle's value is set by what
it changes politically, not by its casualty count.
</Example>

### Assunpink and Princeton

The ten days closed in the same register. Washington recrossed the
Delaware, and on January 2 held the line of Assunpink Creek at Trenton
against Cornwallis's counterattack; that night the army slipped around the
British flank by back roads and struck the garrison at Princeton on the
morning of January 3. General Hugh Mercer, unhorsed and refusing surrender,
was bayoneted and died nine days later; Washington rallied the broken
vanguard in the open field.

The British abandoned most of New Jersey and
concentrated near New York, and the strategic posture inverted: before the
crossing the British were attacking a dissolving army, and after Princeton
they stood on the defensive in the state they had just overrun.

About 900 Hessians were captured at Trenton against two American dead, both
of exposure; the exchange's military weight was small and its political
weight decisive — the army re-enlisted past December 31, and the war

## Leutze against the record: 1851's memory of 1776's night

The crossing produced the most reproduced image of the Revolution, and the
image is evidence — of the wrong year.

### Metadata first: Düsseldorf, 1851

The source pass this course has
drilled since <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the first lesson</Ref>
starts with metadata: *Washington Crossing the Delaware* was painted by
Emanuel Leutze, a German-American working in Düsseldorf, in 1850–51,
two years after the failed European revolutions of 1848, for an audience of
European liberals and the American public; the
[twelve-by-twenty-one-foot canvas](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/11417)
drew crowds when exhibited in New York in 1851.

### The canvas against the record

Checked against the record, the canvas fails on its documentary claims. The
boat is wrong: the army crossed in Durham boats, 40 to 60 feet of
flat-bottomed cargo hull, not the crowded rowboat Leutze painted. The flag
is wrong: the Stars and Stripes it displays was not adopted until June
1777, six months after the crossing. The light is wrong: the crossing was
made in darkness and storm, not against a bright dawn.

One ridiculed detail
is defensible: men really did stand, because a working Durham boat's
bilge ran with icy water.

What the painting records accurately is 1851:
a German liberal telling audiences on both continents that a republic's
cause, lately lost in Europe, had once crossed an icy river and won. Read
as evidence of 1776 it is unreliable; read as evidence of what the
Revolution meant to the mid-nineteenth century it is first-rate, and the
course will apply the same inversion to Civil War monuments later.

<KeyFact>
A history painting is a primary source for the year it was painted, not the
year it depicts: Leutze's canvas documents 1851's politics of memory, and
the record of 1776 (boats, flag, darkness) contradicts it point by point.
</KeyFact>

## Saratoga: the proof France was waiting for

Trenton kept the war alive; Saratoga changed whose war it was. Britain's
plan for 1777 sent General John Burgoyne south from Canada up the Lake
Champlain corridor toward Albany, expecting support from Howe's army in New
York. Howe instead sailed for Philadelphia and took it; Burgoyne, unaided,
was worn down in two battles near Saratoga on the upper Hudson and
surrendered his entire army on October 17, 1777: about **5,900 men**
marching into captivity.

### The audience in Paris

The surrender's decisive audience was in Paris. Benjamin Franklin had been
there since 1776 seeking an alliance; the French foreign ministry, eager to
reverse the verdict of the Seven Years' War, required proof that the
rebellion could destroy British armies rather than merely evade them.
Saratoga was the proof.

On February 6, 1778, France signed two treaties
with the United States: a commercial treaty and a
[Treaty of Alliance](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/fr1788-2.asp)
binding each party to fight until American independence was secured, with
neither making a separate peace. France entered the war that summer; Spain
joined France in 1779; Britain declared war on the Dutch Republic in 1780.

  src="./figures/war-global-1778.svg"

### One front among four

French entry changed the war's arithmetic more than its battles. Britain
now defended the Caribbean sugar islands (worth more in trade than the
thirteen colonies), plus Gibraltar, under Franco-Spanish siege from 1779 to
1783, plus India and the home waters, against the combined Bourbon fleets.
The Royal Navy remained the best in the world and could no longer be
strong everywhere at once.

Philadelphia was evacuated in June 1778 to
concentrate force; the American theater became one front among several; and
British strategy shifted south, betting on Loyalist support in Georgia and
the Carolinas. In the entire war the Royal Navy lost local command of
American waters exactly once. The next section but one is about that once.

<KeyFact>
Saratoga's 5,900 prisoners mattered as proof more than as men: within four
months France had signed an alliance pledged to American independence, and
Britain's colonial rebellion became a four-front world war its navy could
not dominate everywhere at once.
</KeyFact>

## Three wars in one: neighbors, nations, and the enslaved

The account so far is the war between armies, and it undercounts the war.
Three other conflicts ran inside it, each with its own record.

### Neighbors: the civil war among colonists

Perhaps a fifth of white colonists sided with the Crown, and some 60,000
Loyalists ultimately left, about one person in forty
(<Ref lesson="american-revolution">the paradox section</Ref> established
both figures). The exile total measures the losing side of what was, on
the ground, a civil war.

<Figure
  src="./figures/loyalist-exile.svg"
  alt="Two small charts: a bar dividing white colonists into a Loyalist fifth and a patriot-or-divided remainder, and a grid of forty dots with one marked, representing the roughly 60,000 exiles as about one person in forty, bound for Nova Scotia, Quebec, Britain, or the Caribbean"
  caption="The civil war's losing side, tallied: perhaps one white colonist in five Loyalist, and about one colonist in forty ultimately in exile."

The southern campaign made it explicit: after British field
armies took Georgia and the Carolinas in 1778–80, the interior collapsed
into partisan fighting between Loyalist and patriot militias, neighbor
against neighbor and family against family, with reprisal killings on both
sides. The Treaty of Paris would recommend restoring confiscated Loyalist
property; the states ignored the clause, and the exiles stayed exiled, in
Nova Scotia, Quebec, Britain, and the Caribbean.

### Nations: the war for the interior

The Native interior chose sides on the logic
<Ref lesson="natives-and-english">this course has used before</Ref>: each
nation's choice was rational on its own information. The deepest split ran
through the Haudenosaunee, the Iroquois confederacy south of Lake Ontario
that <Ref lesson="indigenous-america">the first lesson</Ref> introduced,
six member nations by this date. One of the six, the Oneida, declared
neutrality in June 1775, describing the conflict as a quarrel between "two
brothers," and later fought beside the Americans; most of the confederacy
fought with Britain.

The major nations beyond the confederacy made the same choice as its
majority: the Shawnee of the Ohio Valley, the Cherokee of the southern
Appalachian interior, and the Creek of present-day Georgia and Alabama
sided with Britain, the power whose
<Ref lesson="awakening-and-empire">Proclamation Line</Ref> had at least
nominally restrained settler expansion.

<Figure
  src="./figures/haudenosaunee-split.svg"
  alt="Diagram of the Six Nations' choices: Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga grouped as fighting with Britain; the Oneida (neutral from June 1775, then allied) and most Tuscarora grouped as fighting with the Americans; below, a band stating that beyond the confederacy the Shawnee, Cherokee, and Creek also chose Britain"
  caption="The war ran through the confederacy itself: four nations with Britain, the Oneida and most Tuscarora with the Americans, and the major interior nations with Britain."
/>

The frontier war was waged on
subsistence: in 1779 a Continental expedition under General John Sullivan
burned some forty Haudenosaunee towns and their winter corn across central
and western New York. At the peace,
the cost of the losing bet was written in silence: the treaty transferred
sovereignty over the land east of the Mississippi without mentioning the
nations who lived on it or had fought for it.

### The enslaved: the third war

For enslaved people the war was an opening, and the record of what they did
with it is established:
<Ref lesson="american-revolution">the previous lesson</Ref> put Dunmore's
proclamation of 1775 and the historians' estimate of **30,000 to 100,000**
wartime escapes to British lines on the table. The range is wide for two
reasons: people counted as property generate inventories, not registries
of flight; and thousands died of smallpox and fever in the camps they fled
to.

What the documentary floor
looks like is one ledger: the "Book of Negroes," compiled at New York in
1783, lists by name, age, and former enslaver roughly **3,000** Black men,
women, and children evacuated with the British to Nova Scotia, recorded
because the peace treaty barred "carrying away any Negroes," and the
British commander, holding that people freed by proclamation were not
property to be returned, ordered them listed and shipped anyway.

Black men
fought on the American side as well, most visibly in Rhode Island's
integrated regiment; at Yorktown a French officer sketching the allied army
drew a Black light-infantryman first among his four American types.

<KeyFact>
The Treaty of Paris moved the United States boundary to the Mississippi
across lands whose Native owners its text never mentions, and the Book of
Negroes names roughly 3,000 people who left New York with the British
rather than remain enslaved in the republic they were leaving.
</KeyFact>

## Yorktown: seven weeks without the Royal Navy

Contingency cut both ways, and the southern war proved it before it ended
the war. At Charleston in May 1780 the British captured an entire American
army of more than 5,000, the war's worst American defeat, and the ledger
logic of Trenton ran in reverse: a whole field force gone at a stroke. But
the southern strategy bled in the interior fighting, and in 1781 Cornwallis
marched his army into Virginia and fortified Yorktown, a tobacco port on
the Chesapeake, to rest and resupply by sea. His safety assumed the one
constant of the war: that the sea belonged to Britain.

<Figure
  src="./figures/chesapeake-trap.svg"
  alt="Schematic map of the 1781 campaign: Washington and Rochambeau's armies marching 450 miles south from New York toward the Chesapeake; Cornwallis fortified at Yorktown inside the bay with about 8,000 men; de Grasse's 28 ships of the line arriving from the Caribbean and lying across the bay mouth from August 30 to October 19; and Graves's 19 ships shown sailing down from New York and turning back on September 5"
  caption="The trap as geography: two armies closing by land, one fleet across the bay mouth, and the Royal Navy turned back at the Capes."
/>

For seven weeks it did not. Admiral de Grasse brought the French Caribbean
fleet (28 ships of the line and 3,000 troops) into the Chesapeake on
August 30, 1781, while Washington and the French general Rochambeau
force-marched their armies 450 miles south from New York in secrecy. On
September 5 the British fleet under Admiral Graves, 19 ships of the line
against 24, fought de Grasse off the Virginia Capes; the battle was
tactically indecisive and strategically total, because Graves turned back
to New York to refit and the bay stayed French. Cornwallis was sealed off.

The rest was siege arithmetic. About 19,000 French and American troops
invested some 8,000 British; the trenches opened September 28, the heavy
guns fired from October 9, and the outlying redoubts 9 and 10 fell to
night assaults on October 14. On **October 19, 1781** Cornwallis
surrendered roughly **7,500** soldiers and seamen: the second entire
British army lost in four years. The relief fleet from New York sailed
that day and arrived off the Capes on October 24, five days late.

In London the surrender decided the war's politics: on February 27, 1782
the Commons voted to end offensive war in America, and in March the
ministry of Lord North, Britain's prime minister since 1770, fell.

### What Britain lost, stated precisely

The war ended by negotiation, and the terms measure what was and was not
decided. The [Treaty of Paris](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/paris.asp),
signed September 3, 1783, recognized American independence, drew the
boundary at the Mississippi, and confirmed American fishing rights off
Newfoundland; its clauses on Loyalist property were ignored by the states,
and its silence on Native nations has already been noted.

What Britain did
not lose is equally part of the record: it kept Canada, held Gibraltar
through the siege, and beat de Grasse's fleet decisively in the Caribbean
in April 1782, six months after Yorktown. Britain did not collapse; it
concluded that one theater of a four-front world war no longer justified
its cost, and cut the loss. The empire that walked away intact is the same
one the republic meets again in
<Ref lesson="jeffersonian-america">the War of 1812</Ref>.

### Contingency and structure: both necessary, neither sufficient

The war's outcome supports a precise causal claim, not a romantic one. The
structural conditions (space, French power, British overreach) were
necessary: no fog saves an army with nowhere to retreat to, and no crossing
wins a war without an ally's navy six years later.

The contingencies were
equally necessary: the wind and fog of August 30, the ten days in the snow,
the one autumn the Royal Navy lost a bay: remove any of them and the
structure has nothing to work through.

Washington's generalship belongs in
the same register: he lost most of his set-piece battles (Long Island,
White Plains, Brandywine, Germantown), and the record supports a different
excellence: he kept an army in existence for eight years, deferred to the
civil power while doing it, and held the alliance together. The war cost
about 25,000 American dead, roughly one in a hundred of the population:
proportionally deadlier than any later American war except the Civil War.

<KeyFact>
Structure and contingency were jointly necessary and separately
insufficient: space and French power set the conditions, and the datable
accidents — the fog of August 30, the ten days in the snow, seven weeks of
a French bay — decided when the conditions produced independence.
</KeyFact>

What is now established: the Revolution was won at measurable odds (about
900 prisoners for two dead at Trenton, 5,900 at Saratoga priced into a
French alliance, seven weeks of lost sea power priced into 7,500 at
Yorktown), by structure and contingency jointly, in a war that was also a
civil war among colonists, a losing war for most Native belligerents, and
an emancipation route for thousands of enslaved people. Independence in
hand, the problem became constitutional: the colonies' institutions of
self-government had scaled from town to province, and no republic in the
historical record had governed a continent.

answer: a design deliberately assembled from classical Roman and English
constitutional materials.

<Exercise>
Apply the pricing rule from the worked example to Saratoga. The haul was
about 5,900 prisoners against Trenton's 900, six and a half times larger.
State (a) what Saratoga was worth militarily, (b) what it was worth
politically, and (c) why this lesson nonetheless treats Trenton, the
smaller victory, as the war's survival event and Saratoga as its
internationalization event.

<Solution>
(a) Militarily Saratoga eliminated one of Britain's two field armies in
North America and closed the Champlain–Hudson invasion route: a real but
recoverable loss for an empire that could hire and ship replacements.

(b) Politically it was the single most valuable event of the war: it was
the demonstrated proof the French ministry had explicitly waited for:
that the rebellion could force the surrender of a British army, not merely
evade one. Within four months France signed the commercial treaty and the
Treaty of Alliance, pledged to American independence with no separate
peace, which converted Britain's one-front colonial war into a four-front
world war and produced, at Yorktown, the fleet that ended it.

(c) The two battles answer different questions. Trenton answered whether
an American army would exist in January 1777: without it the enlistment
expiry of December 31 dissolves the force, and there is no army for
Saratoga's campaign season. Saratoga answered whether anyone else would
pay for the war. Sequence matters: survival is the precondition of
internationalization, which is why the smaller ledger is the more
existential one. The rule from the worked example holds in both cases:
value is set by what a battle changes politically, not by its count of
prisoners.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the full source pass on Trumbull's *The Death of General Mercer at the
Battle of Princeton* (painted from about 1787 onward). (a) Metadata:
painter, dates, purpose, audience. (b) Prediction from the metadata: what
would you expect it to get right, and what wrong? (c) One thing it records,
one thing it argues, one thing it omits. (d) Use: state what the painting
can and cannot establish, and compare its evidentiary standing with
Leutze's canvas.

<Solution>
(a) John Trumbull, a Continental Army veteran and former aide to
Washington, painted Princeton in versions from about 1787 into the early
nineteenth century, as part of a deliberate program of national history
painting for American public memory, including Capitol commissions.

(b) A veteran painting officers he knew, years later, for national
commemoration: expect reliable likenesses, uniforms, and equipment, and
expect the event's chaos reorganized into a legible martyrdom scene with
the composition centered on heroic death and command presence.

(c) Records: the individual likenesses (many painted from life), the
dress and weapons of both armies, and who was present. Argues: that
Mercer's death was a noble sacrifice under Washington's eye: martyrdom
and leadership as the meaning of Princeton. Omits: the rout of the
American vanguard that preceded the rally, the confusion of a
meeting engagement, and the ordinary killing that had no compositional
role.

(d) It can establish what participants and their generation wanted
Princeton to mean, and it preserves genuinely documentary details
(faces, uniforms) unavailable elsewhere; it cannot establish the event's
actual choreography, which it subordinates to composition. Its standing
differs from Leutze's in degree, not kind: Trumbull is closer to the
events (an eyewitness generation, painting from life) but both canvases
are primary sources for the politics of memory at their moment of
production (Trumbull's early republic, Leutze's 1851), and neither is a
record of the battlefield.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the following as necessary, enabling, or precipitating conditions of
British defeat by 1783, defending each placement in a sentence: (1) the
3,000 miles of ocean and the continental theater; (2) French naval and
financial intervention; (3) the ten days of Trenton and Princeton; (4)
Britain's decision to bet the southern campaign on Loyalist support. Name
one placement a reasonable reader could dispute, and why.

<Solution>
(1) Space is **necessary**: it made every British campaign an
ocean-supplied occupation, made the rebellion's survival strategy (trade
territory for time, keep the army in being) possible at all, and meant no
captured city ended the war.

(2) French intervention is **necessary**: the rebellion had no navy, and
Yorktown, the surrender that broke Parliament's will, required local
naval superiority that only France ever supplied; French loans also
carried Congress's finances.

(3) The ten days are **precipitating** for everything downstream: they
converted the army's legal dissolution on December 31 into survival,
without which there is no 1777 campaign, no Saratoga, and nothing for
France to back. (They are not sufficient: a survival event, not a
victory event.)

(4) The southern bet is **enabling** of the specific end state: it
committed a British field army to the interior on an overestimate of
Loyalist strength, ground it down in a civil war, and delivered it to a
coastal cul-de-sac dependent on sea control that lapsed.

Disputable placement: (3) can be argued as necessary rather than
precipitating, on the ground that the December 1776 dissolution would have
ended the war outright; the counter is that "necessary" is better reserved
for standing conditions rather than events, and that some later rebuilding
of a force was conceivable, which is exactly the kind of judgment the
necessary/enabling/precipitating vocabulary exists to make explicit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The historians' estimate for wartime escapes by enslaved people spans
30,000 to 100,000, while the Book of Negroes records roughly 3,000 names.
Using the sourcing discipline of this course and the paradox established
in <Ref lesson="american-revolution">the previous lesson</Ref>, state
(a) what the Book of Negroes can establish, (b) why it cannot bound the
total, and (c) what the width of the 30,000–100,000 range itself documents
about the archive of slavery.

<Solution>
(a) The ledger establishes a documented floor and its texture: at least
about 3,000 named individuals, with ages and former enslavers, left New
York with the British in 1783 as free people: proof that the British
freedom promise was honored at evacuation for this group, over American
treaty objections, and proof of exactly who some of the self-emancipated
were.

(b) It records one port's evacuation in one year. It excludes those
evacuated from Charleston and Savannah, those who died of smallpox and
fever in wartime camps (a large share of Dunmore's recruits), those who
reached British lines but were not evacuated, those re-enslaved, and
those who fled without ever reaching British lines. A floor from one
ledger cannot bound a total generated across six years and every theater.

(c) The range's width is itself evidence about the archive: enslaved
people enter colonial records as property (inventories, sale deeds,
losses claimed by enslavers), not as persons whose movements were
registered. Historians must reconstruct flight from enslavers' claims and
military paperwork, which is why the estimate spans a factor of three.
The paradox from the previous lesson gains its quantitative texture here:
the republic's founding archive counted these people carefully as wealth
and barely at all as actors.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A survey sentence claims: "America won its independence because Washington
was a great battlefield commander." Name two facts established in this
lesson that the claim fails to account for, and state what would have to
be true, but is not, for it to survive. Then state the defensible version.

<Solution>
Two facts. First, the battle record: Washington lost most of his
set-piece engagements (Long Island, White Plains, Brandywine,
Germantown), and his most consequential successes were a logistics
operation (Boston), a raid on an outpost (Trenton), and a siege decided
by a French fleet (Yorktown); the war's largest battlefield surrender of
British troops before Yorktown, Saratoga, happened under other commanders
entirely. Second, the alliance: the victory-forcing instrument, local
naval superiority in the Chesapeake, was French, and no quality of
Washington's tactics could have produced it.

For the claim to survive, the record would need to show Washington
winning the war's decisive pitched battles; it shows him surviving the
losses of them.

The defensible version: Washington was a great *war* commander rather
than a great battle commander: he kept an army legally and physically
in existence for eight years, chose survival over battle after 1776,
subordinated himself to Congress (which made the army politically safe
to maintain), and functioned as the alliance's indispensable partner.
On the asymmetry this lesson opened with (Britain had to conquer,
Washington had only to persist), persistence was the war-winning skill.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A second survey sentence claims: "Yorktown proves the American rebellion
defeated the British Empire." Name three facts from this lesson that force
a more precise statement, write that statement, and say what it predicts
about Anglo-American relations after 1783.

<Solution>
Three facts. First, six months after Yorktown the Royal Navy decisively
defeated de Grasse in the Caribbean (April 1782), and Gibraltar held
through its entire siege: British power was intact outside the American
theater. Second, Britain kept Canada and its Caribbean colonies; the
empire lost one region, not its existence. Third, the decision to stop
was political and fiscal, a Commons vote of February 27, 1782 to end
offensive operations, taken inside a four-front world war in which
America was one front.

Precise statement: the rebellion, once allied to France, raised the cost
of the American theater beyond what Parliament judged the theater worth,
and Britain cut its losses there while defending the rest of the empire
successfully.

Prediction: an undefeated empire with undiminished sea power and
adjacent territory will keep pressing on the republic's commerce,
sailors, and frontier, which is the collision course that produces the
War of 1812.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

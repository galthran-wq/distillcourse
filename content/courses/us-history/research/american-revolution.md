# Research brief: american-revolution (The American Revolution)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Analyze the causes of the Revolution and the paradox of a liberty revolution that preserved slavery.

## Sources to consult

- **American Yawp Ch.5 "The American Revolution"** — the spine: origins and
  causes 1763-75, independence, the war, and its consequences. The chapter states
  the central paradox explicitly (liberty revolution preserving slavery;
  anti-central-power revolt producing a new central state; elite leadership
  dependent on popular mobilization) — that formulation is this lesson's thesis.
- **National Archives, "The Declaration of Independence: A History"** and the
  annotated Declaration transcript — drafting by the Committee of Five, Congress's
  July 2-4 edits (including deletion of the slave-trade passage), Timothy
  Matlack's engrossing, the August 2 signing, and the preservation history.
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 4** — pairs Paine's *Common Sense* with the committee draft
  of the Declaration showing Congress's edits (Maier, *American Scripture*,
  App. C). Also the Fred Anderson *A People's Army* thesis (essay 1): Seven
  Years' War veterans' contractual expectations of military authority shaped
  Revolutionary attitudes — a good "how historians argue" aside.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.4-5** — "Slavery, Freedom, and the Struggle for Empire,
  to 1763" and "The American Revolution, 1763-1783", for the boundaries-of-freedom
  reading.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Imperial finance started the crisis.** After the Seven Years' War Britain's
   national debt reached **13.5 times its annual revenue** (Yawp Ch.5). Sugar Act,
   Stamp Act, Townshend duties, and the Tea Act follow from that arithmetic. Open
   with the number — the lesson's opening sentence depends on it.
2. **Colonial theory of the dispute.** Representation, consent to taxation, and
   the distinction colonists drew between internal and external taxation;
   Parliament's Declaratory Act rejecting the distinction outright.
3. **Escalation, 1770-1775.** Boston Massacre, Tea Party, Coercive Acts, the
   First Continental Congress, Lexington and Concord. Keep the narrative tight;
   the analytic content is that each British response widened colonial unity.
4. **The Declaration as a structured argument, and as an edited document.**
   Three parts: natural-rights premise, 27 grievances offered as proof of a
   design of tyranny, and the operative declaration. Then the editing: Congress
   struck Jefferson's passage indicting the king for the slave trade; 56 men
   signed, most on August 2, not July 4.
5. **The war and its social consequences.** Between **30,000 and 100,000 enslaved
   people self-emancipated by fleeing to British lines** (Yawp Ch.5) — the largest
   emancipation event in North America before 1863, and the fact that most sharply
   frames the paradox. Also: Loyalist exile, Native nations' catastrophic
   position, and the wartime expansion of continental institutions.
6. **The paradox, stated plainly.** The Revolution universalized a rights
   vocabulary it declined to apply universally; it also produced, within a decade,
   demands for a stronger central state than the one it overthrew.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The 13.5x debt ratio** as an opening quantitative hook, built into a small
  figure comparing British debt service against revenue.
- **The struck slave-trade passage** (National Archives essay): show the fact of
  the deletion, note that the Archives essay describes without excerpting it, and
  make the editorial decision itself the object of analysis.
- **30,000-100,000 self-emancipated** (Yawp Ch.5) — set directly beside the
  liberty rhetoric. No commentary needed; the juxtaposition is the argument.
- **Original parchment vs. Stone engraving** (National Archives): the surviving
  original is nearly illegible after 35 years hanging in sunlight; the 1823 Stone
  engraving is what every reproduction actually shows — and the wet-transfer
  process may itself have stripped ink from the original. This is a first-class
  primary-vs-reproduction lesson and belongs in this chapter.
- Optional aside: Anderson's *A People's Army* claim (MIT Week 4) as an example
  of a testable historical thesis.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- "No taxation without representation" was a constitutional argument about
  consent, not a complaint about tax rates — colonial tax burdens were low.
- The Declaration did not create independence; Lee's Resolution (July 2) did. The
  Declaration justified it.
- The Revolution was also a civil war: perhaps a fifth of colonists were Loyalist,
  and Native nations mostly allied with Britain for defensible reasons.
- Do not narrate battles at length; the lesson's goal is causal, not military.
- Careful with the signing date: August 2, 1776, not July 4 — the Archives itself
  corrects this misconception.

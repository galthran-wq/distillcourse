# Research brief: american-revolution (The Imperial Crisis and the Declaration of Independence)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Analyze how imperial finance and the constitutional argument over consent escalated into independence, reading the Declaration as a structured legal argument.

RE-SCOPED under the course re-architecture: this lesson now carries the
CAUSES (1763-1775) and the DECLARATION; the war itself moved to the new
war-of-independence lesson (Trenton, Saratoga, the French alliance, Yorktown,
and the war's social history in numbers). Keep the handoff clean: this lesson
ends at Lexington-Concord and July 1776 plus the paradox stated; battle
narrative belongs next door.

THESIS ROLE. The colonists' argument was institutional: assemblies that had
governed and taxed themselves for a century (module 1's compounding
institutions) refused to be taxed by a body in which they did not sit. The
lesson shows the imperial crisis as a collision between Britain's fiscal
arithmetic and the self-government the colonies already practiced — and the
Declaration as that argument written into the founding promise the rest of
the course tracks (Douglass's "GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT" reading returns in
sectional-crisis).

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Imperial finance started the crisis.** Britain's post-1763 debt at 13.5
   times annual revenue (recap from awakening-and-empire); Sugar Act, Stamp
   Act, Townshend duties, Tea Act as the arithmetic's consequences. The
   opening sentence depends on the number.
2. **The colonial theory of the dispute — an institutional argument.**
   Representation and consent; internal vs external taxation; the colonial
   assemblies' century of practiced self-taxation as the ground of the
   objection (link module 1's polities explicitly); Parliament's Declaratory
   Act rejecting the distinction outright. "No taxation without
   representation" was a constitutional claim about consent, not a complaint
   about rates — colonial tax burdens were low; say so with the comparative
   figure.
3. **Escalation, 1765-1775.** Stamp Act Congress and non-importation (the
   boycott as economic instrument, with women's spinning associations as its
   enforcement), Boston Massacre, Tea Party, Coercive Acts, First
   Continental Congress, Lexington and Concord. Tight narrative; the
   analytic content is that each British response widened colonial unity —
   the disunion of 1754 (Albany) becoming the Association of 1774.
4. **The Declaration as a structured legal argument, and as an edited
   document.** Three parts: natural-rights premise, 27 grievances offered as
   proof of a design of tyranny, operative declaration. The editing:
   Congress struck Jefferson's slave-trade passage; Lee's Resolution
   (July 2) created independence, the Declaration justified it; 56 signed,
   most on August 2.
5. **The paradox, stated plainly and handed forward.** The Revolution
   universalized a rights vocabulary it declined to apply universally — the
   30,000-100,000 self-emancipations to British lines are introduced here as
   the sharpest fact (the war lesson carries the mechanics) — and produced,
   within a decade, demands for a stronger central state than the one
   overthrown. The founding-as-promise register: state the facts and the
   vocabulary side by side; Douglass's later reading of the documents closes
   the loop in sectional-crisis.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Keep the war out: Lexington-Concord is this lesson's last event;
  everything military after April 1775 belongs to war-of-independence.
- The Declaration did not create independence; Lee's Resolution did.
  August 2, not July 4, for most signatures.
- A fifth of colonists were Loyalist and Native nations mostly sided with
  Britain — introduce here in one paragraph as the civil-war reality, then
  hand the full treatment to war-of-independence.
- The paradox must stay stated in facts (documents and numbers side by
  side), not adjudicated by adjective — the register rule.
- The natural-rights premise is Lockean; note it plainly here — it is the
  counterweight the roman-blueprint lesson needs when Wood's republican
  reading arrives (the two vocabularies coexisted).

## Canon additions (curated expansion, July 2026)

- **Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution*** (FULL TEXT
  read; cached `texts/bailyn-clean.txt`; 1967 pagination) — the intellectual
  substructure under this brief's escalation ladder:
  - Ch. III's power-vs-liberty grammar — the catalogue of power-as-predator
    metaphors ("encroaching", "grasping", "jaws... always opened to devour",
    p.56) explains WHY each British measure read as part of a design.
  - Ch. IV + "A Note on Conspiracy" — the colonists strung 1763-76 into one
    deliberate pattern, and Bailyn argues this was period-rational inference,
    not paranoia: the analytic bridge between this brief's "imperial finance"
    opening and the Declaration's "long train of abuses" language.
  - Ch. V.1 — virtual vs actual representation as the constitutional
    argument's worked example (Manchester/Birmingham parallel), directly
    serving the consent-and-representation key idea.
  - Ch. VI — the "contagion of liberty": the same argument-shape turned on
    slavery (Otis 1764: "all men" means "white or black", p.237),
    establishment, and deference — scholarly ground for this brief's paradox
    key idea.
  - Set-piece quotes: Adams 1818, "The Revolution was in the minds and hearts
    of the people... before the war commenced" (Ch. V epigraph, p.160); the
    Adams library bequest naming Sidney, Locke, and Cato's Letters (p.21).
- **Documentary corpora (FREE)** — Founders Online (founders.archives.gov)
  for Adams/Jefferson/Franklin correspondence quoted above; Avalon Project
  (already a course source) for the statutes and state papers of 1763-76.

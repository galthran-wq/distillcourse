# Research brief: awakening-and-empire (The Great Awakening and the Seven Years' War)

status: planned
module: contact-and-colonies
type: core
goal: Explain how the First Great Awakening and the Seven Years' War each bound thirteen separate colonies together and set the terms of the imperial crisis.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From middle-colonies, the immediately preceding lesson, the
  reader arrives with three regional colonial societies established, each
  rich, literate, and self-governing on the strength of a selected population
  and compounding institutions; those societies have not yet acted as one
  public or collided with imperial authority.
- **Contributes:** This lesson supplies the binding: the First Great
  Awakening and the Seven Years' War as the colonies' first shared,
  intercolonial experiences, closing with 1763's fiscal arithmetic —
  Britain's debt at roughly 13.5 times annual revenue — the fact that forces
  the imperial collision.
- **Hands off:** The next lesson, american-revolution (opening the
  Revolution and Founding module), takes up the imperial crisis and
  constitutional argument that 1763's debt set in motion, ending at the
  Declaration of Independence.

THESIS ROLE. The module's hinge lesson: it converts thirteen separate colonial
societies (the previous five lessons) into one connected public with shared
experiences — a revival everyone heard about and a war everyone paid for — and
hands the founding module its opening fact (Britain's 13.5x debt). The
Awakening also feeds the thesis directly: an unusually literate, print-dense
population is what made an intercolonial mass event possible at all.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The Awakening as the first intercolonial mass event.** Whitefield's
   1739-41 tours: preaching to open-air crowds from Georgia to Maine,
   audiences Franklin measured in the tens of thousands, newspapers covering
   him in every colony — the first experience colonists consumed
   simultaneously. The mechanism was the print and literacy infrastructure
   from new-england-covenant plus the pluralist press of middle-colonies:
   connect explicitly.
2. **What the revival changed structurally.** Denominations split (New Light
   vs Old Light) and multiplied; itinerants preached without invitation
   across parish lines; laypeople judged ministers and changed churches.
   Religious authority became something chosen — a habit of judging
   established authority that the imperial crisis would generalize. State
   this as the standard scholarly reading, attributed, not as inevitability.
3. **The war's American beginning.** 1754: a 22-year-old Virginia militia
   colonel named Washington ambushes a French party at Jumonville Glen and
   surrenders Fort Necessity; Braddock's column is destroyed on the
   Monongahela in 1755 with Washington rallying the survivors. The world war
   (Europe, India, the Caribbean, the Philippines) grew from an Ohio Valley
   land dispute — state the escalation plainly, with the Albany Congress
   (1754) and its rejected Plan of Union as the failed first union.
4. **Native nations were parties, not terrain.** The war was fought through
   Native alliance systems (French-allied nations, the Iroquois balance,
   Pontiac's War in 1763 as the coda); the Proclamation Line of 1763 was
   Britain's attempt to stabilize the interior — and became a colonial
   grievance. Cross-reference the alliance logic from natives-and-english.
5. **1763 as the hinge, numbered.** France expelled from the continent;
   Britain's debt at roughly £133 million, about 13.5 times annual revenue;
   a 10,000-man peacetime army to pay for; and colonists who had just
   learned, in Anderson's reading, to see military service as contractual.
   The next module's opening sentence is this lesson's closing fact —
   hand it off explicitly.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Franklin's acoustic computation** as the worked example: reconstruct his
  estimate (semicircle radius within which a voice carries, area, packing
  density → ~30,000 hearers) and have the reader repeat it with different
  assumptions — a period back-of-envelope the course's quantitative register
  can reuse. Source: Autobiography, quoted directly.
- **"Join, or Die" (1754)** read as a source: what the segmented snake
  records (disunion as the default), what it argues (union), and the fact
  that every assembly rejected the Albany Plan — the distance between 1754
  and 1776 measured by one cartoon.
- **The debt arithmetic**: £133M debt against ~£10M annual revenue; the
  interest share of the budget; then the list of revenue acts it generated
  (Sugar, Stamp) as the forward pointer.
- **Whitefield and Franklin as a business story**: the skeptic who printed
  the revival — subscription lists, journal print runs — evidence that the
  Awakening ran on the commercial press.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not overclaim the Awakening→Revolution causal line: it is a scholarly
  interpretation (attribute it), supported by structural evidence
  (denominational splits, itinerancy, lay judgment), not a documented chain
  of intent. State it as the standard reading with its evidence.
- "First" Great Awakening: distinguish explicitly from the Second
  (religion-and-reform lesson) — different era, different theology, different
  consequences; one sentence.
- The Seven Years' War is the war's British-American name; note French and
  Indian War as the American usage once.
- Washington's 1754 role must be stated factually (ambush, capitulation
  document he signed unread in French, admitting "assassination") — the
  future commander's first war was a debacle he learned from; no hagiography,
  no gotcha.
- Pontiac's War and the Proclamation Line belong to the imperial-crisis
  setup; keep them tight here, the grievance analysis lives in
  american-revolution.

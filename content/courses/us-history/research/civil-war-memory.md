# Research brief: civil-war-memory (The Civil War in American Memory)

status: planned
module: civil-war-and-reconstruction
type: extra
goal: Evaluate how reconciliationist memory eclipsed emancipationist memory of the Civil War.

`extra` (starred) chapter. No core lesson may require it; it deepens
redemption and civil-war-memory themes for readers who want the historiography.

THESIS ROLE. The course's memory-methods chapter: how the record established
in the previous five lessons was later renarrated, and how to date a memory
artifact against the events it depicts. For the thesis it guards the
register — the Lost Cause is the cautionary case of a national story built by
selection against the documents, and the lesson's method (check the memory
claims against the secession declarations, monument dates against
disenfranchisement dates) is the same evidence-first discipline the course
applies to its own thesis in historiographical-lenses.

## Sources to consult

- **Blight, HIST 119 Lectures 26-27** — "Race and Reunion: the Civil War in
  American memory" and "Legacies of the Civil War". The tripartite framework
  (reconciliationist / emancipationist / white supremacist memory) is the
  lesson's analytic spine, together with the claim that national reunion was
  purchased by erasing emancipation's meaning.
- **MIT 21H.102 Session 2 lecture notes** — Lost Cause memory: Colfax recast as a
  "riot", Confederate memorialization (Stone Mountain), Wilson's 1913 Blue-Gray
  reunion, against Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction* and emancipationist memory.
- **Foner, *Reconstruction*, Epilogue** — the long afterlife and the link to the
  civil rights era; also the Dunning School's role as academic Lost Cause.
- **American Yawp Ch.15-17** — for the reunion-era context.
- **Blight's John Brown material (Lecture 9)** — the competing visual "legends"
  of Brown as a case study in image-driven memory.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Three memory traditions.** Emancipationist (the war as a revolution in
   freedom, held by Black Americans and abolitionists); reconciliationist (the
   war as a tragic family quarrel between equally brave white soldiers); white
   supremacist (the Lost Cause: a noble Confederacy, a benign slavery, and
   Reconstruction as misrule). The second and third fused; the first was pushed
   out of public commemoration.
2. **How the fusion happened.** Veterans' reunions; the Blue-Gray reunions
   culminating at Gettysburg in 1913 with Wilson presiding and no Black veterans
   present as participants; the memorialization wave of 1890-1920 and again in
   the 1950s-60s (dates matter: monuments cluster in the disenfranchisement era
   and the civil rights era, not in the 1870s).
3. **Memory built in artifacts.** Stone Mountain (begun 1915, the largest
   Confederate memorial); the United Daughters of the Confederacy's textbook
   campaigns; the Dunning School's academic version; *Birth of a Nation* (1915)
   and its political effect.
4. **The erasures, documented.** The first Memorial Day: Black Charlestonians
   honoring Union dead on May 1, 1865 — later nationalized and stripped of its
   Black origin. The 1876 Centennial Exposition, attended by roughly a quarter of
   the US population, celebrating industrial progress while Reconstruction was
   being overthrown by violence in Mississippi.
5. **Images make myths.** John Brown as a case: the 1859 photograph, Hovenden's
   1882-87 painting inventing "the legend of the kiss" two decades after the
   fact, and later reinterpretations. The painting is evidence of memory, not of
   1859.
6. **Why it matters analytically.** The memory settlement made Jim Crow
   nationally tolerable; the monuments' construction dates are evidence of what
   the monuments were for; and modern disputes over them are disputes about
   which of the three traditions the public commemorates.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Hovenden's painting analyzed**: the invented kiss, the composition, the
  audience it was painted for, and the date gap. Worked example: distinguish what
  the painting documents (1880s memory) from what it depicts (1859).
  Parallel exercise: apply the same distinction to Stone Mountain or to the
  Carpenter cabinet engraving from the emancipation lesson.
- **The monument-dedication timeline**: dedications per year 1865-1970 with the
  two clusters visible, set against disenfranchisement and civil-rights dates.
- **Douglass's "rope of sand"** and his Lazarus line — the emancipationist voice
  refusing the settlement.
- **The 1913 Gettysburg reunion** as a staged reconciliation with a documented
  exclusion.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Memory analysis is not a substitute for the history: keep the factual record
  (established in the previous five lessons) clearly separate from claims about
  how it was later narrated.
- The Lost Cause is a set of claims that can be checked against evidence
  (especially the secession declarations from sectional-crisis) — check them,
  rather than treating it as merely an alternative perspective.
- Do not present all commemoration as bad faith; reconciliation had genuine
  motives for veterans, which is exactly what made the settlement durable.
- Attribute Blight's tripartite framework to him; it is an interpretive
  scheme, not a natural category.
- Contemporary monument controversies are outside this course's scope: state the
  historical evidence about construction dates and purposes and stop there.

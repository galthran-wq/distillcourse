## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From natives-and-english, the immediately preceding lesson,
  the reader arrives having seen New England's selected population, its
  institutions, and the war that secured its land; the Chesapeake and its
  labor system remain to be placed beside it.
- **Contributes:** This lesson supplies the comparative synthesis — two
  economies, two demographies, two polities (assembly and county court
  against covenanted town) — and traces the legal turn to hereditary racial
  slavery to its statutory core, completing the settler-selection measurement
  common to both regions.
- **Hands off:** The next lesson, middle-colonies, takes up a third English
  society — New York and Pennsylvania — as the pluralist, commercial variant
  on the same compounding institutions.

THESIS ROLE. The module's comparative synthesis. New-england-covenant
established the selected population and its institutions; this lesson sets the
other English society beside it and shows that the differences were systematic:
who migrated and how long they lived (two demographies), what they produced
and how labor was bound (two economies), and how they governed (two polities).
The settler-selection measurements complete here: by the eighteenth century
free colonists in BOTH regions were among the best-paid ordinary people in the
world — the comparison explains the labor systems, not a difference in
prosperity. The statute ladder stays as documented causal history at its core
(term and heredity removed for one class of laborers), stated in the statutes'
own words but compressed; the full rung-by-rung treatment moves to exercises
and the References.

## Key points the revised lesson must cover

1. **Two demographies (kept as drafted).** The Chesapeake's mortality regime
   (120,000 migrants → 90,000 people by 1700) against New England's natural
   increase (21,000 → 91,000), with the Dane family as the document window.
   Recap new-england-covenant's numbers by cross-reference, not repetition.
2. **Two economies.** Tobacco monoculture for export with bound labor and
   riverfront plantations against diversified farms, fish, timber, and
   shipping with family labor; the headright against the town grant as the
   land systems. The wage measurement: by the eighteenth century free
   colonial laborers and farmers out-earned their English counterparts —
   land-to-labor ratios made ordinary people rich and made bound labor
   profitable, the same price structure read from two sides.
3. **Two polities — the new weight.** Virginia: royal governor, council,
   elected House of Burgesses (1619, oldest continuous legislature in
   America), county courts and parish vestries run by the gentry.
   New England: the covenanted town and town meeting under an elected
   General Court. Both were self-governing by London's standards — the
   assembly-versus-town-meeting contrast is a difference in WHO participated
   and at what level, not in whether colonists governed themselves. Salutary
   neglect as the imperial condition that let both compound (forward pointer
   to awakening-and-empire and american-revolution).
4. **The turn to slavery, at its causal core.** The ledger mechanism (kept
   as drafted: falling mortality flipped the servant/slave purchase), the
   pre-statute ambiguity (Johnson, Punch, Key in brief), and the two
   load-bearing statutes quoted — 1662 (heredity) and 1705 (consolidation) —
   with 1667/1691 compressed to a sentence each. Bacon's Rebellion kept as
   the political accelerant with Morgan's reading. The full six-rung ladder
   becomes an exercise walk-through rather than five prose sections.
5. **One Atlantic economy by 1750 (kept, tightened).** The gentry South and
   farm-merchant North as shores of one economy; New England's slave-trade
   participation stated with the Rhode Island numbers; the corrected
   (non-triangle) flow diagram kept.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Keep both original pitfalls sets: no "1619 fully-formed slavery"
  shorthand, no slavery-free New England, indenture ≠ slavery (term and
  heredity), no teleology, Dane as providence narrative.
- New: do not let the two-polities section become civics celebration — state
  the franchises' actual boundaries (church membership, property, race, sex)
  in the same paragraphs as the institutions.
- New: the wage claim is about free colonists; say so in the same sentence,
  and keep the bound-labor connection explicit (high wages for the free and
  bound labor for the rest were one price structure).
- Compression must not soften the record: the 1662 and 1705 quotations stay
  verbatim; what shrinks is rung count, not clarity about what was built.

## Canon additions (curated expansion, July 2026)

- **Fischer, *Albion's Seed*** (summary-based; cached `texts/albionseed-*`;
  an influential but contested synthesis — Sosin/McKinney/Zelinsky critiques
  on sourcing and cultural determinism; flag it as interpretation) — the
  strongest available frame for this lesson's two-societies comparison,
  extended to four: East Anglia→Massachusetts (ordered liberty),
  south-of-England Cavaliers and servants→Virginia (hegemonic liberty,
  Berkeley's royalist government, 40,000-50,000 indentured servants), North
  Midlands Quakers→Delaware Valley, borderers→backcountry. The ~24-'ways'
  comparative grid (speech, family, child-rearing, rank, liberty) is a
  ready-made comparison-table figure; the closing four-liberties-in-tension
  claim (pp.897-98) is a strong capstone quotation if page-verified. Material
  color for the Chesapeake side: red high heels and plumed hats vs Puritan
  pea porridge — verify before quoting.

# Research brief: colonial-societies (Colonial Societies: Chesapeake, New England, and the Turn to Slavery)

status: planned
module: contact-and-colonies
type: core
goal: Compare the Chesapeake and New England colonial societies and trace the legal turn from indentured servitude to hereditary racial slavery.

Lesson 2 of the course (C3): still an easy opener. The win is that the reader can
say precisely *when and how* slavery became racial and hereditary in English
America — a question most people cannot answer.

## Sources to consult

- **American Yawp Ch.2 "Colliding Cultures"** (Jamestown, New England) and
  **Ch.3 "British North America"** — colonial settlement patterns, indentured
  servitude, and the turn to racial slavery. Ch.3 is the core chapter here.
- **American Yawp Ch.4 "Colonial Society"** — the Atlantic economy and the
  colonial social hierarchy; use lightly, only for the eighteenth-century payoff.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.2-3** ("European Colonies and Native Nations, 1600-1660";
  "The Rise of Anglo-America, 1660-1750") — the freedom-boundaries framing:
  English liberty defined partly against the unfreedom of servants and slaves.
- **MIT 21H.101 Weeks 1-3** — the Dane family opener (John Dane's spiritual
  autobiography plus the printed Dane pedigree chart) and the "Chesapeake and
  New England" comparative week. The whole pedagogical device — start from one
  ordinary family's documents — is worth borrowing for this lesson's opening.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Two demographies, two societies.** Chesapeake: overwhelmingly male,
   high mortality, migrants arriving as indentured servants for tobacco. New
   England: family migration, low mortality, rapid natural increase, town-and-
   church settlement. Nearly everything else about the two societies follows.
2. **Indentured servitude was the Chesapeake's first labor system.** Terms of
   4-7 years, passage in exchange for labor, freedom dues at the end; a servant
   was unfree but temporarily and not heritably so. Many died before their term
   ended.
3. **The legal construction of racial slavery.** Trace the statutes: the 1662
   Virginia law making a child's status follow the mother (partus sequitur
   ventrem); 1667 baptism no longer conferring freedom; the 1705 slave code
   consolidating the regime. The point is that hereditary racial slavery was
   *built by legislation*, decade by decade, not inherited whole from 1619.
4. **Why the turn happened.** Falling English emigration, rising servant
   survival rates (making lifetime slavery more valuable), Bacon's Rebellion
   (1676) demonstrating the danger of a large class of armed poor whites and
   Black laborers with common grievances, and expanding direct access to the
   Atlantic slave trade.
5. **Colonial society by 1750.** A hierarchy stretched between great planters
   and enslaved workers in the South, and merchant-farmer-artisan New England;
   both embedded in one Atlantic economy of sugar, tobacco, fish, and people.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **John Dane's family, opened as documents** (MIT 21H.101 Week 1): pair his
  1670s spiritual autobiography ("A Declaration of Remarkable Providences in the
  Course of my Life") with the printed Dane pedigree chart. Use it exactly as
  Maier does — an ordinary, non-elite colonist as the entry point, with the
  family tree making marriage networks and generational survival concrete.
  The New England demographic argument becomes visible in one family.
- **The statute ladder** — quote short operative phrases from the 1662 and 1705
  Virginia laws directly; the sequence read in order is the argument. Verify
  wording against a text of the Virginia statutes before quoting.
- **Bacon's Rebellion as the hinge** (Yawp Ch.3): narrate briefly, then state the
  structural consequence rather than the story's drama.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Reject the "1619 = slavery begins, fully formed" shorthand. The 1619 arrivals'
  status was ambiguous; the regime took roughly a century to build in law.
- Do not treat New England as slavery-free: New England merchants financed and
  provisioned the slave trade and held enslaved people; the difference is scale
  and centrality, not absence.
- Indentured servitude and slavery are not the same institution; do not blur
  them to make either point. State the two decisive differences: term, and
  heritability.
- Avoid teleology — nothing made the shift inevitable in 1620; name the specific
  contingent pressures.
- Dane's autobiography is a providence narrative: it is evidence of how a
  Puritan understood his life, not a neutral chronicle. Say so when using it.

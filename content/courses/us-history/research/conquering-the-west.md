# Research brief: conquering-the-west (The Conquest of the West)

status: planned
module: industry-and-empire
type: core
goal: Analyze the incorporation of the West — Indian wars, the destruction of the bison, allotment and assimilation, and the frontier myth.

THESIS ROLE. The enabling-conditions ledger's western page, both columns: the
railroad-and-homestead machinery that converted the continent into farms and
exports (the power the thesis measures), and the documented means — treaty
breach, subsistence destruction, allotment, boarding schools — by which the
land was cleared of its owners. Turner's frontier thesis appears here twice:
as the era's own self-explanation, and as the first lens the
historiographical-lenses chapter will test. The register holds: policy texts,
acreage numbers, and the Army's own photographs carry the account; no
editorializing in either direction.

- **American Panorama, "Land Acquisition and Dispossession: Mapping the Homestead

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Conquest was infrastructural before it was military.** The Pacific Railway
   Act and the Homestead Act (both 1862) put land and transport policy in place;
   railroads carried settlers, soldiers, and hunters and made the region's
   resources exportable. Railroad time standardization (1883) is the neat emblem:
   private corporations reset the nation's clocks.
2. **The treaties and their breach.** Fort Laramie (1868) recognized the Great
   Sioux Reservation including the Black Hills; the 1874 gold discovery and the
   ensuing invasion produced the war that included Little Bighorn (1876) and the
   subsequent seizure of the Hills. Use it as the pattern case: treaty, breach,
   war, cession.
3. **The bison.** Herds numbering in the tens of millions reduced to a few
   hundred wild animals by the mid-1880s, driven by hide markets, railroads, and
   an explicit military understanding that destroying the herds destroyed Plains
   subsistence and independence.
4. **Allotment and assimilation.** The Dawes Act (1887) broke reservations into
   individual allotments and opened the "surplus" to settlers — roughly two-thirds
   of remaining tribal land lost by 1934. Boarding schools (Carlisle, founded
   1879, "kill the Indian, save the man") removed children, cut hair, banned
   languages, and photographed the results as before-and-after propaganda.
5. **Wounded Knee (December 29, 1890).** The Ghost Dance, the killing of roughly
   250-300 Lakota including women and children, the mass burial, and the
   Army's own photographs of it. Three years later Turner announced the frontier
   closed and made "free land" the explanation of American democracy — juxtapose
   the two directly.
6. **Who else built and was excluded.** Chinese laborers built the western
   railroad and were barred by the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), the first
   race-based US immigration ban. Mexican-American landholders in the Southwest
   lost land through courts and squatting. The "empty West" was neither empty nor
   equally open.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The three-beat conquest sequence** (MIT L5): destroy the subsistence base
  (bison) → break the political base (war, allotment) → remake the survivors
  (boarding schools). Structure the lesson on it.
- **The Torlino before/after portraits** analyzed as propaganda: the staged
  Indigenous dress in "before", the lighting differences, the three-year gap.
  Worked example: list what the pair is designed to make the viewer conclude and
  what it actually documents. Parallel exercise: apply the same reading to the
  Wounded Knee burial photograph's inscribed caption.
- **The Homestead Act's two ledgers** (American Panorama): acres patented to
  homesteaders against acres removed from tribal control in the same decades.
- **Turner's thesis stated and tested**: the claim, the evidence it rests on, and
  the counter-evidence (conquest, federal subsidy, corporate capital).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The genocide question is genuinely debated among historians; present the
  evidence (intent statements, policy, mortality, forced assimilation) and the
  terms of the debate rather than asserting a verdict as settled.
- Wounded Knee was a massacre, not a battle, though the Army's own caption calls
  it a battle — that discrepancy is itself teachable.
- Native nations were not passive: name specific leaders, strategies,
  accommodations, and legal resistance, and note that Native peoples persisted
  through allotment and boarding schools.
- Bison collapse had multiple causes (market hunting, introduced cattle disease,
  drought, competition from horse herds) alongside deliberate destruction; give
  the mix and keep the policy dimension.
- Homesteading was not the main channel of western land transfer — railroads and
  speculators received far more; correct the yeoman myth with the numbers.

## Canon additions (curated expansion, July 2026)

- **Gates, *History of Public Land Law Development*** (FREE, full text;
  cached `texts/gateslandlaw*.txt` + `gateslandlaw.pdf`; verify all table
  numbers against page images) — the land-law machinery of incorporation:
  - Ch. XV: the Homestead Act measured against outcomes — including the
    built-in myth-bust that Daniel Freeman's "first homestead" was "one of a
    dozen or more firsts" before being named official (his certificate is
    captured: gateslandlaw-s2-freeman-homestead-certificate, indexed).
  - Ch. XVI-XVIII: the "incongruous land system" — unit-size escalation
    160→640→320→640 acres as the government re-learning aridity
    (ready-made figure); the "12 by 14" dollhouse fraud and the Kansas sod
    home photograph (both captured, indexed) as evidence-vs-statute set
    pieces; Powell's classification report as the road not taken.
  - Ch. XIV: railroad checkerboard grants and Gates's own historiographical
    strike — "not a scintilla of evidence" the double-minimum price
    recouped the grants: a named, citable myth correction.
- **Anderson & Hill, frontier self-government corpus** — the 2004 Stanford
  UP book itself remains print-only and unread; unread book-only claims
  (e.g. the water-rights and Native-property-rights chapters' specific
  numbers) must still be flagged unverified before quoting. But the FREE
  corpus around the book has now been read in full (cached under
  `texts/wildwest-*`, see `research/sources.yaml` for the complete access
  ledger) and yields verified claims and two real primary documents:
  - **Verified thesis statement**, straight from Anderson & Hill's own 1979
    article (`texts/wildwest-jls1979.txt`, the free original of the book's
    core argument): "The West during this time often is perceived as a
    place of great chaos... Our research indicates that this was not the
    case; property rights were protected and civil order prevailed."
    Four case studies, each with quoted primary language: land claims
    clubs, cattlemen's associations, mining camps, and wagon trains.
  - **Land claims clubs (verified).** The Claim Association of Johnson
    County, Iowa (est. 1839) elected a President, Vice-President, Clerk/
    Recorder, seven Judges of Claims (any five composed a court, three of
    those had to agree), and two Marshals; sanctions ran from arbitration
    fees to social ostracism ("we will neither neighbor with them...
    Trade barter deal with them in any way whatever"). Set piece: **cache
    the actual 1839 constitution**, reprinted verbatim from the original
    manuscript in Benjamin F. Shambaugh, "Frontier Land Clubs, or Claim
    Associations," *Annual Report of the American Historical Association
    for the Year 1900*, Vol. 1 (GPO, 1901), pp. 67-84 (constitution text
    at pp. 73-79) — `texts/wildwest-shambaugh-claimclubs-1900.txt`, page
    scan `images/wildwest-shambaugh-johnson-county-constitution.png`
    (printed p. 73, "ARTICLE 1ST" opening).
  - **Wagon-train constitutions (verified).** Wagon companies typically
    waited until past the US jurisdictional edge, then elected a Captain,
    Assistant Captain, Treasurer, and Secretary, and adopted a written
    constitution plus numbered by-laws covering jury trial (5 jurors for
    ordinary disputes, 12 requiring unanimity for capital crimes),
    banishment, gambling and firearm-safety rules, guard-duty rotation,
    and disposition of a deceased member's property. Set piece: **cache
    the actual constitution**, the 1849 "Constitution and By-Laws of the
    Green and Jersey County Company" (a California-bound company that
    organized at St. Joseph, Missouri), reprinted verbatim in Elizabeth
    Page, *Wagon West: A Story of the Oregon Trail* (Farrar & Rinehart,
    1930), Appendix C, pp. 336-341, signed by committee members Elon
    Eldred and A. R. Knapp — `texts/wildwest-page1930-greenjersey-
    constitution.txt`, page scan `images/wildwest-greenjersey-wagontrain-
    constitution.png` (printed p. 337, "Art. 1" through "Art. 8," opening
    of the By-Laws).
  - **Mining-district codes (verified, via Charles Howard Shinn, *Mining
    Camps: A Study in American Frontier Government*, 1885, cached
    `texts/wildwest-shinn1885-districtcodes-excerpt.txt` pp. 233-257 and
    `texts/wildwest-shinn1885-lawyers-excerpt.txt` pp. 118-122 — the exact
    source Anderson & Hill cite for their mining-camp case study).
    Springfield District restricted arbitration and jury service to
    American citizens; the Union Mining District barred lawyers outright
    ("Resolved, that no lawyer be permitted to practice law in this
    district, under penalty of not more than fifty nor less than twenty
    lashes, and he forever banished," quoted in Anderson & Hill 1979 from
    Shinn). Pitfall to flag alongside the "not so wild" thesis: Columbia
    District's code (in force by 1856, population 5,000+) explicitly
    barred "Asiatics [and] South-Sea Islanders" and non-naturalized
    non-Europeans from mining claims — bottom-up frontier self-government
    could be exclusionary as readily as it could be orderly; use this to
    complicate, not contradict, the institutions thesis and to connect
    directly to this lesson's Chinese-exclusion point.
  - **Cattlemen's associations (verified).** An 1883 Musselshell-range
    (Montana) association published a notice in the *Helena Daily Herald*:
    "We the undersigned, stock growers of the above described range,
    hereby give notice that we consider said range already overstocked;
    therefore we positively decline allowing any outside parties... the
    use of our corrals, nor will they be permitted to join us in any
    roundup." Quoted with exact book-page citations (Anderson & Hill 2004,
    pp. 148-56, 165, 202) in Robert Higgs, "How the Western Cattlemen
    Created Property Rights" (Independent Institute / *The Freeman*, 2005),
    `texts/wildwest-higgs-cattlemen.txt` — a free source that quotes the
    still-unread book directly and can be cited for those specific pages.
  - **Free article-version ledger** (see `research/sources.yaml` for full

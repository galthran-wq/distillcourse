# Research brief: conquering-the-west (The Conquest of the West)

status: planned
module: industry-and-empire
type: core
goal: Analyze the incorporation of the West — Indian wars, the destruction of the bison, allotment and assimilation, and the frontier myth.

THESIS ROLE. The enabling-conditions ledger's western page, both columns: the
railroad-and-homestead machinery that converted the continent into farms and
exports (the power the thesis measures), and the documented means — treaty
breach, subsistence destruction, allotment, boarding schools — by which the
land was cleared of its owners. Turner's frontier thesis appears here twice:
as the era's own self-explanation, and as the first lens the
historiographical-lenses chapter will test. The register holds: policy texts,
acreage numbers, and the Army's own photographs carry the account; no
editorializing in either direction.

- **American Yawp Ch.17 "Conquering the West"** — the spine narrative.
- **Milestone Documents** #29 Homestead Act, #30 Pacific Railway Act, #38 Fort

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Conquest was infrastructural before it was military.** The Pacific Railway
   Act and the Homestead Act (both 1862) put land and transport policy in place;
   railroads carried settlers, soldiers, and hunters and made the region's
   resources exportable. Railroad time standardization (1883) is the neat emblem:
   private corporations reset the nation's clocks.
2. **The treaties and their breach.** Fort Laramie (1868) recognized the Great
   Sioux Reservation including the Black Hills; the 1874 gold discovery and the
   ensuing invasion produced the war that included Little Bighorn (1876) and the
   subsequent seizure of the Hills. Use it as the pattern case: treaty, breach,
   war, cession.
3. **The bison.** Herds numbering in the tens of millions reduced to a few
   hundred wild animals by the mid-1880s, driven by hide markets, railroads, and
   an explicit military understanding that destroying the herds destroyed Plains
   subsistence and independence.
4. **Allotment and assimilation.** The Dawes Act (1887) broke reservations into
   individual allotments and opened the "surplus" to settlers — roughly two-thirds
   of remaining tribal land lost by 1934. Boarding schools (Carlisle, founded
   1879, "kill the Indian, save the man") removed children, cut hair, banned
   languages, and photographed the results as before-and-after propaganda.
5. **Wounded Knee (December 29, 1890).** The Ghost Dance, the killing of roughly
   250-300 Lakota including women and children, the mass burial, and the
   Army's own photographs of it. Three years later Turner announced the frontier
   closed and made "free land" the explanation of American democracy — juxtapose
   the two directly.
6. **Who else built and was excluded.** Chinese laborers built the western
   railroad and were barred by the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), the first
   race-based US immigration ban. Mexican-American landholders in the Southwest
   lost land through courts and squatting. The "empty West" was neither empty nor
   equally open.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The three-beat conquest sequence** (MIT L5): destroy the subsistence base
  (bison) → break the political base (war, allotment) → remake the survivors
  (boarding schools). Structure the lesson on it.
- **The Torlino before/after portraits** analyzed as propaganda: the staged
  Indigenous dress in "before", the lighting differences, the three-year gap.
  Worked example: list what the pair is designed to make the viewer conclude and
  what it actually documents. Parallel exercise: apply the same reading to the
  Wounded Knee burial photograph's inscribed caption.
- **The Homestead Act's two ledgers** (American Panorama): acres patented to
  homesteaders against acres removed from tribal control in the same decades.
- **Turner's thesis stated and tested**: the claim, the evidence it rests on, and
  the counter-evidence (conquest, federal subsidy, corporate capital).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The genocide question is genuinely debated among historians; present the
  evidence (intent statements, policy, mortality, forced assimilation) and the
  terms of the debate rather than asserting a verdict as settled.
- Wounded Knee was a massacre, not a battle, though the Army's own caption calls
  it a battle — that discrepancy is itself teachable.
- Native nations were not passive: name specific leaders, strategies,
  accommodations, and legal resistance, and note that Native peoples persisted
  through allotment and boarding schools.
- Bison collapse had multiple causes (market hunting, introduced cattle disease,
  drought, competition from horse herds) alongside deliberate destruction; give
  the mix and keep the policy dimension.
- Homesteading was not the main channel of western land transfer — railroads and
  speculators received far more; correct the yeoman myth with the numbers.

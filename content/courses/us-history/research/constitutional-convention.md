# Research brief: constitutional-convention (From Confederation to Constitution)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Explain how the Constitutional Convention's compromises produced the federal structure of 1787.

THESIS ROLE. The keystone of thesis element (2): the compounding institutions
of module 1, plus the classical/English design vocabulary from roman-blueprint
(now this lesson's prerequisite), assembled under deadline into the machine
this lesson dissects. The design's durability is the measured claim — the
oldest surviving written national constitution, amendable by design (the
superseded-clause map is the proof the lesson closes on). Wood's *Creation of
the American Republic* now grounds the state-constitutions-as-rehearsal
section: recap roman-blueprint's sovereignty story in two sentences and build
the convention narrative on it rather than re-teaching it.

## Sources to consult

- **Wood, *The Creation of the American Republic*, Part Two and Ch.VIII-XI**
  — the state constitutions 1776-80 as the running experiment (Pennsylvania's
  radical unicameralism vs Massachusetts's 1780 balance), the "vices of the
  system" catalog of the 1780s (paper money, debtor relief, factional
  lawmaking) as what the delegates thought they were fixing, and the
  convention-of-the-people device that made "We the People" a working legal
  mechanism rather than rhetoric. Summary-based access; verify prose quotes.

- **National Archives, "A More Perfect Union"** essay — the convention narrative:
  May-September 1787, Washington presiding, Madison's notes, Virginia Plan vs.
  New Jersey Plan, the Great (Connecticut) Compromise, the three-fifths
  compromise, the Committee of Detail, Gouverneur Morris's final prose, Jacob
  Shallus's engrossing, the September 17 signing (39 of 55).
- **National Archives, annotated Constitution transcript** — Articles I-VII with
  bracketed notes flagging every clause later superseded by amendment
  (three-fifths by the 14th; Senate election by the 17th; direct taxes by the
  16th; succession/tie-break by the 25th and 12th; sovereign immunity by the
  11th; fugitive-slave clause by the 13th). That annotation is a ready-made
  teaching device for "the document is amendable by design".
- **MIT 21H.101 Weeks 5-6** — the three-problems-in-sequence structure: state
  constitutions first (Virginia, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, 1776, plus
  Virginia's religious-freedom statute), then the federal convention (Randolph's
  Virginia Plan speech, Madison's notes for Sept 10 and 12-17). Adopt this
  sequencing — the Constitution is taught as three arguments, not one document.
- **American Yawp Ch.6 "A New Nation"** — the Confederation period's failures
  (no tax power, no enforcement, Shays's Rebellion) and early federal politics.
- **Milestone Documents** #3 Articles of Confederation, #7 Virginia Plan,
  #8 Northwest Ordinance, #9 Constitution — the primary artifacts.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **What the Articles could not do.** No power to tax, no executive, no federal
   courts, unanimity required for amendment, requisitions the states ignored.
   Shays's Rebellion (1786-87) as the demonstration that convinced the elite.
2. **The state constitutions were the rehearsal.** Virginia, Pennsylvania, and
   Massachusetts (1776-80) solved suffrage, establishment, and executive power
   differently; the federal framers had a decade of comparative evidence.
   Wood's addition: the 1780s "vices" — state legislatures behaving as the
   factional bodies 1776's virtue-assumption said they would not — are what
   the delegates named as the problem, and Massachusetts's 1780 procedure
   (drafted by convention, ratified by the towns) supplied the constituent
   device Philadelphia and Article VII would reuse.
3. **Two plans and one bargain.** Virginia Plan (proportional representation,
   national veto over state laws) vs. New Jersey Plan (one state one vote);
   the Connecticut Compromise gives proportional House and equal Senate. Say
   plainly that this was a deadlock-breaking trade, not a theory.
4. **Slavery written into the arithmetic.** The three-fifths clause (Art. I §2),
   the twenty-year protection of the slave trade (Art. I §9), and the
   fugitive-slave clause (Art. IV §2) — three concessions that made ratification
   possible in the South and shaped national politics for seventy years.
5. **The seven articles as a machine.** I legislative (10 sections), II executive,
   III judicial, IV states' relations, V amendment, VI supremacy, VII ratification
   (nine states). Read Article V as the designed escape hatch, and connect it to
   the annotated superseded clauses.
6. **Signing and its limits.** 39 of 55 delegates signed; Mason, Randolph, and
   Gerry refused; fourteen had already left. The document was "unanimous" by
   state delegation, not by person.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The three-column state-constitution comparison** (MIT Week 5): Virginia,
  Pennsylvania, Massachusetts on suffrage, church establishment, and executive
  power. Maier assigns all three together precisely so they can be compared.
- **Hamilton's June 18, 1787 speech** praising the British monarchy as "the best
  in the world" and floating a president-for-life (Archives, "A More Perfect
  Union") — use as a foil showing how open the design space was.
- **The "His Highness the President" title fight** belongs in the next lesson but
  can be foreshadowed in one sentence.
- **Jacob Shallus**, the assistant clerk who engrossed the document and whose
  identity was unknown for 150 years (Archives Q&A) — one line, as a reminder
  that famous documents have anonymous makers.
- **The superseded-clause map**: build the Archives' own bracketed annotations
  into a figure and use it as the lesson's closing check.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The Convention was called to amend the Articles and exceeded its mandate; say
  so rather than presenting the Constitution as the meeting's assignment.
- Do not call the three-fifths clause a statement about personhood ratios: it is
  an apportionment formula, and its practical effect was to inflate Southern
  representation and electoral votes.
- Madison's *Notes* are a reconstruction he revised for decades — the best record
  we have, not a transcript. Flag this when quoting the convention.
- Resist "the framers intended X" as a single voice: they disagreed sharply, and
  the document is the residue of their disagreement.
- Separation of powers is not isolation of powers — set this up here; Federalist
  47 resolves it in the next lesson.

## Canon additions (curated expansion, July 2026)

- **Lutz, *The Origins of American Constitutionalism*** (full OCR cached
  `texts/lutz-origins.txt`; verify quotes vs print) — three arguments that
  serve this brief directly:
  - Ch. 12: the 1787 text's institutions traced clause-by-clause to state
    constitutions and colonial compacts — scholarly ground for the existing
    "state constitutions were the rehearsal" key idea.
  - Ch. 11: Madison's own convention notes show 40 of his 71 proposals lost
    and the Virginia Plan beaten — the compromise machinery, not a single
    architect; a clean corrective set piece.
  - Conclusion: the Constitution as textually incomplete without the state
    constitutions and the Declaration — a framing note, use sparingly.
- **Lutz (ed.), *Colonial Origins*, docs 76-80** (FREE; cached
  `texts/lutz-colonial-origins.pdf`/`.txt`) — the confederation prehistory in
  primary text: New England Confederation 1643, Albany Plan 1754, Articles of
  Confederation (doc 78), plus Penn's 1697 and Galloway's 1774 plans in the
  appendix. The doc 77 headnote's structural comparison (Albany Plan/Articles
  descend from the 1643 confederation, NOT from the Iroquois model —
  delegate selection, unanimity, representation all differ) settles a common
  classroom claim with evidence.
- **Tier 2 references** — Rakove, *Original Meanings* (Pulitzer) and Amar,
  *America's Constitution: A Biography* for clause-level drafting and
  ratification-era meaning; print, not read by the swarm — verify before
  quoting. Farrand's *Records* via Founders Online / LoC "Century of
  Lawmaking" (FREE) for convention-debate primary text.

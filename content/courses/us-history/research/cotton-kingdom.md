# Research brief: cotton-kingdom (The Cotton Kingdom and the Domestic Slave Trade)

status: planned
module: slavery-and-crisis
type: core
goal: Analyze the political economy that made cotton and slavery expand together, using export and migration data.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** By this point the argument has traced self-government from
  covenant to constitutional order, watched the design survive its early
  stress tests, and followed the market revolution and mass democracy that
  engaged the economic engine; religion-and-reform, the immediately
  preceding lesson, showed the Second Great Awakening converting revival
  into abolitionism without yet naming the system abolitionism opposed.
- **Contributes:** This lesson's share of the spine step is the first half —
  "slavery expanded as a system," measured from cotton's export curve, the
  enslaved population, and the domestic slave trade as one causal chain.
- **Hands off:** The next lesson, manifest-destiny, takes up how this
  system's land hunger drove the continental acquisitions that reopened the
  slavery question the Missouri Compromise had closed.

Carries course outcome co-data. The demo (CottonSlaveryScrubber) is specced in
course.yaml; the prose must give the reader a concrete task before free play.

THESIS ROLE. The enabling-conditions argument's hardest chapter, told at full
documented weight: the cotton gin (technology), land seized from Native
nations (cheap land), and a bound labor system expanded together into the
export engine of the antebellum economy — the causal chain stated from
prices, export shares, and migration counts. The register is the course's
standing one: statutes, prices, and the planter's own sentence carry the
analysis; the Douglass–Lincoln framing (the founding as promise, this system
as its contradiction) is set up here and paid off in sectional-crisis and
emancipation. Howe's Ch.4 ("The World that Cotton Made") corroborates the
spine numbers.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The numbers, first and precisely.** Cotton exports rose from about 5 million
   pounds in 1793 to roughly 2 billion pounds in 1860, when cotton was more than
   60% of all US exports; the enslaved population reached 3.95 million by 1860;
   yield per enslaved worker in Mississippi rose about 600% between 1820 and 1860.
   Lead with the arithmetic — the argument is quantitative.
2. **The mutual-reinforcement mechanism.** Cotton profits bought people; more
   people cleared and worked more land; more cotton bought more people. Quote the
   planter logic reproduced in the Yawp: "to sell cotton in order to buy negroes —
   to make more cotton to buy more negroes."
3. **The domestic slave trade.** With the Atlantic trade banned in 1808, the
   internal trade supplied the cotton frontier: nearly one million people moved
   from the upper South to the Deep South, 1810-1860, the majority sold rather
   than migrating with an enslaver. Family separation was the system's routine
   mechanism, not its excess.
4. **Where the land came from.** The 1830s peak coincides with removal: cotton
   expanded onto land taken from the Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Cherokee.
   Removal and the slave trade are one process viewed from two angles — connect
   explicitly to jacksonian-democracy.
5. **The regime as lived.** Labor discipline (the gang system, the whip, the
   "pushing system" behind the yield increase), law (enslaved people as property
   with no standing), and sexual violence. Use the Celia case (Missouri, 1855):
   a 19-year-old enslaved woman executed for killing the man who had raped her
   for years — the law recognized no self-defense claim because it recognized no
   right to refuse.
6. **The proslavery argument.** Fitzhugh's claim that slavery was a humane
   alternative to Northern wage labor — read as evidence of how the system
   defended itself, and as the direct target of free-labor ideology later.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The escalating market sequence** (Yawp Ch.11's own visual argument): sale
  posters → auction-house engraving → county-level enslaved-population density
  map → the Gordon photograph as the sequence's end point. Reproduce the
  *escalation*, not just the final image.
- **The Gordon carte-de-visite (1863)**: 1863 abolitionist evidence precisely
  because it was photographic. Handle with restraint and explain what it was made
  for and how it circulated.
- **The Celia case (1855)** as a worked legal analysis: the statute, the defense
  offered, the ruling, the execution — and what each step reveals about the legal
  status of an enslaved person.
- **A per-decade migration computation**: from the Panorama data, compute net
  export from Virginia and net import into Mississippi for one decade, then have
  the reader do the same for another decade (worked example → exercise).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The Panorama forced-migration map is a *model*, not a record: the census never
  recorded where an enslaved person was born, so county-level flows are estimated
  from decennial counts using a Bancroft-derived equation. Say this — it is a
  first-class lesson in what to do when the needed data was never collected.
- Cameron Blevins's published critique of that map (that two-line narrative
  previews risk reducing testimony to data points) is worth one sentence: it
  keeps the quantitative section honest.
- Do not let statistics displace people: pair every aggregate with one named,
  documented individual.
- "King Cotton" was a Southern political slogan; use it as a quoted claim, not as
  the lesson's own analytic term.
- Avoid implying enslaved people were passive: sabotage, work slowdowns, flight,
  family preservation, and revolt (Nat Turner, 1831) belong in the section on the
  regime as lived.

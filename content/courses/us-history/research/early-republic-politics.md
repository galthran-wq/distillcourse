# Research brief: early-republic-politics (Federalists and Republicans, 1789-1800)

status: planned
module: early-republic
type: core
goal: Explain how a founding generation that dreaded faction produced the first party system and the first peaceful transfer of power.

## Sources to consult

- **Wood, *Empire of Liberty*** — the depth source. **Ch.1** (experiment in
  republicanism), **Ch.2** "A Monarchical Republic" (Washington's levees, the
  Senate titles debate), **Ch.3** "The Federalist Program" (assumption, the Bank,
  tariffs), **Ch.4** (the unplanned emergence of the Jeffersonian Republican
  party), **Ch.5** (the French Revolution in America, Genêt, Democratic-Republican
  societies), **Ch.6** (Adams), **Ch.7** "The Crisis of 1798-1799" (XYZ, Quasi-War,
  Alien and Sedition Acts, Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions), **Ch.8** "The
  Jeffersonian Revolution of 1800".
- **American Yawp Ch.6-7** — the narrative spine for the Confederation aftermath
  and the early republic.
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 8** — the primary-source set: Washington's Farewell Address,
  the Sedition Act, Federalist/Republican newspaper polemic.
- **Milestone Documents** #10 Washington's First Inaugural, #11 Federal Judiciary
  Act, #14 Alien and Sedition Acts.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **A monarchical republic.** Washington and the Federalists deliberately built
   ceremony — levees, formal tours, the titles debate — to lend a fragile republic
   dignity. Wood's paradox is the section's thesis: republican legitimacy borrowed
   monarchical form.
2. **Hamilton's program split the government.** Assumption of state debts,
   the Bank of the United States, the excise, and the *Report on Manufactures*;
   Madison and Jefferson's opposition on constitutional and sectional grounds.
   Parties formed around a fiscal program, not around abstractions.
3. **The French Revolution polarized everything.** Genêt's mission, the
   Democratic-Republican societies, the Jay Treaty firestorm, and Washington's
   Farewell warning against party and permanent alliances — issued as he retired
   from a government already split by both.
4. **The crisis of 1798-99.** XYZ Affair → Quasi-War → Alien and Sedition Acts →
   prosecutions of Republican editors → the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions
   asserting a state interposition doctrine that would echo through nullification
   and secession. Flag that forward link explicitly.
5. **1800 and the peaceful transfer.** The Jefferson-Burr electoral tie, 36
   House ballots, militias readying, and then a transfer of power between hostile
   parties without violence — an event with no clear modern precedent at the time,
   and the reason the 12th Amendment exists.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The titles debate** (Wood Ch.2): Adams rejecting "President of the United
  States" as too common — "there are, after all, Presidents of Fire Companies and
  of a Cricket Club" — and pushing "His Highness"; the House killing it. A compact
  comic set piece about what kind of state this was going to be.
- **"Congressional Pugilists" (1798)**: Griswold caning Lyon on the House floor,
  Lyon fighting back with fire tongs — the first brawl in the House, and period
  partisan propaganda about it. Use it as evidence of how total the split was.
- **The Sedition Act prosecutions**: name defendants (Matthew Lyon among them) and
  the offense — criticizing the president in print — and note the Act's expiry
  date coinciding with the end of Adams's term.
- **The 36-ballot count** (Wood Ch.8), reconstructed as a figure, with one
  contemporary's line that a free people had changed governments "without
  confusion, villainy and bloodshed".
- **The Lansdowne portrait** as the visual argument of Ch.2 — staging, columns,
  sword, oratorical gesture.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The first parties were not modern parties: no primaries, no formal membership,
  and both sides claimed to oppose party as such while building one.
- "Republican" here means Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans, unrelated to the
  1854 Republican Party. Say this once, plainly, early.
- The Alien and Sedition Acts were never struck down by any court; they lapsed
  and were repudiated politically — an important point about how constitutional
  limits actually operated before judicial review took hold.
- The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions were written secretly by Jefferson and
  Madison while serving in the government they were attacking.
- Do not describe 1800 as a "revolution" without noting it was a revolution in
  personnel and principle, not in institutions.

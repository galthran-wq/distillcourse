# Research brief: emancipation (Emancipation)

status: planned
module: civil-war-and-reconstruction
type: core
goal: Analyze how enslaved people's flight and Lincoln's policy together turned the war for Union into a war for freedom.

Document-heavy lesson; the manuscript sequence is the argument.

THESIS ROLE. The self-correction thesis's central chapter: the promise of the
founding documents enforced at the price the war set — driven jointly from
below (flight to Union lines) and from the top (Lincoln's staged legal
instruments), completed by constitutional amendment. The register is already
this lesson's method: manuscripts, dates, and exact legal reach, no
sentiment. The course key result (war for Union becomes war for emancipation,
~750,000 dead) lives here.

## Sources to consult

- **Library of Congress, "Abraham Lincoln and Emancipation"** essay (linked from
  the Lincoln Papers digital collection) — the fullest narrative: Fort Monroe
  contraband, the Greeley letter, the July 22 1862 cabinet reading, Antietam as
  the trigger, the January 1 signing, and the provenance of each manuscript.
- **Library of Congress Lincoln Papers** — the preliminary draft (mal1723200),
  the final lithograph (mal2082000), the 13th Amendment ceremonial resolution
  (mal4361100), and the Frequently Requested Documents essay (Conkling, Corning,
  and Hodges letters; the Douglass correspondence).
- **Blight, HIST 119 Lectures 15-16** — Lincoln, leadership, and race;
  "Days of Jubilee": Stephen Ash's three-zone geography of freedom (garrisoned
  towns / no-man's-land / unconquered hinterland) explaining why the date of
  actual liberation depended on army geography, not on the proclamation.
- **McPherson, *Battle Cry of Freedom*, Ch.16** ("We Must Free the Slaves or Be
  Ourselves Subdued") and **Ch.18** (Antietam's diplomatic effect).
- **American Yawp Ch.14**, "War for Emancipation 1863-1865".
- **Milestone Documents** #32 Emancipation Proclamation, #33 War Dept. General
  Order 143 (US Colored Troops), #36 13th Amendment.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **It began from below.** May 1861: Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James
   Townsend row to Fort Monroe; Benjamin Butler declines to return them and
   classifies them as contraband of war. Thousands follow. The First and Second
   Confiscation Acts (1861, 1862) ratify what flight had already forced.
2. **Lincoln's constraint and his caution.** The border states: "I hope to have
   God on my side, but I must have Kentucky." The Greeley letter (Aug 22, 1862):
   "My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either
   to save or to destroy slavery" — written *after* he had already drafted the
   proclamation. Public position and private policy were different objects; that
   gap is a teachable point about reading political documents.
3. **The two drafts.** The preliminary proclamation read to the cabinet July 22,
   1862 (held back on Seward's advice until a victory); issued after Antietam on
   September 22; the final proclamation January 1, 1863. Compare their texts:
   compensated emancipation and colonization language dropped, Black enlistment
   language added.
4. **What the document did and did not do.** It freed enslaved people in
   rebel-held territory only, explicitly exempting Union-held Louisiana parishes,
   Tennessee, parts of Virginia, and the loyal border states. As a war measure
   under the commander-in-chief power it could reach only enemy territory. Its
   force came from the army that carried it.
5. **Black soldiers.** General Order 143 creates the US Colored Troops; roughly
   200,000 Black men serve in the army and navy; unequal pay until 1864; the
   Confederate refusal to treat captured Black soldiers as prisoners of war.
   Enlistment converted claims to freedom into claims to citizenship.
6. **The Thirteenth Amendment as the completion.** The proclamation was a war
   measure of uncertain postwar durability; the amendment (passed the House
   January 1865, ratified December 1865) is what abolished slavery — with the
   punishment-for-crime exception that later matters for convict leasing.
7. **What freedom meant on the ground.** Ash's three zones; Thomas Wentworth
   Higginson's account of freedpeople spontaneously singing "My Country, 'Tis of
   Thee" at Hilton Head on January 1, 1863.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The Fort Monroe contraband decision** as the lesson's opening scene, with the
  three men named.
- **Draft-vs-final text comparison** (LOC emancipation essay): the specific
  clauses added and removed. Worked example: read the two texts and state the
  policy change each edit represents; parallel exercise on the Conkling or
  Hodges letter.
- **The signing anecdote**: Lincoln steadying his hand after hours of New Year's
  handshaking so the signature would not tremble and be read as hesitation —
  "I never in my life felt more certain that I was doing right."
- **The Hodges letter (April 4, 1864)**: "I claim not to have controlled events,
  but confess plainly that events have controlled me" — Lincoln's own account of
  the agency question the lesson is about.
- **The destroyed manuscript**: the signed final proclamation was sold at the
  1864 Northwestern Sanitary Fair and burned in the 1871 Chicago Fire; only the
  lithograph Lincoln had ordered survives. A provenance story worth one figure.
- **Douglass's August 1864 correspondence** with Lincoln about a contingency plan
  to help people escape if Lincoln lost re-election.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not say the Proclamation "freed the slaves"; state exactly whom it covered
  and how liberation actually occurred (army presence, flight, then the 13th).
- Equally, do not adopt the cynical inversion ("it freed nobody"): it changed the
  legal status of roughly 3 million people in rebel territory, made every
  advancing Union army an instrument of emancipation, and opened enlistment.
- **Image caption check:** the NARA "milestone" Emancipation Proclamation scan
  and the LOC lithograph are different objects, and the LOC essay states the
  final manuscript was destroyed in 1871. Verify which document each capture
  shows before writing a caption.
- The "Great Emancipator" and "reluctant liberator" framings are both crude; the
  evidence supports a constrained politician moving under pressure from enslaved
  people, soldiers, and events.
- Attribute all Lincoln quotations to the specific letter or speech and date.

## Canon additions (curated expansion, July 2026)

- **Oakes, *Freedom National*** (FULL TEXT read; cached `texts/oakesfn.pdf` +
  shard extractions `oakesfn-s1..s4.txt`; cite the 2013 Norton edition) —
  the scholarly spine for this whole lesson, in the exact register the course
  intro promises (the founding as promise, the war as enforcement):
  - Ch. 3: the Fortress Monroe episode in full — Baker, Townsend, and
    Mallory's escape; the Butler-Cary exchange (Virginia as "a foreign
    country"); cabinet endorsement within a week; the May 30, 1861
    instructions as the first federal fugitive policy. Gem: slavery began in
    1619 at Hampton Roads and began to end at the same waters in 1861.
  - Ch. 4 + 7: the two Confiscation Acts as the statutory emancipation the
    Proclamation myth erases — "By the Act of Congress they are clearly
    free" (Butler, Oct 1862). The purpose-vs-effect rhetorical discipline is
    the analytic key to Lincoln's public caution (pairs with the Greeley
    letter already in this brief's key ideas).
  - Ch. 6: "self-emancipation" — its genealogy (Creole, Amistad) and the
    Sea Islands as the first full-scale test; Sam Mitchell's mother at Port
    Royal ("dat ain't no t'under, dat Yankee come to gib you Freedom").
  - Ch. 9: the Mythmaking section — Reluctant Emancipator (Greeley) vs Great
    Emancipator (Emerson), both wrong about chronology; use as the lesson's
    historiographical close.
  - Ch. 10: the ~700-word Proclamation's two buried operative changes
    (enticement lifted; black enlistment opened) — exactly the "what the
    document did and did not do" key idea, now with page-level support.
  - Vote tallies as evidence (93-55, 33-6, 117-2) fit the course's
    numbers-carry-the-argument tone.

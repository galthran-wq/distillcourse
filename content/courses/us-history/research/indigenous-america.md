# Research brief: indigenous-america (Indigenous America and the Columbian Exchange)

status: planned
module: contact-and-colonies
type: core
goal: Explain how European contact transformed the Americas demographically and ecologically.

This is lesson 1 of the course (pedagogy C3): the most polished, ending in a felt
win — the reader should finish able to read a 1502 map as evidence and to state
the demographic argument with numbers.

## Sources to consult

- **American Yawp Ch.1 "Indigenous America"** — the spine. Cahokia, Chaco Canyon,
  Pacific Northwest societies; Spanish conquest of the Aztec and Inca; the ~90-95%
  population collapse within ~150 years of contact, attributed primarily to
  epidemic disease rather than direct violence. This is the chapter's central
  demographic claim and the course's opening key result.
- **American Yawp Ch.2 "Colliding Cultures"** — the Columbian Exchange thesis:
  reciprocal but wildly asymmetric (Old World pathogens devastate the Americas;
  New World crops — potato, maize, cassava — fuel European and Asian population
  growth). Also Spanish America and the arrival of European rivals.
- **Give Me Liberty Part 1, Ch.1 "Old Worlds and New" / Ch.2 "European Colonies
  and Native Nations, 1600-1660"** — supplies the freedom-lens framing the course
  reuses: whose freedom, on what material basis, with what boundaries.
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 2 "The Indians' America"** — for the pedagogical move of
  starting from Indigenous societies as societies, not as a prologue to Europe.
- **National Archives / Milestone Documents** — not used here (list starts 1776);
  say so if the reader wonders where the documents are.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Pre-contact scale and complexity.** Cahokia at its height (c. 1100) was
   larger than contemporaneous London; Chaco Canyon's great houses required
   long-distance timber and road networks. Use these as physical evidence, not
   adjectives — the argument is that complex urban societies existed
   independently on both sides of the Atlantic.
2. **The demographic collapse and its cause.** ~90-95% of the Indigenous
   population died within roughly 150 years. The mechanism is epidemiological:
   populations with no prior exposure to smallpox, measles, influenza, and typhus,
   compounded by war, enslavement, and the destruction of food systems. State the
   uncertainty honestly — pre-contact population estimates vary by a factor of
   several, and the percentage is more robust than the absolute number.
3. **The Columbian Exchange as a two-way, unequal transfer.** Set up the ledger
   explicitly: pathogens and livestock and grains west; maize, potatoes, tomatoes,
   cassava, cacao, tobacco east. The asymmetry is the point — the eastward flow
   raised European carrying capacity while the westward flow emptied the Americas.
4. **Conquest followed collapse, it did not cause it.** Cortés and Pizarro toppled
   the Aztec and Inca states with small forces because they arrived amid epidemic
   mortality and recruited Indigenous allies with their own grievances. Correct
   the "few Europeans beat empires" myth by naming the two real mechanisms.
5. **Reading a source.** Introduce the course's method with the Cantino
   planisphere (1502): what the map shows, what it conspicuously lacks, and what
   that absence documents about European knowledge nine years after 1492.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Cahokia vs. London population comparison** (Yawp Ch.1) — a single numeric
  comparison that resets the reader's default picture.
- **The Cantino planisphere read as evidence** (Yawp Ch.1/2 figure). Walk the
  reader through it: the Tordesillas line drawn straight through an ocean nobody
  had crossed at that latitude; a detailed African coast against a fragmentary
  South American one. This is the lesson's "I can do this" win — the reader
  performs a source reading and gets a defensible conclusion.
- **The archaeological-reconstruction + period-European-map pairing** is the
  Yawp's own visual argument for parallel civilizations; the lesson should
  reproduce the *pairing idea*, drawing the reconstruction side.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not present the collapse as a natural accident that absolves conquest:
  enslavement, forced labor (encomienda), war, and the destruction of food
  systems raised mortality and prevented demographic recovery.
- Population estimates are contested (Dobyns vs. Denevan vs. more recent work);
  give a range, attribute it, and do not present a single precise figure as
  settled.
- Avoid "the Americas were empty/pristine" and equally avoid flattening hundreds
  of distinct nations into one "Indigenous America" — name specific peoples.
- The Columbian Exchange is not a metaphor for cultural mixing; it is a
  biological and agricultural transfer. Keep the term precise.
- No lesson-scale claims about "civilizational superiority" — the evidence
  supports epidemiological contingency, not that.

   Reader must be able to see: the detailed West African coastline, the ragged
   partial South American coast, the vertical Tordesillas demarcation line, and
   the decorative compass roses. Placement: centered, full width. This is the
   lesson's core source-reading object; a redrawn map would destroy exactly the
   "what they did not yet know" evidence.

   westbound (pathogens, cattle, pigs, wheat, sugarcane) and eastbound (maize,
   potato, tomato, cassava, cacao, tobacco), with the arrow widths and a one-line
   tally of the asymmetric outcome. Idea from Yawp Ch.2's exchange discussion.

   pre-contact population bar with the estimate range shown as an error band;
   frames 2-4 step through 1500 / 1550 / 1650, one epidemic wave annotated per
   frame, ending at ~5-10% of the starting bar. One thing changes per frame.
4. `draw:` single `<Figure>` — **Cahokia's scale**: schematic plan of Monks Mound
   and the central plaza with a human-scale bar and a same-scale silhouette of
   1100 London for comparison. One idea: this was a city.

   diagram showing epidemic mortality and Indigenous alliance-building as the two

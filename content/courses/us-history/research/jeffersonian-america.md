# Research brief: jeffersonian-america (Jeffersonian America: Louisiana, the Marshall Court, and the War of 1812)

status: planned
module: early-republic
type: core
goal: Explain how the Louisiana Purchase, judicial review, and the War of 1812 consolidated the republic.

## Sources to consult

- **Wood, *Empire of Liberty*** — **Ch.10** "The Jeffersonian West" (Louisiana
  Purchase, Lewis and Clark, Indian policy, the Burr conspiracy), **Ch.11-12**
  (law, an independent judiciary, Marshall and the origins of judicial review),
  **Ch.17** "Republican Diplomacy" (carrying trade, impressment, the Chesapeake
  affair, the Embargo Act), **Ch.18** "The War of 1812", **Ch.19** epilogue.
- **Milestone Documents** #15 Marbury v. Madison, #16 Jefferson's secret message
  on Lewis and Clark, #17 Louisiana Purchase Treaty, #18 Treaty of Ghent,
  #19 McCulloch v. Maryland, #22 Gibbons v. Ogden.
- **American Yawp Ch.7 "The Early Republic"** — Jeffersonian era, the War of
  1812, and Indigenous resistance (Tecumseh's confederacy), which the Wood
  volume treats thinly.
- **The Federalist No. 78** (already read in the previous module) — the textual
  root of judicial review, to be cross-referenced, not re-taught.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Marbury's two-step (1803).** Marshall holds Marbury's right was violated and
   a remedy exists, then holds that the statute granting the Court jurisdiction
   to issue the writ is unconstitutional — so the Court cannot act. He scores the
   point against Jefferson while avoiding an order Jefferson could ignore, and
   establishes that "a law repugnant to the Constitution is void". No federal
   statute is struck down again until Dred Scott (1857) — say so; it shows how
   slowly the power was actually used.
2. **The Louisiana Purchase (1803).** 828,000 square miles for $15 million,
   doubling the country; Jefferson's own constitutional scruples overridden by
   the opportunity; the Lewis and Clark expedition authorized by a secret message
   to Congress. Frame it as "empire of liberty" — expansion as the multiplication
   of a citizen-farmer republic — and immediately note whose land it was.
3. **Indigenous resistance.** Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa's pan-Indian confederacy,
   Tippecanoe (1811), and the alliance with Britain — the War of 1812's western
   theater was, from the Native side, a war for the Ohio Valley.
4. **The Embargo (1807-09).** Jefferson's attempt to substitute commercial
   coercion for war: exports fell nearly 80% in a single year, and enforcement
   required Hamiltonian-style federal coercion on the Canadian border — a
   Republican administration undoing its own small-government premise.
5. **The War of 1812.** Failed invasions of Canada, naval single-ship victories,
   the Creek War, the burning of Washington (August 1814), the Hartford
   Convention, and New Orleans (January 8, 1815) fought two weeks after the
   Treaty of Ghent was signed. The treaty resolved none of the stated causes;
   the war nonetheless produced a durable national self-confidence.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Marbury's reasoning as a four-step chain** (Wood Ch.12): right violated →
  remedy exists → this Court lacks jurisdiction under the Constitution → the
  statute purporting to grant it is void. Diagram it; then have the reader apply
  the same structure to a hypothetical in an exercise.
- **The embargo's arithmetic** (Wood Ch.17): the ~80% export collapse of 1808 as
  a chart, with the political fallout annotated.
- **New Orleans's timing irony** (Wood Ch.18): ~2,000 British casualties to about
  70 American, two weeks after peace was signed — and it was this battle, not the
  treaty, that Americans remembered as winning the war.
- **The burning of Washington**, using the 1814 engraving as contemporaneous
  reportage.
- Optional aside: the Burr conspiracy and treason trial as a test of the treason
  clause's deliberately narrow definition.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Marbury did not invent judicial review out of nothing (see Federalist 78 and
  state precedents) and did not immediately establish routine judicial supremacy.
- The Louisiana Purchase bought France's *claim*, not clear title: the land was
  occupied by Native nations who were not party to the sale.
- The War of 1812's causes (impressment, neutral rights) go unmentioned in the
  Treaty of Ghent — the war ended in status quo ante. Resist "the second war of
  independence" framing without qualifying it.
- The Hartford Convention did not propose secession outright, but it damaged the
  Federalists fatally — note the difference between what was proposed and what
  was believed.
- Wood's volume is thin on women and on enslaved and Native people; supplement
  from the Yawp rather than reproducing that gap.

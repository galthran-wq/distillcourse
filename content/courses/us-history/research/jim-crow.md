# Research brief: jim-crow (Jim Crow: Disenfranchisement and Segregation)

status: planned
module: industry-and-empire
type: core
goal: Explain how the South legally dismantled Black citizenship between 1890 and 1910 and how the Supreme Court ratified it.

THESIS ROLE. The self-correction's longest suspension, documented as legal
engineering: race-neutral statute text producing racial results, ratified by
the Court the constitutional machine relies on. For the course's arc this
lesson is the "before" that makes civil-rights-movement's enforcement story
measurable — the registration-collapse numbers established here are the
baseline the VRA's numbers reverse. The register does the work: statutes,
registration counts, spending ratios, and Wells's own tabulation method
(count the cases, test the claimed causes) — her method is the course's
method, and the lesson should say so.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Reconstruction's amendments established Black citizenship and
  the vote on paper, and Redemption overthrew Reconstruction's enforcement
  by terror and bargain; immigration-and-the-city established this era's
  parallel habit of restricting citizenship by ostensibly race-neutral
  statute rather than by naming race outright.

- **Contributes:** This lesson's share of the spine's move — the correction
  suspended in the South — the race-neutral statutes, poll taxes, literacy
  tests, and grandfather clauses that dismantled Black citizenship between
  1890 and 1910, ratified by the Court in Plessy.

- **Hands off:** American-empire takes up the empire acquired offshore,
  testing the same racial and constitutional exclusions this lesson
  documents against the newly annexed territories and peoples.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Disenfranchisement was engineered to be race-neutral on its face.**
   Mississippi's 1890 constitution as the template: poll tax, literacy and
   "understanding" clauses administered by white registrars, residency
   requirements, and felony-disqualification lists chosen for the offenses
   Black defendants were most often convicted of. Grandfather clauses exempted
   white voters. Give the registration numbers before and after — Louisiana's
   Black registration falling from over 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 by 1904 is
   the standard figure to verify and quote.
2. **Why the Fifteenth Amendment did not stop it.** Nothing in the statutes
   mentions race; Section 2 of the Fourteenth (reducing representation for
   disenfranchisement) was never enforced; and federal enforcement machinery had
   been dismantled after 1877 (cross-reference redemption).
3. **Segregation as law, not custom.** Streetcars, railroads, schools, hospitals,
   cemeteries, and courtroom Bibles legislated separate between the 1880s and
   1910s — a modern, urban, statutory system, not a leftover of slavery.
4. **Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).** A deliberate test case brought by a New Orleans
   committee; the 7-1 ruling that separate accommodations do not imply
   inferiority; Harlan's dissent. Note the doctrine's operative effect: "equal"
   was never enforced, and per-pupil spending gaps of 5:1 or worse were routine.
5. **Terror enforced the statutes.** Lynching counts by year (Tuskegee/NAACP
   series), the ritual and publicity of spectacle lynchings, and Ida B. Wells's
   statistical investigative journalism refuting the standard justification.
   Wells's method — count the cases, examine the alleged causes — is the analytic
   model to show.
6. **Two strategies in response.** Washington's Atlanta Compromise (1895):
   accommodation, vocational education, economic self-help. Du Bois (1903):
   political rights first, higher education, and the Niagara Movement leading to
   the NAACP (1909). Present both as strategies under constraint, not as virtue
   and vice.
7. **The Great Migration's precondition.** Everything in this lesson is what
   people left; forward-link to world-war-one.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The registration collapse, computed**: take one state's Black registration
  before and after its new constitution, compute the percentage change, and
  identify which device did the work. Worked example on Louisiana; parallel
  exercise on Alabama or Mississippi.
- **Plessy vs. Harlan, side by side**: the majority's "badge of inferiority"
  reasoning and Harlan's color-blind-Constitution dissent, quoted and then
  answered against each other. Pair the quotation with Brown's 1954 "inherently
  unequal" (forward reference) — the two-quote pairing across 58 years is the
  arc the National Archives' own ordering suggests.
- **Wells's tabulation**: reproduce the analytic move — count lynchings, classify
  alleged causes, show that the assault justification fails on the numbers.
- **Washington vs. Du Bois** as the module's paired-dissent set piece.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Jim Crow was not everywhere identical: Northern segregation was largely by
  practice, contract, and violence rather than statute — do not let "the South"
  imply that the North was open.
- "Separate but equal" was never a description; the equal half was a fiction from
  the start, and the spending data shows it.
- Do not present Washington as simply capitulating: he funded litigation
  secretly and built institutions; the disagreement with Du Bois was strategic.
- Verify lynching totals against a named source series (Tuskegee Institute or the
  NAACP compilation) and state the definition used.
- Disenfranchisement also removed poor white voters in several states — an
  intended effect for the Redeemer elite, and evidence that this was class
  politics as well as racial politics.

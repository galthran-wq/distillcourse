# Research brief: manifest-destiny (Manifest Destiny and the Mexican-American War)

status: planned
module: slavery-and-crisis
type: core
goal: Explain how continental expansion reopened the slavery question the Missouri Compromise had closed.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Cotton-kingdom, the immediately preceding lesson, has
  established cotton and slavery expanding together as one measured system —
  export prices, population growth, and the domestic slave trade all driven
  by the same land hunger, with Indian removal supplying the land it
  expanded onto.
- **Contributes:** This lesson's share of the spine step is the acquisitions
  — Texas, Oregon, the Mexican Cession — that completed the continent and
  reopened the slavery question the Missouri Compromise had closed.
- **Hands off:** The next lesson, sectional-crisis, takes up the compromise
  machinery's attempt, and eventual failure, to answer the question these
  acquisitions reopened.

THESIS ROLE. The land condition at continental completion — and its price:
the acquisitions that made the United States a two-ocean power (Texas,
Oregon, the Mexican Cession) immediately destabilized the political system
that made them, because every acre reopened the slavery question. The lesson
keeps both halves of the thesis's ledger visible: the measured scale of
expansion, and the war and broken guarantees it ran on. Howe's Ch.18-19
supply the manifest-destiny ideology (O'Sullivan's coinage) and the
war-to-Wilmot chain.

## Sources to consult

- **Howe, *What Hath God Wrought*, Ch.18 "Westward the Star of Empire" and
  Ch.19 "The War Against Mexico"** — the trails, manifest destiny as
  ideology, and the war's role in sharpening the sectional conflict; Howe's
  counterfactual that a Clay victory in 1844 might have changed the road to
  war, flagged as his aside. Summary-based access.

- **American Yawp Ch.12 "Manifest Destiny"** — the spine: westward expansion,
  Oregon migration, the Mexican-American War, and the ideology itself.
- **Blight, HIST 119 Lecture 6** — "Expansion and Slavery: legacies of the Mexican
  War and the Compromise of 1850"; the argument that the war's territorial gain
  is what made the 1850s unmanageable.
- **McPherson, *Battle Cry of Freedom*, Prologue and Ch.2** — "From the Halls of
  Montezuma" and "Mexico Will Poison Us": the Mexican War framed as the opening
  act of the sectional crisis, and the Wilmot Proviso as the hinge.
- **Milestone Documents** #20 Missouri Compromise, #24 Treaty of Guadalupe
  Hidalgo, #21 Monroe Doctrine.
- **American Panorama, Overland Trails 1840-1860** — routes, forts and river
  crossings, year-by-year traveler volume, and linked emigrant diaries. Use for
  the migration numbers and one diarist's voice.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The prior settlement.** The Missouri Compromise (1820) drew a line at
   36°30′ and was widely believed to have removed slavery from national politics
   for a generation. State what it settled, so the reader can see what expansion
   unsettled.
2. **Manifest destiny as ideology.** John L. O'Sullivan's 1845 coinage; the
   providential and racial content of the claim; how it served both agrarian
   land hunger and commercial interest in Pacific ports.
3. **Overland migration in numbers.** Oregon, California, and Mormon trails,
   1840-1860; how many travelled per year and how the volume spikes with the
   1849 gold rush. Ground it with one diarist's entries.
4. **Texas and Oregon.** Annexation (1845) as a slavery question from the start;
   the Oregon settlement at 49° as the negotiated counterpoint to war with
   Mexico — expansion by treaty in one direction, by invasion in the other.
5. **The war and its terms.** 1846-48, the disputed Rio Grande boundary, the
   invasion and occupation of Mexico City, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo
   transferring roughly 525,000 square miles, and the nominal citizenship and
   property guarantees to Mexican residents that were widely ignored afterward.
6. **The Wilmot Proviso and free soil.** Proposed within months of the war's
   start: no slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico. It passed the House
   repeatedly and died in the Senate — and it converted every subsequent
   territorial question into a slavery vote. Free soil is not abolition:
   distinguish the two positions carefully, since the Republican Party is built
   on the first.
7. **Contemporary opposition.** Lincoln's Spot Resolutions as a freshman
   congressman, Thoreau's tax refusal, and Whig doubts — the war was contested
   at the time, not retrospectively.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **"Mexico will poison us"** (McPherson Ch.2 title, from Emerson's warning that
  the US would swallow Mexico as arsenic) — the lesson's organizing quotation.
- **The Overland Trails panel** (American Panorama): trail routes with forts and
  crossings, the year-by-year volume timeline, and a named diarist's entries at
  waypoints. Recreate the map-plus-timeline-plus-primary-source structure, drawn
  by us, with one real diary excerpt quoted.
- **The Proviso vote as a sectional readout**: tabulate the House vote by section
  rather than by party to show party discipline dissolving. Worked example: read
  the vote and state what it predicts; parallel exercise on a later vote.
- **The 36°30′ line vs. the Mexican Cession**: show why the Missouri line could
  not simply be extended — the new territory's geography and the free-soil
  objection.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not narrate the war's campaigns; the lesson is about the political
  consequence of the acquisition.
- Manifest destiny was a contested slogan, not a national consensus; Whigs
  opposed the war and many opposed annexation.
- The trails' emigrants were a small fraction of westward movement, and the
  greatest danger was disease, not attacks — correct the standard image.
- Guadalupe Hidalgo's guarantees to Mexican landholders were systematically
  defeated in US courts and by squatting; the treaty's text and its
  implementation are different objects.
- Free soil often coexisted with anti-Black racism (keeping territories white as
  well as free) — say so, because it explains later Republican politics.

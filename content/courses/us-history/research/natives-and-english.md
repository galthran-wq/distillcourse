# Research brief: natives-and-english (Native Nations and the English: Trade, Alliance, and War)

status: planned
module: contact-and-colonies
type: core
goal: Analyze Native–English relations in the seventeenth century as both alliance and war, using the Pequot War and King Philip's War as the measured cases.

THESIS ROLE. The thesis requires the Native-English story told factually both
ways: the alliance and trade that were real and mutually chosen (established in
new-england-covenant), and the wars that broke them, counted. King Philip's War
is the module's hardest number — proportionally the deadliest war in American
history — and it belongs in the causal chain: the war's outcome, plus the
demography of the previous lessons, is what made the mainland safe for the
English settler model. No guilt framing, no victimhood framing: two sets of
polities making rational choices under shifting power, documented from both
archives.

## Sources to consult

- **American Yawp Ch.2-3** — the Pequot War, King Philip's War, and
  Anglo-Native relations in Virginia (Powhatan wars) and New England. Spine.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.2** — the fur-and-wampum trade economy and the
  comparative treatment of English vs French vs Dutch Native policy.
- **Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation*** — the Mystic fire passage (1637),
  quotable as the colonial record's own words about what was done.
- **Mary Rowlandson, *The Sovereignty and Goodness of God* (1682)** — the
  first American bestseller; a King Philip's War captivity narrative to read
  with the records/argues/omits method (it documents the war's texture AND
  argues a providential frame).
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 3** — the New England week pairs settlement with
  conflict; borrow the pairing.
- Casualty and population figures: the standard King Philip's War estimates
  (roughly 1 in 10 English men of military age killed; some 25 towns attacked
  and about 12 destroyed; Native losses by death, disease, and enslavement
  far higher, with southern New England's Native population falling by
  60-80%). Verify against a named synthesis (Lepore, *The Name of War*, or
  Philbrick) before quoting precise numbers.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The trade equilibrium came first.** Wampum as currency, furs for metal
   and cloth; both sides gained; Native nations used English trade and
   alliance in their own rivalries (Wampanoag vs Narragansett, Mohegan vs
   Pequot). For two decades the coexistence was profitable and deliberate on
   both sides — state it as an equilibrium, not a prelude.
2. **The Pequot War (1636-37), counted.** Trade disputes and jurisdictional
   killings escalating; the English-Mohegan-Narragansett coalition; the
   Mystic massacre — several hundred Pequot, mostly women and children, killed
   in an hour, recorded without apology by the English commanders; the 1638
   Treaty of Hartford outlawing the Pequot name. Bradford's own passage
   quoted: the record is the colonial record.
3. **Why the alliance system broke, mechanically.** Land: English livestock
   trampling Native fields, courts treating unfenced land as unowned;
   demography: settlers doubling each generation while epidemics kept cutting
   Native numbers; sovereignty: colonial courts asserting jurisdiction over
   Native people (the trial and execution of three Wampanoag in 1675 as the
   trigger); succession: Massasoit's sons Wamsutta and Metacom inheriting an
   alliance that no longer paid. Each mechanism datable — the same method as
   colonial-societies' statute ladder.
4. **King Philip's War (1675-76), the measured case.** Metacom's coalition;
   25 of ~90 English towns attacked, ~12 destroyed; roughly 1 in 10 English
   men of military age killed — proportionally above the Civil War; Native
   southern New England devastated — thousands dead, survivors enslaved
   (including Metacom's son, sold to the West Indies) or dispersed;
   praying-town Indians interned on Deer Island through the winter. After
   1676 no Native polity could contest southern New England — the settler
   model's demographic runway was cleared by war as well as by disease.
5. **Virginia's parallel, in one section.** The Powhatan confederacy's 1622
   and 1644 attacks and the treaty system that followed; Bacon's Rebellion's
   Indian-war origins (cross-reference colonial-societies) — the pattern was
   continental, not regional.
6. **Both archives.** The colonial archive (Bradford, war narratives,
   Rowlandson) and the Native record (oral tradition, material evidence,
   petitions of the praying towns, and the war's Native veterans' testimony
   filtered through colonial courts). State what each preserves and what
   each cannot.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The proportional-casualty computation** as the lesson's worked example:
  take the English population of New England c.1675 (~55,000-60,000), the
  military-age male fraction, and the death tolls, and compute the share;
  compare with the Civil War's ~2% of total population. Parallel exercise:
  the reader runs the same computation for the Native population's losses
  and states why the uncertainty is larger.
- **Rowlandson read with the standing method**: one passage (the 20th Remove
  or the food passages) — what it records (wartime famine, Native political
  structure, her ransom negotiations with Weetamoo), what it argues
  (providence), what it omits (the war's causes; her captors' own losses).
- **The escalation ladder to 1675**: livestock cases, land deeds, the 1671
  Taunton arms surrender, the Sassamon trial — one datable step per rung,
  reusing the statute-ladder device from colonial-societies.
- **Bradford on Mystic, quoted cold**: "It was a fearful sight to see them
  thus frying in the fyer..." — the colonial record's own words carry the
  event; no editorializing needed either direction.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Keep both rationalities on the page: Massasoit's alliance and Metacom's war
  were both reasoned responses to measurable shifts in power; neither
  "friendship" nor "inevitable race war" survives the documents.
- Do not naturalize the outcome: disease did most of the demographic work
  (lesson 1), but the wars were policy, fought and chosen; keep the two
  mechanisms distinct as the indigenous-america lesson modeled.
- Enslavement of Native captives (sold to the Caribbean) is documented and
  belongs in the tally; it is routinely omitted.
- The "praying towns" complicate both simple stories: Christian Wampanoag and
  Massachusett fought on both sides and were interned by the English anyway.
- Numbers vary by source; give ranges and name the synthesis used.
- Rowlandson's narrative was edited and framed by Increase Mather's circle;
  say so when using it.

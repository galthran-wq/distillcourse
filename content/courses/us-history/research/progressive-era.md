# Research brief: progressive-era (The Progressive Era)

status: planned
module: reform-war-normalcy
type: core
goal: Explain Progressivism's two reform impulses — moral purity and scientific efficiency — and their constitutional harvest.

THESIS ROLE. The amendment machinery at industrial scale: four constitutional
amendments in seven years — the system rewriting itself through Article V
under organized public pressure, two of the four enacting Populist demands
from 1892. For the thesis this is institutions compounding again (reform
movements → amendments → new state capacity), with the boundaries (who the
19th Amendment reached in practice) stated in the same sections per the
course register.

## Sources to consult

- **MIT 21H.102 Lecture 9 "Progressive Reform"** (published notes) — the dual
  strands: "purity, propriety, morality" (WCTU, anti-vice, the Mann Act) against
  "rationality, efficiency, science" (muckraking, scientific management, civic
  housekeeping), with women's reform activism as the bridge between domestic and
  public authority. This two-faces framing is the lesson's organizing device.
- **American Yawp Ch.20 "The Progressive Era"** — settlement houses, suffrage,
  and the Wilson New Freedom vs. Roosevelt New Nationalism contrast.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.18 "The Progressive Era, 1900-1916"**.
- **Milestone Documents** #53 16th Amendment, #54 17th Amendment, #55
  Keating-Owen Child Labor Act, #59 19th Amendment.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Progressivism was a method, not a party.** Reformers in both parties and
   outside them, sharing a belief that expertise and organized publicity could
   correct industrial disorder. Name its constituencies: middle-class
   professionals, women's organizations, social gospel churches, muckraking
   journalists, and some business interests seeking stable regulation.
2. **The two impulses, stated as a pair.** Efficiency: scientific management,
   commission and city-manager government, the Federal Reserve, the FTC, the
   Pure Food and Drug Act, conservation. Purity: temperance and prohibition,
   anti-vice campaigns, the Mann Act, censorship, and — at its worst —
   eugenics and immigration restriction. The same movement produced both.
3. **Muckraking as an engine.** Tarbell on Standard Oil, Steffens on city
   government, Sinclair's *The Jungle* (aimed at labor, landing on meat
   inspection), and Hine's child-labor photographs for the National Child Labor
   Committee — investigation as a lever on legislation.
4. **Women's reform activism.** Settlement houses (Hull House) as data-gathering
   institutions as much as service providers; "civic housekeeping" as the
   argument that municipal life is domestic life at scale; the WCTU as the
   largest women's organization of the era; and the suffrage campaign's final
   phase — NAWSA's state-by-state strategy against the National Woman's Party's
   picketing and imprisonment.
5. **The constitutional harvest.** Four amendments in seven years: 16th (income
   tax, 1913), 17th (direct election of senators, 1913), 18th (prohibition,
   1919), 19th (women's suffrage, 1920). Two of them enact Populist demands from
   1892 — connect back to capital-and-labor.
6. **The boundaries.** Progressivism largely accepted or advanced segregation
   (Wilson segregated the federal civil service); many suffragists made explicitly
   racial arguments; the 19th Amendment left most Black women in the South
   disenfranchised by the devices from the jim-crow lesson. The exclusion is not
   an appendix — it is a defining feature.
7. **1912 as the movement's referendum.** Roosevelt's New Nationalism (regulate
   bigness) vs. Wilson's New Freedom (break it up) vs. Debs's socialism (six
   percent of the vote) vs. Taft — four programs, one election.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The two-faces chart** (MIT Lecture 9's own device): purity reforms and
  efficiency reforms as two columns, with a middle column of reforms that belong
  to both (child labor law, prohibition-as-public-health).
- **Hine's breaker boys as policy argument**: what the NCLC commissioned, how the
  photographs were captioned with ages and hours, and what legislation followed
  (Keating-Owen, 1916, struck down 1918). Worked example: read one photograph and
  its caption as an evidentiary claim; parallel exercise on a Riis image from
  capital-and-labor (spaced review).
- **The suffrage timeline**: 1848 Seneca Falls → 1878 first congressional
  introduction → state victories in the West → 1920 ratification. 42 years from
  introduction to ratification; 72 from Seneca Falls.
- **The 1912 four-way election** as a map plus platform comparison.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not define Progressivism by a list of good outcomes; it included eugenic
  sterilization laws, immigration restriction, and disfranchisement "reforms"
  sold as anti-corruption.
- Prohibition was a Progressive reform, not a puritan hangover — treat it as
  public-health-and-social-control policy with real support from women's
  organizations and employers.
- The 19th Amendment enfranchised women in law; in practice Black women in the
  South remained excluded until 1965. State this whenever the amendment is
  celebrated.
- Keating-Owen was struck down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918): federal child
  labor regulation waited until 1938. Reform is not the same as durable law.
- Distinguish municipal, state, and federal Progressivism; most of the movement's
  early wins were local.

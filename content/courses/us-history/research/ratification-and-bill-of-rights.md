# Research brief: ratification-and-bill-of-rights (Ratification and the Bill of Rights)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Evaluate the Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments over ratification and explain how the Bill of Rights emerged from that fight.

## Sources to consult

- **The Federalist Papers (Avalon Project)** — read the essays themselves:
  **No. 1** (Publius's six-part roadmap and the "reflection and choice" framing),
  **No. 10** (faction defined; the two cures; the extended republic),
  **No. 39** (neither national nor federal but a composition of both),
  **No. 47** (Montesquieu does not require total separation),
  **No. 51** (ambition counteracts ambition; the double security),
  **No. 84** (the argument *against* a bill of rights),
  **No. 85** (ratify-then-amend, given Article V's 9-of-13 threshold).
- **National Archives, "A More Perfect Union"** (Ratification Battles section)
  and the **Bill of Rights transcript + "How Was It Made"** pages — the
  ratification sequence, the nine-state threshold met July 2, 1788, and the
  twelve proposed articles of 1789.
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 7** — Virginia and New York ratifying-convention speeches
  (Pendleton, Madison); ratification taught as its own argument, distinct from
  the convention.
- **American Yawp Ch.6 "A New Nation"** — the political context of ratification.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The Anti-Federalist case, stated fairly and first.** A consolidated national
   government over a territory this large will become distant, aristocratic, and
   coercive; there is no bill of rights; the standing-army and taxation powers are
   unbounded. Patrick Henry's Virginia-convention attack on "ridiculous ideal
   checks and contrivances" is the quotable version.
2. **Madison's extended-republic answer (No. 10).** Faction is defined as a group
   adverse to others' rights or the aggregate interest; the two cures for faction
   (remove the causes, or control the effects) with the first rejected as worse
   than the disease; a large, diverse republic makes an oppressive majority harder
   to assemble. This inverts the classical small-republic assumption.
3. **The hybrid answer (No. 39).** "Neither a national nor a federal Constitution,
   but a composition of both" — foundation federal, House national, Senate
   federal, amendment blended. This is the course key result for this lesson.
4. **The mechanics (Nos. 47 and 51).** Separation does not require isolation;
   what makes it hold is giving each department "the constitutional means and
   personal motives to resist encroachment" — ambition counteracting ambition —
   plus the second, federal axis of division.
5. **Ratification as a real contest.** Nine states by July 2, 1788 (New
   Hampshire); Virginia and New York ratified narrowly and only after promises of
   amendments; North Carolina and Rhode Island held out past inauguration.
6. **The Bill of Rights as the price.** Hamilton's No. 84 argues enumeration is
   unnecessary and dangerous ("the Constitution is itself... A BILL OF RIGHTS");
   Jefferson, writing from France, calls a bill of rights "what the people are
   entitled to against every government on earth". Madison, converted by the
   ratification bargain, drafts and shepherds it. Twelve articles proposed
   September 1789; ten ratified December 15, 1791.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The Bill of Rights parchment's numbering**: the document lists "Article the
  first" through "Article the twelfth". Article the first (a representation
  ratio) was never ratified; Article the second (congressional pay) waited 203
  years to become the 27th Amendment in 1992. Modern renumbering I-X hides this;
  the scan shows it. This is the lesson's best single source-reading moment.
- **No. 51's two aphorisms** — "If men were angels, no government would be
  necessary" and "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition" — quoted exactly,
  then diagrammed.
- **No. 84's counterintuitive argument** against enumerating rights, set against
  the Ninth Amendment as the drafters' answer to it.
- **No. 10's two-cures structure**, walked through as a short argument
  reconstruction the reader can reproduce (worked example → exercise).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The Federalist Papers were campaign documents for New York ratification, not a
  neutral commentary; their influence on ratification itself was limited, and
  their canonical status is largely retrospective.
- Authorship was anonymous ("Publius"); the Hamilton/Madison/Jay attribution is
  later scholarship and a dozen essays were disputed for decades.
- Anti-Federalists were not anti-constitutional cranks; several of their demands
  became the Bill of Rights. Do not caricature them.
- The Bill of Rights bound only the federal government until the Fourteenth
  Amendment and twentieth-century incorporation — flag this forward to jim-crow
  and civil-rights lessons.
- Distinguish ratification (state conventions, 1787-90) from amendment (Article
  V, 1789-91). Different processes, different thresholds.

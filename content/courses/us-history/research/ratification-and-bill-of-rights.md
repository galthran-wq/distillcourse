# Research brief: ratification-and-bill-of-rights (Ratification and the Bill of Rights)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Evaluate the Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments over ratification and explain how the Bill of Rights emerged from that fight.

THESIS ROLE. Two thesis loads meet here. Contingency: Maier's *Ratification*
supplies the margins — 187-168 in Massachusetts, 89-79 in Virginia, 30-27 in
New York — that make the constitutional order a near-run event, not a
foregone conclusion; the reader should leave knowing the design of module 2
survived by a few dozen votes in three states. Institutions: the fight itself
was conducted through the institutions the course has been tracking — town
meetings instructing delegates, elected conventions, newspapers — and its
product (ratify-now-recommend-amendments) shows the amendment machinery
working before the government even existed.

## Sources to consult

- **Maier, *Ratification: The People Debate the Constitution, 1787-1788*** —
  the depth source for the whole lesson. Ch.2 (Mason's defeated
  bill-of-rights motion five days before adjournment — the blunder that made
  the yearlong fight necessary); Ch.3 (the press war: only 12 of 90+ papers
  printed substantial Antifederalist material; the Federalist Papers as one
  entry in a larger argument); Ch.4 (Pennsylvania's forced quorum and rushed
  convention — hardball that backfired; the Findley-Wilson clash); Ch.5-7
  (New England town-meeting deliberation; the Massachusetts Compromise and
  Hancock's brokered entrance; 187-168); Ch.8 (South Carolina's
  malapportionment: 143 coastal delegates for 29,000 vs 93 for a backcountry
  four times larger; Rhode Island's referendum rejection); Ch.9-10 (Virginia:
  Madison vs Henry, 89-79); Ch.11-13 (New York: Hamilton's express riders,
  the city's secession threat, 30-27, the circular letter); Ch.14 and
  Epilogue (North Carolina's initial rejection; the amendments funnel —
  states' structural amendments blocked, rights amendments conceded — under
  Madison's control in 1789). Access summary-based via Klarman's page-cited
  review; verify Maier's prose before quoting.
- **The Federalist Papers (Avalon)** — Nos. 1, 10, 39, 47, 51, 84, 85 as
  before; now framed per Maier as campaign literature inside a much larger
  public argument, canonical mostly in retrospect.
- **National Archives, "A More Perfect Union"** (Ratification Battles) and
  the **Bill of Rights transcript + "How Was It Made"** pages — the
  sequence, the nine-state threshold (July 2, 1788), the twelve articles.
- **MIT 21H.101 Week 7** — Virginia and New York convention speeches
  (Pendleton, Madison; Henry's "ridiculous ideal checks and contrivances").
- **American Yawp Ch.6** — political context.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Article VII changed the game.** Nine of thirteen states, by convention
   rather than legislature — the departure from the Articles' unanimity rule
   inverted holdout leverage (the last states risked exclusion, not veto)
   and made "conventions of the people" (Wood's device, from
   constitutional-convention) the ratifying instrument. State the design
   before the fight.
2. **The Anti-Federalist case, stated fairly and first.** A consolidated
   government over this scale will become distant and coercive; no bill of
   rights; unbounded taxation and standing-army powers. Per Maier: a
   spectrum of positions, not a bloc — from amendments-first moderates to
   outright rejectionists — and "Anti-Federalist" is the winners' label;
   say so.
3. **Madison's extended-republic answer (No. 10)** — faction defined, the
   two rejected cures, the inversion of the small-republic assumption.
   Cross-reference roman-blueprint: this is the argument that ended
   classical politics (Wood Ch.XV), made in a newspaper.
4. **The hybrid answer (No. 39)** — "neither a national nor a federal
   Constitution, but a composition of both."
5. **The contest itself, state by state, with numbers.** The press skew; the
   Pennsylvania rush and its backlash; Massachusetts as the turning point —
   Federalists arriving to a likely-hostile convention, the paragraph-by-
   paragraph debate, Hancock's compromise (ratify now, recommend
   amendments), 187-168 — the template every later close state followed;
   South Carolina's malapportionment; Virginia 89-79 with Henry commanding
   a quarter of the floor time; New York 30-27 under news of ten states and
   a city threatening to ratify alone; North Carolina and Rhode Island out
   past inauguration. Contingency stated in the vote table.
6. **The Bill of Rights as the fight's product.** Hamilton's No. 84 argument
   against enumeration; the states' recommended amendments split into
   rights provisions (later conceded) and structural limits on federal power
   (blocked); Madison — converted by the ratification bargain and his
   campaign against Henry's Virginia machine — distilling them into twelve
   articles, ten ratified December 1791. Maier's corrective: no state but
   North Carolina conditioned ratification on prior amendments, and
   contemporaries did not call the ten "the Bill of Rights."

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The vote table** as the lesson's spine figure: every state, date, margin
  — landslides in small states, razors in the big three; the reader computes
  how many total vote-switches would have defeated ratification (a few dozen).
- **The Massachusetts Compromise worked**: the problem (a likely-losing
  Federalist position), the device (unconditional ratification plus
  recommended amendments), Hancock's price (documented politics), and the
  diffusion of the template to Virginia and New York. Worked example;
  parallel exercise: the reader analyzes New York's circular letter as the
  same device pointed the other way.
- **The Bill of Rights parchment's numbering**: "Article the first" (never
  ratified) and "Article the second" (ratified 1992 as the 27th) visible in
  the scan — kept, the lesson's best single source-reading moment.
- **No. 51's aphorisms** quoted exactly and diagrammed — kept.
- **The amendments funnel** (Maier's Epilogue): states' heterogeneous
  recommendations → Madison's rights-only selection → twelve → ten — the
  structural demands (the actual Anti-Federalist program) visibly filtered
  out.
- **The Findley-Wilson "Swedish jury" exchange** (Maier Ch.4) as one
  paragraph of color evidencing that convention debate was real argument,
  not theater.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The Federalist Papers were campaign documents with limited contemporary
  reach outside New York; their canonical status is retrospective (Maier's
  corrective, now sourced).
- Anti-Federalists were not cranks; several demands became the Bill of
  Rights — and their structural demands (tax limits, army limits) were
  refused; state both halves.
- The press skew and malapportionment mean "the people debated" under real
  structural tilt; Maier raises "was it a fair fight?" — present the
  evidence, let the vote table and the reader judge (register rule: no
  adjective verdicts).
- The Compromise of 1877-style backroom detail (Hancock's price) is
  documented politics, not scandal-mongering; cite Maier's chapters.
- The Bill of Rights bound only the federal government until incorporation —
  keep the forward flag to jim-crow and civil-rights lessons.
- Distinguish ratification (conventions, 1787-90) from amendment (Article V,
  1789-91).

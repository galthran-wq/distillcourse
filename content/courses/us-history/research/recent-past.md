# Research brief: recent-past (Globalization, 9/11, and Polarization)

status: planned
module: postwar-america
type: core
goal: Examine the post-Cold War arc from globalization through 9/11 and the 2008 crisis to partisan polarization.

Final core lesson: it must close the course by returning to the thesis the
introduction argued — selection, compounding institutions, improbability,
enabling conditions — and by pointing back at named earlier lessons rather
than adding new machinery.

THESIS ROLE. The closing audit. The era supplies the thesis's terminal
measurements (the unipolar moment; the world's largest economy globalized
under institutions it designed) and its live counter-evidence (polarization
indices, two impeachments, January 6, the 2008 crisis testing which New Deal
guardrails held). The lesson's final section restates the causal chain the
course argued, cites the lesson that established each link, states the
evidence cutoff, and refuses prediction — then hands the reader to
historiographical-lenses, where the thesis itself goes on the bench. Foner's
three-part freedom question stays available as a source-reading instrument in
this closing audit, not as the frame.

## Sources to consult

- **American Yawp Ch.30 "The Recent Past"** — globalization, 9/11 and its wars,
  the 2008 crisis, marriage equality, contemporary politics. The Yawp's first
  edition stops updating in the mid-2020s; check the second-edition text for the
  latest coverage and state the course's own cutoff explicitly.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.27 "A New World Order, 1989-2004"** and **Ch.28 "A Divided
  Nation"** — the freedom lens applied to the contemporary period.
- **MIT 21H.102 Sessions 24-26** — neoliberalism and the digital age (Barlow's
  1996 cyberlibertarian declaration as a period document), risk society, and
  "toward a history of the present".
- **American Panorama, "Electing the President, 1840-2020"** — electoral and
  popular-vote results for ~50 elections, built to foreground voters and regional
  patterns; the data source for the polarization figures.
- **Milestone Documents** — ends at 1965; say so when the reader looks for
  documents here, and use presidential-library and congressional sources instead.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The unipolar moment and globalization.** The Soviet collapse (1991); NAFTA
   (1994) and the WTO (1995); China's WTO accession (2001); container shipping
   and supply-chain integration; the 1990s expansion and the tech boom; and the
   distributional record — aggregate gains, concentrated manufacturing losses in
   specific regions (the "China shock" literature), and rising inequality.
2. **The digital transition.** The commercial internet from the mid-1990s,
   Barlow's *Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace* (1996) as a period
   artifact of cyberlibertarian expectation, the dot-com bust, and the platform
   consolidation that followed — including the shift of political communication
   onto ad-funded platforms, which matters for the polarization section.
3. **9/11 and the open-ended wars.** The attacks; the 2001 AUMF's breadth (read
   its operative sentence against the Tonkin Gulf Resolution from the-sixties —
   deliberate spaced review); Afghanistan (2001-2021); Iraq (2003) and the
   absence of the weapons that justified it; the PATRIOT Act, surveillance
   expansion revealed in 2013, Guantánamo, and the torture program documented by
   the 2014 Senate report. Costs: roughly 7,000 US military deaths, hundreds of
   thousands of deaths in the war zones, and trillions in expenditure.
4. **2008.** Housing bubble, securitization, and leverage; the crash; TARP and
   the Fed's response; roughly 8.7 million jobs lost and about 10 million homes
   lost to foreclosure; the racial wealth gap widening sharply (connect
   explicitly to redlining and affluent-society). The Depression-era guardrails
   that held (FDIC) and those that had been repealed or never covered shadow
   banking — a direct test of the new-deal lesson's claims.
5. **Rights, extended.** *Lawrence* (2003), *Obergefell* (2015); the disability,
   immigrant-rights, and Black Lives Matter movements; and the countervailing
   line — *Shelby County* (2013) gutting VRA preclearance, which returns the
   course to the enforcement problem from redemption and jim-crow.
6. **Polarization as the era's structural fact.** Partisan sorting in Congress
   and the electorate, geographic sorting, media fragmentation, and the decline
   of split-ticket voting; two impeachments, contested election results, and
   January 6, 2021. Present measurable indicators rather than adjectives.
7. **Close the course.** Restate the argued causal chain — selected
   population, compounding institutions, improbable revolution, enabling
   conditions — with one linked lesson and one measurement per link, and
   set beside it the era's open counter-evidence (polarization, 2008,
   January 6) without adjudicating what is still moving. State the course's
   evidence cutoff and refuse prediction; point the reader at
   historiographical-lenses for the thesis's own trial.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Two authorizations compared**: the Tonkin Gulf Resolution (1964) and the
  2001 AUMF, read side by side for scope, duration, and named adversary. Worked
  example: identify what each delegates and to whom; parallel exercise: draft the
  narrowing amendment each would need.
- **2008 as a mechanism**: trace one subprime mortgage through securitization,
  rating, leverage, and default into a bank failure — the same
  transmission-chain method used in great-depression, applied to a new system.
- **The wealth-gap continuity**: median household wealth by race in 2007 and
  2013, with the redlining and GI Bill chapters cited as the earlier links.
- **Barlow's 1996 declaration read as a period source** — what it predicted and
  what happened instead.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Recent history has no settled historiography: label contested claims as
  contested, and prefer measurable indicators to characterization.
- Avoid partisan framing entirely: describe policies, votes, and outcomes with
  sources; where parties differ, say what each did.
- Do not present globalization as uniformly good or bad; state the aggregate and
  distributional findings separately, both with sources.
- Give the course's evidence cutoff date explicitly and do not extrapolate past
  it.
- Polarization measures (DW-NOMINATE, split-ticket rates, geographic sorting) are
  specific instruments with known limits; name the instrument when using a number.

# Research brief: religion-and-reform (The Second Great Awakening and Antebellum Reform)

status: planned
module: early-republic
type: core
goal: Explain how evangelical revival fueled abolitionism, temperance, and women's rights.

THESIS ROLE. The selected population's religious culture as a
reform-generating engine: disestablishment (an institutional choice) created
a competitive religious market; the revival it produced converted private
conviction into organized civic association — the temperance, abolition, and
women's-rights machinery that later supplies the civil-war and suffrage
modules their movements. Distinguish this SECOND Great Awakening from the
First (awakening-and-empire) in one sentence. Howe adds the postmillennial
mechanism (perfection expected in history, hence reform now) and frames
Seneca Falls as the era's culmination; the Declaration-of-Sentiments-vs-
Declaration parallel is also the founding-as-promise register working: the
reformers' instrument was the founding document itself.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Disestablishment made religion competitive.** With no established church to
   subsidize, denominations had to recruit; the ones that adapted their form to
   ordinary people — Methodists and Baptists, with circuit riders and lay
   preachers — grew explosively while the learned establishment churches shrank.
   Give the growth numbers.
2. **The camp meeting as a technology.** Cane Ridge (1801) as the type case:
   multi-day outdoor gatherings, simultaneous preaching, bodily manifestations,
   and mass conversion. Its theology mattered: salvation available to anyone who
   chose it, which made moral effort the individual's responsibility.
3. **From personal perfection to social reform.** If sin is chosen, it can be
   abolished — hence temperance (the largest reform movement by membership),
   sabbatarianism, prison and asylum reform, education reform, and utopian
   communities (Oneida, the Shakers, Brook Farm).
4. **Abolitionism's radical turn.** From gradualism and colonization (the
   American Colonization Society, Liberia) to immediatism: Garrison's *Liberator*
   (1831), the American Anti-Slavery Society (1833), and Black abolitionists —
   David Walker's *Appeal* (1829), Frederick Douglass's *Narrative* (1845) and
   his newspaper. Note that abolitionists were a despised minority in the North
   as well as the South, and were mobbed.
5. **Women's rights out of abolition.** Women organized in antislavery societies,
   were barred from speaking or seating (London 1840), and turned the argument on
   its own movement; Seneca Falls (1848) rewrote the Declaration of Independence
   as the Declaration of Sentiments. The rhetorical borrowing is the evidence of
   descent.
6. **The reform impulse had boundaries.** Nativism and temperance overlapped;
   many reformers were hostile to Catholic immigrants; and the women's movement's
   leadership was overwhelmingly white and propertied.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Cane Ridge's numbers** (Wood Ch.16): 15,000-20,000 attendees in a state whose
  largest town held under 2,000 — quantify the anomaly.
- **Lorenzo Dow's mileage** (Wood Ch.16): 10,000 miles, 500-800 meetings in 1805
  — one itinerant's logistics as a proxy for the movement's reach.
- **Declaration of Sentiments vs. Declaration of Independence**: print the two
  opening passages in parallel and let the substituted words carry the argument.
  Worked example: identify every deliberate alteration and state its effect.
  Parallel exercise: do the same for one of the Sentiments' grievance clauses
  against a Declaration grievance.
- **Colonization vs. immediatism**: the American Colonization Society's Liberia
  program (with the period Liberia map from the Yawp) set against Walker's and
  Garrison's rejection of it.
- **The camp meeting depicted** (1819 aquatint) as period visual testimony to the
  form.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Revivalism was not uniformly progressive: the same theology produced
  anti-Catholic nativism and, in the South, a scriptural defense of slavery.
- Do not describe abolitionism as a Northern consensus. It was a minority
  movement; Garrison was nearly lynched in Boston in 1835.
- Colonization was supported by many white antislavery figures and rejected by
  most free Black Northerners — the split matters and is often flattened.
- Seneca Falls was not the beginning of women's activism, and its suffrage
  resolution was its most contested plank, passing narrowly.
- Keep utopian communities proportionate: vivid, but demographically tiny.

# Research brief: roman-blueprint (The Roman Blueprint: Classical and English Sources of the Constitution)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Analyze how the constitutional design of 1787 was assembled from its named sources — Polybius's mixed government, the English constitutional inheritance, Montesquieu, and the republican ideology of 1776.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The argument has established the selected, self-governing colonial population and the imperial collision, and — from war-of-independence, the immediately preceding lesson — a war won at measurable odds that achieved independence but left open the question of whether self-government could scale from town to continent.

- **Contributes:** This lesson's share of the spine's sixth step — the framers answered the scaling question by design, and this lesson supplies the genealogy: Polybius's mixed government, the English constitutional inheritance, and Montesquieu, deliberately assembled into the design vocabulary the next lesson's convention machines into a document.

- **Hands off:** Constitutional-convention takes up how that classical and English vocabulary was machined, under deadline and compromise, into the federal structure signed at Philadelphia in 1787.

THESIS ROLE. Thesis element (2) at its keystone: the constitutional order was
not improvised — its framers deliberately assembled it from classical Roman and
English constitutional materials, and the assembly can be traced clause by
clause. This lesson supplies the intellectual genealogy that
constitutional-convention (the machine) and ratification-and-bill-of-rights
(the fight) then presuppose. It also completes the compact-to-constitution
ladder begun in new-england-covenant: the bottom-up colonial institutions and
the top-down classical design meet in 1787.

## Sources to consult

- **Wood, *The Creation of the American Republic, 1776-1787*** — the
  governing source. Part One (Ch.I-III): the Whig science of politics,
  republicanism defined ("the sacrifice of individual interests to the
  greater good of the whole"), moral reformation. Part Two (Ch.IV-VII):
  state constitution-making as the rehearsal. Ch.VIII-IX: sovereignty
  migrating from legislatures to conventions of the people. Ch.X-XI: the
  1780s "vices" and republican remedies (Trevett v. Weeden and Varnum's
  "altogether fiduciary" argument as proto-judicial-review). Ch.XIV: Adams's
  mixed-government theory and its obsolescence. Ch.XV: "The End of Classical
  Politics." Access is summary-based; the Ch.II definition and the Ch.XV
  conclusion are safely quotable (widely reproduced verbatim); verify
  anything else.
- **Polybius, *Histories* Book VI** — the mixed-government and anacyclosis
  passages; free full text (Loeb/Perseus). Quote the cycle argument briefly
  in translation.
- **Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* Book XI, ch.6** ("Of the
  Constitution of England") — the separation-of-powers chapter the framers
  cited; free text. Madison calls him "the oracle" in Federalist 47.
- **The Federalist** (Avalon) — No. 47 (Montesquieu handled), No. 63 (the
  Senate and the "auxiliary precautions" of republics ancient and modern),
  No. 70 (Roman consuls/dictator analogies), No. 9 (improved science of
  politics). Cross-reference ratification-and-bill-of-rights and
  federalist-papers rather than re-teaching.
- **English constitutional documents** — Magna Carta (1215) cl. 39-40, the
  Petition of Right (1628), the Bill of Rights (1689): each has direct
  descendant clauses in the US Bill of Rights; free texts (Avalon).
- **Bailyn, *The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution*** — the
  Commonwealth-Whig pamphlet inheritance (Trenchard and Gordon's Cato's
  Letters); summary-level use, as the companion frame to Wood.
- **Hayward, "The Liberal Republicanism of Gordon Wood" (Claremont/AEI)** —
  the Lockean counter-reading; one paragraph of it keeps the lesson honest
  (see pitfalls).

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The classical vocabulary was working equipment.** Publius, Cato,
   Brutus, Cincinnatus, a Senate, a Capitol: the pseudonyms of the
   ratification debate and the institutions' names are one continuous
   citation of Rome. The founders' educations (Latin required for college
   entry) made Polybius and Plutarch shared reference points — state it as
   the documented reading list, with Cato's Letters and the Whig canon as
   the transmission channel (Bailyn).
2. **Polybius's model, stated and applied.** The cycle (kingship →  tyranny
   → aristocracy → oligarchy → democracy → mob rule) and the cure: mix the
   three pure forms so each checks the others — Rome's consuls, senate, and
   assemblies as his worked example. Then the 1787 mapping: president /
   Senate / House as the mixed forms translated into elective terms — with
   the crucial difference (below) that all three derive from the people.
3. **The English inheritance, clause by clause.** Magna Carta cl. 39 → due
   process; the 1689 Bill of Rights → petition, arms, bail, cruel-and-
   unusual, no standing army without consent; the common-law jury; the
   colonial charters as written fundamental documents. The framers saw
   themselves claiming English constitutional rights, then converting an
   unwritten constitution into a written, superior one.
4. **Montesquieu and the separation doctrine.** The Spirit of the Laws'
   England chapter; the state constitutions' separation clauses (Virginia
   1776, Massachusetts 1780's "government of laws and not of men"); and
   Madison's correction in Federalist 47-51 — separation maintained not by
   parchment declaration but by blended, mutually checking powers.
5. **Wood's arc: virtue to interest.** 1776's republicanism assumed civic
   virtue — public good over private interest — and the 1780s state
   legislatures disappointed the assumption (paper money, debtor relief,
   factional lawmaking: the "vices"). The republican remedies (bicameralism,
   independent executives and judges, and sovereignty relocated to
   conventions of the people, with Trevett v. Weeden's fiduciary argument as
   proto-judicial-review) redesigned the system to work with interest rather
   than presume virtue. Wood's Ch.XV verdict quoted: the Madisonian theory
   of faction "marked an end of the classical conception of politics" — the
   framers used classical materials to build something classical theory said
   was impossible (an extended republic).
6. **Cincinnatus enacted.** Washington's 1783 resignation of his commission
   (George III's reported "greatest man in the world" remark, flagged as
   reported) and his 1797 retirement after two terms — the classical script
   of the citizen who returns to his farm, performed twice, and the Society
   of the Cincinnati named for it. Precedent as institutional design: the
   two-term norm held until 1940 and became the 22nd Amendment.
7. **The result, measured.** The output of this assembly — the oldest
   surviving written national constitution and the most imitated political
   design of the modern era (written constitutions, separation of powers,
   judicial review, federal structures adopted worldwide) — stated as
   measurements with the imitation examples named, per the course register.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The clause-genealogy table** as the lesson's centerpiece: one column of
  1787-91 clauses, one column of named sources (Polybius/Rome, Magna Carta,
  1689 Bill of Rights, Montesquieu, colonial practice), each pairing with
  its textual evidence. Worked example: trace one clause (cruel and unusual
  punishment: 1689 text → Virginia 1776 → Eighth Amendment, near-verbatim).
  Parallel exercise: the reader traces due process (Magna Carta cl. 39 →
  Fifth Amendment) and the presidential-oath/veto's Roman analogues.
- **Wood's definition quoted and tested**: "the sacrifice of individual
  interests to the greater good of the whole" as 1776's premise; then the
  1780s evidence that it failed as a governing assumption; then Federalist
  10/51's redesign — the reader watches one premise get replaced by
  engineering.
- **The Adams paradox** (Wood Ch.XIV): Adams argued constitutions must
  mirror real social orders; his structures (bicameralism, strong executive)
  were adopted at Philadelphia while his social premise (fixed orders to
  balance) was repudiated — a built-in discussion case, stated as Wood
  states it.
- **Trevett v. Weeden (1786)**: Varnum's argument that legislative power is
  "altogether fiduciary" — judicial review's logic appearing in a Rhode
  Island paper-money case before Marbury; forward pointer to
  jeffersonian-america.
- **The two resignations** told in documents: the 1783 resignation address
  and the Farewell Address, with the Cincinnatus frame the era itself used.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Wood's republican-synthesis is a strong, field-defining interpretation,
  not settled fact: pair it with the Lockean/natural-rights counter-reading
  (Hayward's critique; the Declaration's own Lockean structure from
  american-revolution) and say the framers drew on both vocabularies. The
  historiographical-lenses chapter revisits this dispute; plant the pointer.
- The classical references were selective and sometimes ornamental; the
  lesson's claim is documented usage (names, citations, structures), not
  that the founders replicated Rome. State what is genealogy and what is
  analogy.
- Do not present mixed government as democracy: Polybius's model balances
  social orders; the American translation made all branches derive from the
  people — Wood's own point about what changed. Getting this right is the
  lesson's precision test.
- The George III "greatest man in the world" quotation is reported at
  second hand (West's account); flag it as such.
- "Oldest written constitution" needs its qualifier: oldest *national*
  written constitution still in force (San Marino's statutes and state
  constitutions are the usual quibbles); state it precisely once.
- Slavery sat inside the classical frame too (Roman precedent was cited in
  proslavery argument later); one sentence of forward honesty to
  cotton-kingdom's Fitzhugh material.

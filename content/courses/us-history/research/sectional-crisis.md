# Research brief: sectional-crisis (The Sectional Crisis, 1850-1861)

status: planned
module: slavery-and-crisis
type: core
goal: Analyze the escalation from the Compromise of 1850 through Dred Scott and John Brown to secession.

Longest lesson in the module (30 min) and the causal hinge of the course. The
demo (SenateBalanceTimeline) is specced in course.yaml.

THESIS ROLE. The compromise machine reaches its load limit: the lesson shows
the institutions that had absorbed every previous conflict (Missouri, 1850)
failing one component at a time — party system, Court, Senate floor, ballot —
under the contradiction the cotton-kingdom lesson measured. The
founding-as-promise register gets its primary text here: Frederick Douglass's
July 5, 1852 Rochester address and his 1860 Glasgow speech reading the
Constitution as "a GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT" — the abolitionist claim ON the
founding, set directly against Taney's Dred Scott reading of the same
document. Two constitutional readings, both quoted; the war decides between
them. Reinforces the contingency concept (the 1860 four-way split).

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The Compromise of 1850 as a package that satisfied nobody.** California
   free, territorial popular sovereignty, the slave trade ended in DC, and a
   drastically strengthened Fugitive Slave Act that conscripted Northern
   citizens into enforcement — the last provision made slavery a Northern
   experience and radicalized Northern opinion.
2. ***Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) as a political event.** Sales figures, the
   Fugitive Slave Act as its provocation, and its role in making the abstract
   concrete for Northern readers. Treat fiction as evidence of and cause of
   opinion, following the MIT model.
3. **Kansas-Nebraska (1854) destroyed the party system.** Repeal of the Missouri
   line by popular sovereignty; the Whigs collapse; the Know-Nothings surge and
   fade; the Republican Party forms as a Northern free-soil coalition — a party
   with no Southern presence at all. That fact is what makes 1860 structurally
   different from every earlier election.
4. **Violence enters politics.** Bleeding Kansas (Pottawatomie, Lecompton) and
   the Brooks caning of Sumner (1856) on the Senate floor, with Northern and
   Southern reactions to the caning read against each other.
5. **Dred Scott (1857).** Held that Black Americans were not and could not be
   citizens ("no rights which the white man was bound to respect") and that
   Congress could not bar slavery from the territories — which made the
   Republican platform unconstitutional by judicial declaration and destroyed
   popular sovereignty at the same stroke. Blight treats this as the point at
   which compromise became impossible.
6. **John Brown (1859).** Pottawatomie killings, the Harpers Ferry raid, the
   trial and execution; Douglass's refusal to join and his warning that Brown
   would be trapped "in ... steel". Brown's martyrdom, not his living activism,
   is what shaped 1860.
7. **1860 and secession.** A four-way race; Lincoln wins an electoral majority
   with under 40% of the popular vote and no Southern ballot access; South
   Carolina secedes in December; six more follow before inauguration. Use
   McPherson's framing: a democratic "revolution of 1860" answered by a
   conservative "counterrevolution of 1861".
8. **What the seceding states said their cause was.** The Declarations of Causes
   name slavery explicitly and repeatedly. Quote them; this settles the
   causes-of-the-war question with the secessionists' own documents.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Douglass on the Constitution**: the 1852 Rochester address ("What to the
  Slave is the Fourth of July?") and the 1860 Glasgow "GLORIOUS LIBERTY
  DOCUMENT" argument, quoted and set against Dred Scott's holdings — two
  professional readings of one text, worked as the lesson's
  constitutional-interpretation set piece.
- **The six-document secession stack** (MIT Week 13) read in chronological order
  as rhetoric hardening in real time — the lesson's culminating worked reading.
  Worked example: the lesson analyzes the first three; parallel exercise: the
  reader analyzes Davis's Farewell and Lincoln's First Inaugural.
- **The Brooks-Sumner caning** with the 1856 Magee lithograph, plus the two
  sectional reactions (Brooks re-elected and sent replacement canes; Northern
  mass meetings) — one event, two publics.
- **Dred Scott's docket cover** ("Filed 30th December 1854 / Dismissed for want
  of jurisdiction March 6 1857") — an eleven-year lawsuit as a physical object.
- **John Brown, three ways** (Blight L9): the 1859 photograph, Hovenden's
  martyr painting, and later depictions — set up here with the photograph only;
  the myth-making analysis belongs to civil-war-memory.
- **The 1860 electoral map** with the popular-vote breakdown, showing a
  sectional plurality producing an electoral majority.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not let "states' rights" stand as an explanation: the Declarations of Causes
  complain that Northern states exercised *their* rights (personal liberty laws)
  against the Fugitive Slave Act. Name the right in question.
- Popular sovereignty was not a neutral procedure; it invited organized migration
  and fraud, as Kansas demonstrated.
- Lincoln in 1860 was not an abolitionist: he opposed slavery's extension and
  repeatedly disclaimed power over it in the states. Getting this right is what
  makes the secession decision intelligible.
- Brown's moral status is genuinely contested (Blight poses it as an open
  question). Present the evidence and the terms of the dispute; do not resolve it
  by adjective.
- Avoid economic-determinist "tariffs and industry" accounts as the primary
  cause; treat them, as Blight does, as a historiographical position with a
  documented rebuttal.

# Research brief: the-sixties (The Sixties: Great Society and Vietnam)

status: planned
module: postwar-america
type: core
goal: Analyze how the Great Society's legal triumphs and the Vietnam escalation fractured 1960s liberalism.

THESIS ROLE. The high-water mark and first overreach of the postwar state:
the largest legislative wave since 1935 with measured outcomes (the poverty
series, elderly poverty after Medicare) alongside an escalation authorized by
a resolution whose breadth the course reads closely — the delegation-of-war-
power problem the recent-past lesson revisits via the 2001 AUMF. The 1960
margin (112,827 votes) extends the contingency thread; 1968 shows the
coalition's break as datable events.

- **American Yawp Ch.27 "The Sixties"** — the chapter's own throughline:
  simultaneous legal triumph (Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act) and social
  fracture (Vietnam, urban unrest, assassinations), with the decade proving legal
  change achievable while structural inequality persisted.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.25 "The Sixties, 1960-1968"** — plus the Voices of Freedom

- **MIT 21H.102 Session 18 "Vietnam and the New Left"** and **Session 19
  "Power, Protest, and the Politics of Identity"** — the antiwar movement, the
  Free Speech Movement, King's 1967 Vietnam break, and the manifesto stack
  (Black Panther Ten-Point Platform, "The Personal is Political", the Alcatraz
  occupation statement, the Young Lords' 13-Point Program, gay liberation
  manifestos).
- **Milestone Documents** #87 JFK's Inaugural, #88 Peace Corps EO, #90 Cuban

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **A wafer-thin mandate.** Kennedy won 1960 by 34,227,096 to 34,107,646 — about
   0.2% of the popular vote. Quote the exact numbers; they puncture the
   assumption that the decade began with a mandate for anything.
2. **Cold War crises.** Bay of Pigs, Berlin, and the Cuban Missile Crisis
   (October 1962) — thirteen days, the reconnaissance photographs, the quarantine,
   and the secret Turkish-missile component of the settlement; the Limited Test
   Ban Treaty followed. Nuclear risk as the decade's floor condition.
3. **The Great Society.** After Kennedy's assassination and the 1964 landslide,
   the largest legislative wave since 1935: Civil Rights Act (1964), Voting
   Rights Act (1965), Medicare and Medicaid (1965), Elementary and Secondary
   Education Act, Economic Opportunity Act and the war on poverty, Immigration
   and Nationality Act (1965) ending national-origins quotas, and the creation of
   HUD. Measure it: the elderly poverty rate falling sharply after Medicare;
   overall poverty falling from about 22% (1959) to about 12% (1969).
4. **Escalation by increment.** Advisers under Eisenhower and Kennedy; the Tonkin
   Gulf Resolution (August 1964) passed on contested reports and used as the
   war's legal basis; Rolling Thunder and ground troops from 1965; roughly
   half a million troops by 1968; the draft's class and race inequities; Tet
   (January 1968) as a military defeat for the North that broke US political
   support; the credibility gap.
5. **The coalition breaks.** 1965-68: Watts and subsequent urban rebellions and
   the Kerner Commission's finding; SNCC's turn to Black Power; King's April 1967
   Riverside speech linking war spending and poverty; campus antiwar mobilization;
   the assassinations of King and Robert Kennedy in 1968; Chicago; Wallace's
   third-party run; Nixon's victory on a law-and-order and silent-majority
   platform. Guns and butter together produced inflation, which the next lesson
   inherits.
6. **The movements multiply.** 1966-1971 manifestos: Black Panther Ten-Point
   Platform, "The Personal is Political" and NOW, the Alcatraz occupation and Red
   Power, the Young Lords, Stonewall and gay liberation. Read them as parallel
   texts making structurally similar claims — the MIT Session 19 device.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The 1960 margin** as a hook: 112,827 votes out of 68 million.
- **The Tonkin Gulf Resolution read closely**: what the reported incidents were,
  what was actually established, and how broad the authorization's text is.
  Worked example: identify the delegation of war power in the operative sentence;
  parallel exercise on the 2001 AUMF (recent-past forward link).
- **The manifesto stack** (MIT Session 19): put three platforms side by side and
  extract the shared structure — a named group, a diagnosis of systemic
  subordination, and enumerated demands. This is the lesson's best worked
  comparison.
- **Great Society outcomes measured**: poverty and elderly-poverty rates before
  and after, and Medicare enrollment in year one.
- **King's Riverside speech** as the moment the two halves of the decade collide.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The Great Society was not defeated by Vietnam alone: fiscal pressure,
  administrative design, and the 1966 midterms all mattered. Avoid the single
  cause.
- "The war on poverty failed" is contradicted by the poverty series; what failed
  was the promise to end poverty, and community-action politics fractured
  local coalitions. State both.
- Tet was a US and ARVN tactical victory and a strategic political defeat — the
  distinction is the lesson.
- Do not romanticize the counterculture or reduce the antiwar movement to it;
  the antiwar majority was assembled slowly and included veterans, clergy, and
  eventually mainstream opinion.
- Urban rebellions had documented structural causes (police practice, housing,
  unemployment); the Kerner Commission said so and the administration shelved it.

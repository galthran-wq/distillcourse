# Research brief: war-of-independence (The War of Independence: Improbability in Numbers)

status: planned
module: revolution-and-founding
type: core
goal: Explain how an outmatched rebellion won — Boston, Trenton, Saratoga, the French alliance, Yorktown — with the odds stated in numbers.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The argument has established the selected, self-governing colonial population and the imperial collision of 1763–75, and american-revolution — the immediately preceding lesson — has just carried that collision through the Declaration as a structured legal argument to Lexington and Concord, with the liberty-vs-slavery paradox stated on the record.

- **Contributes:** This lesson's share of the spine's fifth step — the argument became a war won at measurable odds (Trenton's roughly 900 Hessians taken for two dead of exposure, Saratoga, the French alliance, Yorktown), and independence now forces the constitutional question of whether self-government can scale from town to continent.

- **Hands off:** Roman-blueprint takes up how the framers began answering that scaling question — by design, assembling classical Roman and English constitutional materials into a compound republic.

THESIS ROLE. Thesis element (3): the Revolution was a military and political
improbability that succeeded, and the course states the improbability in
numbers rather than adjectives. This lesson introduces the course's standing
concept of *contingency* — outcomes decided by narrow, datable events — which
later lessons reuse (ratification margins, 1800, Atlanta 1864). It also keeps
the war's full social reality on the record: a civil war among colonists,
Native nations' choices, and the enslaved people who fled to British lines.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The mismatch, tabulated first.** Britain: the world's largest navy,
   a professional army plus ~30,000 hired Hessians, and the empire's
   finances. The colonies: no navy, no treasury, a Continental Army that
   rarely exceeded 20,000 effectives and nearly dissolved at every winter
   enlistment expiry. State the asymmetry as the baseline that makes the
   outcome the thing to be explained.
2. **Boston: logistics as the first win.** Knox's noble train — 59 captured
   cannon, ~300 miles from Ticonderoga in 40 winter days — and the overnight
   fortification of Dorchester Heights (March 1776) with prefabricated works;
   Howe's evacuation without assault. The war's first lesson: the rebellion
   could win by engineering what it could not win by battle.
3. **New York: the near-death experience.** Long Island (August 1776) as
   catastrophic defeat; the night evacuation of ~9,000 troops across the
   East River by Glover's Marblehead mariners under fog; the retreat across
   New Jersey; the army down to a few thousand with enlistments expiring
   December 31. McCullough's contingency reading stated plainly: the
   Revolution survived 1776 by evacuation, weather, and days.
4. **Trenton and Princeton, in numbers.** The Christmas-night crossing in a
   nor'easter; the password "Victory or Death"; ~2,400 men against Rall's
   garrison; roughly 900 Hessians captured, ~22 killed, against a handful of
   American wounded and two dead of exposure; Rall's unread warning note;
   Princeton and Mercer's death completing the ten-day reversal. Paine's
   Crisis read to the troops — quote the opening line as the period's own
   framing of the moment.
5. **Saratoga and the alliance.** Burgoyne's surrender (October 1777, ~5,900
   prisoners) as the proof of viability France required; the 1778 treaties;
   what French entry changed, stated as strategy: Britain now defended the
   Caribbean, Gibraltar, and India, and the American theater became one
   front among several. Spain and the Netherlands follow; the Royal Navy
   loses local superiority exactly once — and that once is Yorktown.
6. **The war as civil war and social war.** Loyalists (perhaps a fifth of
   colonists; ~60,000 exiles), Native nations mostly siding with Britain for
   defensible reasons and paying at the peace table, and the enslaved
   people — 30,000-100,000 — who fled to British lines on the promise of
   freedom (Dunmore's Proclamation 1775; the Book of Negroes evacuations).
   Documented causal history in the register the course keeps: the numbers
   and documents speak.
7. **Yorktown as the closing contingency.** De Grasse's fleet holding the
   Chesapeake (Battle of the Capes, September 1781), the siege arithmetic,
   Cornwallis's ~7,200 surrendered, and the fall of North's ministry — one
   week of French naval superiority converting a stalemate into
   independence. Treaty of Paris terms stated (boundaries to the
   Mississippi; the fate of Loyalist and Native interests in the text).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The odds table** as the opening figure: navy, regulars, finances,
  population — Britain vs the colonies, with the one American asset
  (distance and space) stated.
- **The Trenton ledger** as the worked example: forces, losses on each side,
  and the strategic value of ~900 prisoners against two exposure deaths —
  the reader computes the exchange ratio and states why the political value
  exceeded the military one. Parallel exercise: the same ledger for
  Saratoga (5,900 prisoners) and its diplomatic price.
- **Leutze's painting vs the record**: McCullough's own commentary — wrong
  boat, wrong flag (the flag shown wasn't adopted until 1777), wrong
  daylight — worked as a myth-vs-fact source reading; the painting documents
  1851's memory, not 1776's night. Reuses the civil-war-memory method early.
- **The enlistment clock**: the army's strength through 1776 plotted against
  the December 31 expiry — the ticking-clock fact that makes Trenton's date
  intelligible.
- **Rall's pocket note** — the unread warning, told in two sentences as
  McCullough's emblem of contingency, with the source flagged as
  corroborated anecdote.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Contingency is not luck-worship: pair every near-miss with the structural
  factors (space, France, British strategic overreach) — the argument is
  that both were necessary, neither sufficient.
- Keep battle narration disciplined (names, dates, numbers, consequences);
  the lesson is about odds, not tactics.
- The self-emancipation figure spans 30,000-100,000 because the sources are
  fragmentary; give the range and what the Book of Negroes actually records
  (~3,000 evacuated from New York alone, named).
- British "defeat" needs precision: Britain chose to cut losses in one
  theater of a world war; the empire was not broken — say so, it explains
  1812 later.
- Do not quote McCullough's prose from memory; access is summary-based.
  Scene-level details used here (password, Rall note, casualty split) are
  corroborated across the ToC/review record — attribute to McCullough by
  chapter.
- Washington's generalship: the record supports perseverance and political
  skill more than tactical brilliance (he lost most set-piece battles);
  state the assessment the evidence carries.

    natural timeline figure.

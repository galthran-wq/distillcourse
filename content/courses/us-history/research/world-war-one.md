# Research brief: world-war-one (The First World War and Its Aftermath)

status: planned
module: reform-war-normalcy
type: core
goal: Examine US entry into World War I, the home-front repression it licensed, and the failed Wilsonian peace.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Progressive-era established the reform state's constitutional
  harvest — the 16th through 19th Amendments and new regulatory capacity —
  built by a coalition that also accepted segregation and eugenic exclusion
  as part of its program.
- **Contributes:** World-war-one is this step's first world-order reset — a
  state-directed war economy assembled in eighteen months, US finance and
  materiel deciding the Atlantic balance, and Wilson's Fourteen Points
  written as the peace agenda — set against the Espionage and Sedition Act
  prosecutions the promise-and-enforcement ledger tracks, and the Senate's
  rejection of the League.
- **Hands off:** the-twenties takes up the demobilized war economy — the
  Great Migration wartime labor demand set in motion, and the repression
  apparatus turned from wartime dissent onto domestic radicals and
  immigrants in the Red Scare.

THESIS ROLE. The first of the two war mobilizations that reset the world
order (thesis element 4): a state-directed war economy assembled in eighteen
months, US finance and materiel deciding the Atlantic balance, and the
American president writing the peace agenda — alongside the documented costs
at home (Espionage and Sedition Act prosecutions) that the course's
promise-and-enforcement outcome tracks. The rejected League ending matters to
the thesis too: the institutional order declined its first world-hegemony
draft; 1945's acceptance is the contrast world-war-two sets up.

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **From neutrality to belligerence.** Trade and loans binding the US economy to
   the Allies; submarine warfare and the *Lusitania* (1915); the 1916 re-election
   on "he kept us out of war"; resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare
   (February 1917); the Zimmermann Telegram (British-decrypted, offering Mexico
   US territory) published in March; the April 1917 declaration. Six senators and
   fifty representatives voted no — the decision was contested.
2. **Building an army and an economy from scratch.** Selective Service (24
   million registered, 2.8 million drafted); the War Industries Board, Food
   Administration, and railroad nationalization — a state-directed economy
   assembled in eighteen months, and the template New Dealers later invoked.
3. **Manufacturing consent, and punishing dissent.** The Committee on Public
   Information; the Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918); over 2,000
   prosecutions; Eugene Debs sentenced to ten years for an antiwar speech and
   polling nearly a million votes from prison in 1920; *Schenck* (1919) supplying
   the "clear and present danger" test; German-language suppression and vigilante
   violence.
4. **The Great Migration.** Wartime labor demand plus the closing of European
   immigration drew perhaps 500,000 Black Southerners north between 1916 and
   1919 (roughly six million over the full migration to 1970). Consequences: new
   industrial employment, new political weight in Northern cities, and violent
   white backlash — East St. Louis (1917), the Red Summer of 1919, Tulsa (1921).
5. **The 1918 influenza pandemic.** Roughly 675,000 US deaths — far more than
   combat deaths (~53,000 in battle, ~63,000 from disease) — spread by
   troop mobilization and worsened by wartime censorship of the news.
6. **The peace that failed.** The Fourteen Points and self-determination as a
   claim that colonized peoples took seriously; Versailles's punitive terms; the
   Senate's rejection of the Treaty and the League over Article X; Wilson's
   stroke; the 1920 "return to normalcy" landslide.
7. **The Red Scare.** The 1919 strike wave, bombings, Palmer raids, and mass
   deportations — the war's repression apparatus turned on domestic radicals and
   immigrants, running straight into the 1920s restriction politics.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The Zimmermann Telegram** as an intelligence-and-persuasion object: how it
  was intercepted, why Britain had to disguise the source, and what its
  publication did to public opinion. Worked example: separate the telegram's
  actual content from the interpretation placed on it; parallel exercise on a CPI
  poster's claims.
- **Debs's 1918 Canton speech and sentence**: quote the sentence for which he was
  imprisoned, then the 1920 vote total from prison. Two facts, no commentary
  needed.
- **The influenza-vs-combat death comparison** as a single chart — the war's
  deadliest American event was not the war.
- **Great Migration arrow map** with the destination cities' Black population
  growth 1910 vs. 1920.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The US entered late and fought for about seven months in significant numbers;
  do not overstate the military contribution while understating the material and
  financial one.
- "Self-determination" was applied in Europe and refused elsewhere: the
  Vietnamese, Egyptian, Korean, and Arab petitioners at Paris were dismissed —
  a forward link to containment-era conflicts.
- Wilson's racial politics (segregating the federal workforce, screening *Birth
  of a Nation* at the White House) belong in the same lesson as his rhetoric of
  democracy.
- The Senate did not simply reject internationalism; the fight was over Article X
  and Senate war powers, and reservationist compromises failed partly through
  Wilson's own refusal.
- Influenza mortality figures are estimates; give the source and the range.

   Telegram (the State Department decode, RG 59, reproduced on

   able to see: the coded groups struck through and replaced with the

   placed on it — that only works if the reader sees the real decoded page,
   not a typeset reconstruction. Placement: float-right within the road-to-war
   sequence (item 2), at the Zimmermann Telegram frame.

3. `draw:` single `<Figure>` — **deaths, compared**: US battle deaths, US

   One idea: the pandemic dwarfed the war.
4. `source:` no pool candidate — hunt: a Committee on Public Information war

   Public Information". Save as `cpi-propaganda-poster`. Reader must be able
   to see: the poster's full illustration and slogan at legible size. Why
   real: "manufacturing consent" is a claim about a specific persuasion

   wartime propaganda's tone. Placement: centered in the manufacturing-consent

5. `draw:` single `<Figure>` — **the wartime state, assembled**: agencies created
   1917-18 (WIB, Food Administration, Railroad Administration, CPI, Selective

   a note on which returned in 1933 and 1942.
6. `source:` no pool candidate — hunt: a completed WWI Selective Service draft
   registration card (facsimiles at the National Archives' "World War I
   Draft Registration Cards" collection, familysearch.org, and Wikimedia

   see: the card's actual printed questions and a filled-in hand. Why real:
   "24 million registered, 2.8 million drafted" is an abstraction until the
   reader sees the paper instrument that number is counting — the real form,
   not a drawn facsimile. Placement: float-left near the wartime-state figure
   (item 5), in the Selective Service discussion.
7. `draw:` single `<Figure>` — **prosecuting speech**: Espionage and Sedition Act

8. `source:` no pool candidate — hunt: a photographic portrait of Eugene Debs

9. `draw:` single `<Figure>` — **the Great Migration begins**: arrow map of

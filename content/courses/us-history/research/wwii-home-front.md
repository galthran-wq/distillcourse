# Research brief: wwii-home-front (The Home Front: Four Freedoms and Their Boundaries)

status: planned
module: depression-and-war
type: core
goal: Analyze how the war remade American society — women and Black workers in war industry, the GI Bill — while incarcerating Japanese Americans.

## Sources to consult

- **Kennedy, *Freedom from Fear*, Ch.21 "The Cauldron of the Home Front"** —
  women and Black workers in war industry, Japanese American incarceration,
  wartime social change.
- **Give Me Liberty Ch.22 "Fighting for the Four Freedoms"** — the Rockwell
  paintings as the mass-culture form of FDR's speech; Voices of Freedom doc #147;
  the contest over "freedom from want" as socialism.
- **American Yawp Ch.24** — home front sections: women, race, and the war economy.
- **MIT 21H.102 Session 12** — race and wartime propaganda, women's war work,
  Japanese American incarceration and *Korematsu*.
- **Milestone Documents** #68 Executive Order 8802, #70 Executive Order 9066,
  #73 Servicemen's Readjustment Act (GI Bill).

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **The Four Freedoms became mass culture.** Rockwell's four paintings ran in
   the *Saturday Evening Post* in 1943 and then as OWI war-bond posters that
   toured the country and raised well over $100 million. Analyze them as
   arguments: who is depicted, in what setting, and what "freedom from want"
   looks like when drawn as an abundant white family's Thanksgiving.
2. **Women's war work.** Female labor-force participation rising sharply;
   millions entering manufacturing; federal childcare under the Lanham Act;
   and the postwar reversal — layoffs, seniority rules, and a cultural campaign
   returning women to domesticity. State both the entry and the reversal, with
   numbers.
3. **Black war work and the Double Victory campaign.** A. Philip Randolph's
   threatened March on Washington forcing Executive Order 8802 (1941) — the first
   federal fair-employment measure — and the FEPC; the Double V campaign
   (victory over fascism abroad and racism at home); the second Great Migration
   to war-production cities; the Detroit riot of 1943; a segregated military
   whose Black units, including the Tuskegee Airmen, fought under white command.
4. **Japanese American incarceration.** Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942)
   authorizing military exclusion zones — its text never names Japanese
   Americans. Roughly 120,000 people removed, about two-thirds US citizens; the
   camps; *Korematsu* (1944) upholding the exclusion; the 442nd Regimental
   Combat Team; and the 1988 Civil Liberties Act's apology and $20,000
   per-person redress. Contrast with the treatment of German and Italian
   Americans.
5. **The GI Bill and the postwar middle class.** Roughly 8 million veterans
   receiving education and training benefits and 8 million receiving unemployment
   support; homeownership rising from about 40% in 1945 to about 60% within a
   decade. But local and segregated administration, plus the mortgage geography
   from the redlining lesson, distributed those benefits unequally — the single
   largest wealth transfer in American history, delivered along a color line.
6. **The war's social ledger.** Migration to the West and the Sunbelt, union
   membership at a peak, rationing and price control, and an expectation of
   federal responsibility for economic security that survives into the postwar
   settlement.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **Reading the Rockwells as arguments.** Worked example: analyze *Freedom of
  Speech* (a workman standing to speak; who listens; what the composition
  claims about American politics) and *Freedom from Want* (abundance, family,
  whiteness, domesticity) — what each includes and excludes. Parallel exercise:
  do the same for an OWI poster or for EO 8802's text.
- **EO 9066's euphemism, read closely.** Quote the operative clause — power to
  "exclude civilians from military areas" whenever a commander "deems such
  action necessary" — and note it never names an ethnic group. Set it directly
  against the Chinese Exclusion Act's explicit naming (spaced review from
  immigration-and-the-city): two grammars of exclusion, same function.
- **The Lange evacuation photograph**: numbered ID tags on a family's clothing —
  bureaucratic sorting made visible. Note that the WRA commissioned Lange and
  then impounded many of her photographs.
- **GI Bill arithmetic**: benefits claimed by white and Black veterans, and the
  mortgage-approval gap, computed as a wealth transfer.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- "Rosie the Riveter" was a propaganda campaign; most women workers were already
  in the labor force and moved into better-paid war jobs rather than entering for
  the first time. Give the composition data.
- Incarceration was not internment in the legal sense for the two-thirds who were
  citizens; use "incarceration" and explain the terminology choice once.
- *Korematsu* was never formally overruled until dicta in *Trump v. Hawaii*
  (2018); state the legal history precisely.
- The GI Bill's racial disparity was mostly produced by administration and by
  existing mortgage and admissions practice, not by the statute's text — that
  distinction matters and is the same mechanism as the redlining lesson.
- Do not treat the home front as uniformly unifying: strikes, race riots, and
  black-market activity all rose.

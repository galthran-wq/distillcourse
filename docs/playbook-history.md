# Playbook — history overlay

Domain layer for history courses, on top of `docs/playbook-core.md` and the
constitution. Provenance: the 52-episode CrashCourse US History dissection
(July 2026) and the history-education literature audit (July 2026 —
Wineburg/SHEG sourcing research, Reading Like a Historian quasi-experiment,
second-order-concepts tradition, refutation-text and quantitative-literacy
research). We adopt the reference course's STRUCTURE and discipline, never
its voice: the register stays L9-dry.

## Numbers in history (sharpens L12)

In history the number plays the role the formula plays in mathematics: a
load-bearing token that must arrive bound or not at all.

1. **Inclusion test.** A number enters body prose only if the reader must
   *compare, estimate a magnitude, calibrate, or weigh evidence* with it —
   i.e. only if some claim fails without it. Everything else: order-of-
   magnitude words, a figure, a table, or the aside.
2. **Full binding.** Unit, denominator or base of comparison, timeframe, and
   (where contested) source — in the same sentence. Round to the precision
   the claim actually supports; exact values live in tables and source notes
   where auditability matters.
3. **Charts are the home of series.** A dense numeric series is a figure
   with a takeaway caption; prose states what the series means. Diagrams
   and data charts are never rationed by the numbers budget.
4. **Computation only inside source inquiry.** A calculation (rate,
   proportion, real vs nominal, sensitivity of an estimate) is legitimate
   exactly when the calculation IS the historical argument — e.g. how a
   contested starting figure moves a decline percentage. It always ends in
   a prose interpretation, an uncertainty statement, and where possible a
   corroborating non-quantitative source. Plug-constant arithmetic
   (inventing crew sizes and trip lengths to multiply) is busywork
   transplanted from another discipline: banned.

## The sourcing drill (sharpens the records/argues/omits method)

The course's standing method for reading artifacts, upgraded with the
research-backed routine:

1. **Metadata first.** Author, date, audience, purpose — visible before or
   beside the document, never after it.
2. **Prediction before close reading.** One Task: given the metadata, what
   would you expect this source to claim or omit? Then read.
3. **Records / argues / omits** — the existing three-question pass.
4. **Use, not bias.** The closing question is never "is the source biased?"
   but "how does its provenance change what it can be USED to establish?"
5. **Corroboration where sources conflict.** Two to four SHORT excerpts, an
   agreement/disagreement comparison, then an evidence-based judgment. This
   is the Reading Like a Historian routine (central question → background →
   modeled heuristic → conflicting sources → written judgment) adapted to
   self-study: annotated think-alouds and staged reveals replace the
   teacher.

## Anchor timeline (sharpens L11)

- Each module maintains **5–10 anchor events** — high-leverage, dated,
  named once and reused. Every new event is placed RELATIVE to an anchor
  ("a decade after the Pequot War", "the same year as the Armada").
- Show duration and simultaneity, not only sequence — a timeline figure
  states spans, not tick marks. Adjacency on a timeline is not causation;
  causal claims are made in prose, not implied by layout.

## Second-order concepts as exercise stems

Exercises drill the discipline's method — the second-order concepts — with
recurring stems:

- *Evidence*: apply the sourcing drill to a new artifact; state what a
  source can and cannot establish.
- *Causation*: rank causes as necessary / enabling / precipitating, and
  defend the ranking.
- *Significance*: significant to whom, by what criterion, at what scale?
- *Change and continuity*: what changed, at what rate, for which groups —
  and what persisted?
- *Counter-argument*: "someone claims X; name two facts the claim fails to
  account for, and state what would have to be true for it to survive."

## Exercise mix (replaces compute-first defaults)

Per lesson, approximately:

- **20–30% retrieval and chronology** — closed-book recall of anchor
  events, keeper numbers, established claims (spaced: from earlier lessons
  too, per E3).
- **30–40% short document reasoning** — single-source sourcing items,
  paired-source corroboration, "what does this omission establish?"
- **30–40% synthesis and argument** — claim–evidence–reasoning paragraphs,
  causal rankings, counter-argument tasks.

Arithmetic appears only under rule 4 of the numbers section. Solutions are
argued to the same standard as worked examples (E4): the model answer names
the evidence and the warrant, not just the verdict.

## Misconception slots (L13, history instantiation)

History is myth-rich, so genuine refutation targets are common: rugged-
individualism frontier myths, "a few hundred Spaniards conquered an empire
by arms", single-cause war origins. Where the syllabus meets a documented
popular misconception, use the refutation schema from the core playbook.
Where it doesn't, write no slot — a strawman "some believe…" is an L9
violation.

## Testimony (permitted, not required)

The record includes human voices. A quoted sentence from a participant —
in the Lincoln–Douglass register the course already uses — is often the
strongest evidence a claim can carry, and is always preferred over an
invented scene (L9 bans those unconditionally). Administrative records and
testimony are complementary evidence classes; neither is mandatory per
section.

## Appendix: the CrashCourse dissection, in numbers

For calibration, the slot frequencies over the 52-file corpus (47 numbered
episodes + specials): Thought Bubble (one self-contained narrated scene)
48/52; Mystery Document (primary source + live inference chain) 35/52;
Me-from-the-Past (voiced misconception) 27/52; constituency ledger ("what
did this mean for workers / women / farmers / Black Americans") in nearly
every post-1850 episode; one arguable question per episode 47/47; forward
hook 47/47. What we adopt: the argued unit, the misconception engine (dry,
optional), the sourcing drill with visible inference, the ledger-as-facts,
the sparse consequence-framed numbers. What we reject: the comedic persona
layer, invented scenes, editorializing epithets.
